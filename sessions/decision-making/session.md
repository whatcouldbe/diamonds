# Decision Making

Help a team reach a decision people actually stand behind, not just tolerate, by separating understanding the proposal from reacting to it from committing to it.

**Driving question:** How might we make decisions people commit to, even when they don't fully agree?

---

## Key Question

This session doesn't map to a single process checkpoint the way Prioritization does. It supports the moment, inside almost any checkpoint, where a team has gathered enough input and needs to actually converge and commit to a direction rather than keep circling it. It shows up most visibly around "What should we be working on to achieve our goals?" and "How might we best solve the problem?" — use it whenever a team is stuck oscillating on a call it has enough information to make, not when it still lacks the information to make it well.

---

## When to Use

Use this session when a decision has been informed enough by prior work, but the team hasn't converged, and the continued back-and-forth is starting to cost more than the decision itself is worth. It works best when there's a clear (or clearly assignable) decision owner who is getting drowned out, avoiding the call, or unsure how to bring a group along with a decision they don't fully control alone.

It is not a substitute for evidence-gathering — if the team doesn't yet know what it needs to know, send it back to looking or understanding work first. And it's not for decisions that are cleanly one person's call with no need for team buy-in; forcing a group ritual onto an individual decision wastes everyone's time and teaches people to distrust the ritual.

---

## Method Sequence

| Step | Method | Role in this session | Alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Critique | Structures the informing stage — separates clarifying the proposal from reacting to it, before a decision owner commits | — |

Critique already separates understanding a piece of work from evaluating it from improving it — Set Context, Present the Work, Clarifying Questions, Strengths, Challenges, Build On It, Close. That's the exact discipline a decision-commitment conversation needs: mixing clarification with reaction is the single most common way these conversations go sideways. This session borrows Critique's informing stages as-is and adds an explicit commitment ritual that Critique itself doesn't include, because Critique ends at feedback and this session needs to end at a decision.

## Working Session Arc

*Typical duration: 60–90 minutes | Participants: 3–8 (one decision owner plus an informing group)*

### Stage 1 — Frame the Decision
**Time:** 5 min | **Purpose:** Name what's actually being decided and who decides it

State the proposal in one sentence, as a proposal, not an open question. Name the decision owner explicitly, out loud. Confirm the group's role for this session: to inform the decision, not to make it.

### Stage 2 — Present the Proposal
**Time:** 5–10 min | **Purpose:** Get everyone working from the same facts

The decision owner (or someone on their behalf) presents the proposal and the reasoning behind it — what's being proposed, why, and what alternatives were already considered. Keep it factual; save the group's reactions for later.

### Stage 3 — Clarifying Questions
**Time:** 5–10 min | **Purpose:** Make sure everyone understands before anyone responds

The group asks only clarifying questions — no reactions yet. This is the facilitator's first real job: catch anyone reacting instead of clarifying and redirect them to hold it for the next round.

### Stage 4 — Reactions
**Time:** 10–15 min | **Purpose:** Surface strengths, concerns, and suggestions separately

Structured reactions in three lanes: what's working, what concerns you, what would strengthen this. One thought per note, silent write before group discussion. The lanes matter more than the volume — a handful of real reactions beats a pile of vague ones.

### Stage 5 — Decide
**Time:** 5–10 min | **Purpose:** Get an actual call from the decision owner

Turn to the decision owner directly: they've heard the input and seen the reasoning; ask for their decision. Don't let the group make the call for them, and don't let the owner defer it back to the group.

### Stage 6 — Commit
**Time:** 5–10 min | **Purpose:** Turn agreement or disagreement into a recorded commitment

Each person signals explicitly: agree and commit, or disagree and commit. Name the exit hatch before you need it — if someone believes proceeding is genuinely unsafe, not just undesirable, that's a different conversation, and it may mean moving to full consensus instead of closing here.

### Stage 7 — Capture Next Steps
**Time:** 5 min | **Purpose:** Close with commitments, not just a decision

Confirm an owner and timeframe for the first action. Agree on how any recorded disagreement gets shared with people who weren't in the room.

## Teaching Notes

*Use this section in Teaching mode — when the goal is building someone's capability to run this session.*

**What participants should be able to do afterward:**
- Keep clarifying questions and reactions separate in the moment, even when the group wants to blur them
- Run a structured reaction round in three categories instead of an open debate
- Facilitate a commitment round that records dissent without relitigating it
- Tell the difference between "I disagree" and "this is unsafe," and respond to each differently

**Key facilitation moments to teach:**
- The clarify/react boundary: the most common breakdown in practice. Name it out loud the moment it blurs — "that sounds like a reaction, can you hold it for the next round?"
- The commitment round as a record, not a vote: disagreeing and committing isn't being overruled, it's documenting that the objection was heard and the team is moving forward anyway.
- The unsafe-to-proceed exit: distinguishing genuine risk from ordinary disagreement is the legitimate off-ramp to full consensus, not a way to relitigate every decision someone doesn't love.
- Decision-owner passivity: owners sometimes wait for the group, or the facilitator, to make the call for them. The facilitator's job is to hand it back explicitly, by name, at Stage 5.

**Warm-up option:**
Have participants individually reflect on a recent decision — one that felt hard, one that felt easy — before any instruction begins. This reliably surfaces the same handful of patterns (no clear owner, emotional stakes, needing buy-in versus needing input) that the method is designed to address, which makes the method feel like a response to something the room already named rather than a solution looking for a problem.

**Practice structure:**
Pairs work well: one person facilitates the clarifying round, the other facilitates the reactions round, then switch for a second practice run. Feedback in the format: one thing that worked, one thing to try differently, one open question.

## Common Questions

**Who should be the decision owner?**
Whoever is closest to the work and will live with the outcome — not necessarily the most senior person in the room. If the obvious owner declines, the facilitator's job is to help the group find the next-closest person, not to escalate.

**What if the decision splits into several decisions mid-session?**
That's usually a sign the proposal was too broad to begin with. Keep the framing to one specific proposal. If a second decision genuinely surfaces, name it and agree how it will be handled separately — don't try to run two commitment rounds in one sitting.

**What if someone says "I disagree" and won't move past it?**
Ask directly: is this "I'd have done it differently," or "I believe proceeding is unsafe"? The first is disagree-and-commit territory. The second is the legitimate exit to full consensus — it changes the process, not just the mood in the room.

**Can the decision owner also facilitate?**
It works, but it costs neutrality. If it's genuinely their call and they're also running the room, they need to be transparent about which hat they're wearing at each moment — a co-facilitator for the reactions round helps.
