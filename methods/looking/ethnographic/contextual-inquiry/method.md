# Contextual Inquiry

Learning how work actually gets done by having the person **show you** — on the real work, in its real setting — rather than tell you about it.

---

## Purpose

Use when you need to understand how people actually do something — the real sequence, the workarounds, the judgment calls, the tools and artifacts involved — and a conversation alone would give you the tidy version.

**The core move is "show me."** That is what defines the method, more than whether the work is happening live or already finished. Interviewing asks "tell me"; observation says "let me watch"; contextual inquiry asks the person to *show* you while you ask about what you're seeing. You get behavior and explanation together. It is sometimes called shadowing or an observational interview. `[practitioner-taught]`

**The core question:** What does this person actually do, and why — as shown on the real work, not as remembered in the abstract?

**Why it matters:**
- The value is in the gap between how the work was *designed* to happen and how it *actually* happens: "I never do that first — I always do this first." Somebody on a drawing board decided the sequence without watching the person who does the work. `[practitioner-taught]`
- People's account of their own process is not their process. Recall smooths over friction, skips the workarounds, and idealizes the sequence. "Show me" closes the say-do gap that interviewing leaves open.
- The most valuable knowledge is often tacit — skill the person uses without ever having written it down. They can't tell you about it because they don't know it's there. They *can* show it to you. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- It reveals what surrounds the task — the other system they log into first, the workaround before the "real" step. Every experience has a before, a during, and an after. `[practitioner-taught]`

**Where it sits in the process:**
Discovery, in the first diamond — understanding current state before defining the problem. It sits at the rich end of the ethnographic spectrum:

| Method | Mode | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| **Try it yourself** | Experience it | Your perspective, not theirs |
| **Observation / Fly on the wall** | "Let me watch" | Behavior without explanation |
| **Interviewing** | "Tell me" | Perspective and motivation, with say-do gap risk |
| **Contextual inquiry** | "Show me" | Behavior + explanation in context |

**What it's best for — and not:** Contextual inquiry is strongest at surfacing the pain in current work, which tends to produce improvement ideas. If the goal is transformative change, don't expect the inquiry alone to get you there; pair the ideation that follows with a method that releases current constraints. `[practitioner-taught]`

**What it is not:**
- **Not Think-Aloud Testing** — though it gets close. Think-aloud asks someone to attempt a task on something *you* made, to evaluate it. Contextual inquiry asks someone to show you *their own* work, to understand it. If the person doesn't have the real thing yet and you hand them a preview with a task, you've moved into think-aloud territory. The retrospective form also sits close to evaluative research — you are examining an artifact — but its purpose is still to understand how the work was done, not to judge it. `[practitioner-taught]`
- **Not a demo.** A from-scratch re-enactment ("show me how you'd do it") is neither the real thing nor an honest observation. Anchor on real work.

## Setup

**People:** Go in pairs if you can — one person leads the conversation, the other documents. The second person catches what the lead misses ("take a picture of that"). If it's just you, record (with permission) and capture from the transcript afterwards. `[practitioner-taught]`

**Participants:** One at a time. Get as close as you can to the people who actually do the work. Proxies — experts who know the system but don't do the job — are a step removed. They're better than no one, but name the gap. `[practitioner-taught]` Consider the extremes — least and most experienced, or people who avoid the tool and people who can't live without it. `[practitioner-taught]`

**Access:** More than an interview needs. Live inquiry needs you where the work happens, in person or by screen share. Check the logistics early: can this person actually share their screen from where they work (a phone, in the field)? Use whatever video platform is easiest *for them*. `[practitioner-taught]` The retrospective form needs the participant to have the artifact they produced open and shareable.

**Time:** 30–60 minutes. `[reconstructed]` The field-tested retrospective run was planned for 45 and got about 40. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Materials:**
- A short plan: what you want to learn, who you'll observe, and a discussion guide — not a script `[practitioner-taught]`
- A way to capture notes as you go (see Steps)
- Permission to record or screenshot

## Steps

### Choose the form

First question: **can you watch the work happen?**

- **Yes → Live contextual inquiry.** "Show me as you do it." Be there while real work is done, and ask about what you see.
- **No, it's already done → Retrospective walkthrough.** A decision already made, a score already produced, an artifact already built. You can't observe it live, and asking them to redo it produces a staged demo. Instead, have them drive the artifact they actually produced and retrace the real steps on it: "show me *where* you did that." `[field-tested, n=1]`

### 1 · Plan — "go where the action is"
- Identify the tasks you're curious about and the questions you have. `[practitioner-taught]`
- Identify the people you can talk to and observe. `[practitioner-taught]`
- Plan to document as much as possible and collect any artifacts you find along the way. `[practitioner-taught]`
- Mark the **high-value segments** — the parts only this participant can give you — and give them the most time. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- List what prior write-ups *claim* about how the work was done. Each claim is something to confirm in the room, not a fact to build questions on. `[field-tested, n=1]`

### 2 · Open
- Introduce yourself, set the context, and get permission to record — before the recording starts. `[practitioner-taught]`
- Frame it in one line: you want the real, messy version; you're not grading it or fixing it today. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- Ask them to share their screen or the real work and start at the beginning. Ask them to narrate as they go. `[practitioner-taught]`

### 3 · Show me
- **Live:** let them do their work as naturally as possible. An occasional clarifying question is fine ("does this happen a lot?") — but don't put words in their mouth and don't interpret. Some practitioners hold all questions until the task is done, then go back step by step. `[practitioner-taught]`
- **Retrospective:** walk the artifact in order. Ask them to show you one real instance end-to-end ("show me one actual interview, with one specific person"), and have them point to exactly where each thing lives on the artifact. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- **Capture before, during, and after** — what happens before the main task (the other system, the setup) matters as much as the task itself. `[practitioner-taught]`
- Watch faces as well as screens. If something makes them wince, ask about it — people don't always say it out loud. `[practitioner-taught]`
- Capture notes as what works well, what doesn't, and what has potential — the same categories you'll use to codify later. Place them against the screen or step they relate to. `[practitioner-taught]`

### 4 · Probe
- **Divergences:** where did the work or its output disagree with reality? Don't accept "it mostly matched" — find the specific places. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- **Magic wand / what would make this easier:** "If you could change anything about this, what would it be?" In the field-tested run, this surfaced the most actionable finding of the session. `[practitioner-taught]` `[field-tested, n=1]`

### 5 · Close
- "What haven't I asked that I should have?" `[field-tested, n=1]`
- "Who else should I speak with?" and "Can I come back to you with more questions?" `[practitioner-taught]`

### 6 · Codify and synthesize
- Codify notes one item per sticky as roses, thorns, and buds, then cluster them. Give each cluster a descriptive, sentence-length title, not a one- or two-word label. Well-written themes are close to backlog-ready. `[practitioner-taught]`

## Facilitation notes

**Retrace, don't describe.** When the participant drifts into general process description ("usually we…") or races ahead across the artifact, slow them down and pin them to one point: "tell me literally which words on the screen are the ones that relate to that." The specific beats the summary every time. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Don't analyze in the room.** You'll notice patterns as you listen. Park them. Interpreting out loud steers the participant; analysis happens afterwards, from what you captured. `[field-tested, n=1]` `[practitioner-taught]`

**Confirm the inputs; don't import them.** Prior write-ups — a brief, a task card, someone else's summary — are claims to verify. Ask openly first ("what else did you draw on?") before naming a specific source, so you don't lead. An *absent* input is itself a finding. In the field-tested run, a source the prior summary listed as part of the method turned out to have been added later, at a stakeholder's request. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Look for what lives in their head.** In the field-tested run, the key mapping behind the artifact existed only in the practitioner's head, written nowhere. That explained why colleagues trying to reuse the artifact as a template were struggling. An abstract retelling would have skipped it. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Notice what the artifact doesn't hold.** Artifacts are dense and incomplete — earlier data may live in other tools, and some reasoning was never recorded. If something can't be found in a minute, note the gap and move on; don't burn the session searching. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Don't create homework.** Participants may offer to annotate or tidy their work for you. Decline — you want the work as it was, not a cleaned-up version. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Mind the observer effect.** Being watched changes behavior, especially if the participant knows you as an expert; they may keep asking "what would you do?" Set the scene deliberately and stay neutral. A facilitator they don't know can sometimes get a more natural session. `[practitioner-taught]`

**Have a focus.** Know what you're watching *for* — for example, the steps where time goes, or where the work could be simplified. `[practitioner-taught]`

**Start where you are, use what you have, do what you can.** You rarely get the perfect participant, in the perfect setting, with the perfect access. Do the inquiry you can, and name what's missing. `[practitioner-taught]`

## Variations

### Live contextual inquiry
The classic form: in the participant's environment, or over screen share, while real work happens. Richest data; hardest access.

### Retrospective walkthrough (artifact-anchored)
For work already complete. The participant drives the real artifact; you retrace the real steps on it. See `resources/templates/retrospective-walkthrough-guide.md`. `[field-tested, n=1]`

### Remote / screen-shared
Either form works over screen share when the work is digital. You lose the physical environment, so ask directly about what's around them that isn't on screen. For people who can't give you a formal session (on the road, in the field), short asynchronous questions — a message, a comment on a design file — can fill gaps between sessions. `[field-tested, n=1]`

## Outputs

- A **current-state journey** — the real sequence of steps, before, during, and after
- Notes codified as roses, thorns, and buds, placed against the steps they relate to
- **Divergences** — where the work or its output disagreed with reality
- A record of **tacit knowledge** surfaced, and of **absent inputs or missing rationale**
- Referrals to other people to observe

## Connections

### Upstream
- **Interviewing** — shares the protocol architecture; often used alongside, before or after the observation.

### Downstream
- **Rose, Thorn, Bud** — codify the raw notes
- **Affinity Clustering** — find the patterns across sessions
- **Creative Matrix** — observations can shape the matrix's rows and columns `[practitioner-taught]`

### Related Methods
- **Interviewing** — "tell me." Contextual inquiry is interviewing plus observation, on the real work.
- **Observation / Fly on the wall** — "let me watch," without the explanation.
- **Think-Aloud Testing** — evaluates something you made; contextual inquiry understands work they do.
- **Heuristic Review** — when you have no access to people at all, an expert review catches the obvious; it can't show you how the work actually runs.
- **Critique** — structured feedback on an artifact; related posture, different purpose.
