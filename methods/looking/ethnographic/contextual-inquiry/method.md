# Contextual Inquiry

Learning how work actually gets done by having the person **show you** — on the real work, in its real setting — rather than tell you about it.

---

## Purpose

Use when you need to understand how people actually do something — the real sequence, the workarounds, the judgment calls, the tools and artifacts involved — and a conversation alone would give you the tidy version.

**The core move is "show me."** That is what defines the method, more than whether the work is happening live or already finished. Interviewing asks "tell me"; observation says "let me watch"; contextual inquiry asks the person to *show* you while you ask about what you're seeing. You get behavior and explanation together.

**The core question:** What does this person actually do, and why — as shown on the real work, not as remembered in the abstract?

**Why it matters:**
- People's account of their own process is not their process. Recall smooths over friction, skips the workarounds, and idealizes the sequence — not from dishonesty, but because that is how memory works. "Show me" closes the say-do gap that interviewing leaves open.
- The most valuable knowledge is often tacit — skill the person uses without ever having written it down. They can't tell you about it because they don't know it's there. They *can* show it to you.
- It reveals what the environment, tools, and artifacts are doing — the sticky note on the monitor, the spreadsheet that bridges two systems — which no one thinks to mention.

**Where it sits in the process:**
Discovery, in the first diamond — understanding current state before defining the problem. It sits at the rich end of the ethnographic spectrum:

| Method | Mode | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| **Try it yourself** | Experience it | Your perspective, not theirs |
| **Observation / Fly on the wall** | "Let me watch" | Behavior without explanation |
| **Interviewing** | "Tell me" | Perspective and motivation, with say-do gap risk |
| **Contextual inquiry** | "Show me" | Behavior + explanation in context |

**What it is not:**
- **Not Think-Aloud Testing.** Think-aloud asks someone to attempt a task on something *you* made, to evaluate it. Contextual inquiry asks someone to show you *their own* work, to understand it. The retrospective form sits close to evaluative research — you are examining an artifact — but its purpose is still to understand how the work was done, not to judge the artifact.
- **Not a demo.** A from-scratch re-enactment ("show me how you'd do it") is neither the real thing nor an honest observation. Anchor on real work.

## Setup

**Participants:** One participant at a time, ideally. One interviewer plus, if possible, one note-taker. `[reconstructed]`

**Access:** More than an interview needs. Live inquiry needs you in the person's environment (in person or screen-shared) while real work happens. The retrospective form needs the participant to have the artifact they produced open and shareable.

**Time:** 45–90 minutes. `[reconstructed]` The field-tested retrospective run used 45 minutes. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Materials:**
- A short guide — not a script — built around the parts of the work you most need to see
- A capture scheme: a few tags you apply to notes as you go (see Steps)
- Permission to record or screenshot

## Steps

### Choose the form

First question: **can you watch the work happen?**

- **Yes → Live contextual inquiry.** "Show me as you do it." Be there while real work is done, and ask about what you see.
- **No, it's already done → Retrospective walkthrough.** A decision already made, a score already produced, an artifact already built. You can't observe it live, and asking them to redo it produces a staged demo. Instead, have them drive the artifact they actually produced and retrace the real steps on it: "show me *where* you did that." `[field-tested, n=1]`

### 1 · Plan
- Write down what you need to leave with — usually a current-state journey, plus one or two specific questions only this person can answer.
- Identify the **high-value segments** — the parts only this participant can give you — and ring-fence the most time for them. Keep everything else brisk. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- List what prior write-ups *claim* about how the work was done. Treat each claim as something to confirm in the room, not a fact to build your questions on (see Facilitation notes). `[field-tested, n=1]`

### 2 · Open (a few minutes)
- Frame it in one line: you want the real, messy version; you're not grading it or fixing it today.
- Get permission to record. Ask them to open the real work — the live task, or the artifact — and start at the beginning.

### 3 · Show me
- **Live:** let them work; interrupt lightly to ask what they're doing and why. Note the tools, artifacts, and environment doing work alongside them. `[reconstructed]`
- **Retrospective:** walk the artifact in order. At each point, ask them to show you where it happened on the artifact and walk one real example end-to-end. `[field-tested, n=1]`
- Tag notes as you go — for example: essential vs specific to this instance; friction or workaround; reusable structure. Don't redesign in the room. Just capture.

### 4 · Probe the divergences
- Ask where the work or its output disagreed with reality — where the result didn't match what they knew to be true, and what they did about it. Don't accept "it mostly matched"; walk the artifact and find the specific places. These divergences are often the most valuable thing the session produces. `[field-tested, n=1]`

### 5 · Close
- "What haven't I asked that I should have?" Thank them, and confirm you can come back with follow-ups.
- Capture notes the same day, while the context is still fresh.

## Facilitation notes

**Retrace, don't describe.** When the participant drifts into general process description ("usually we…"), pull them back to a specific point on the work: "show me where that happened here." The specific beats the summary every time. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Confirm the inputs; don't import them.** Prior write-ups — a brief, a task card, someone else's summary — are claims to verify, not facts to build questions on. If your questions assume an input was used, you've smuggled in the assumption. Ask outright what was in play. An *absent* input is itself a finding. In the field-tested run, the prior summary's list of evidence sources didn't match what had actually happened: one source had been added later, at a stakeholder's request, and wasn't part of the original method at all. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Look for what lives in their head.** The walkthrough's biggest yield in the field-tested run was tacit knowledge. The mapping from principles to interview questions existed only in the practitioner's head, written nowhere, and that explained why colleagues trying to reuse the artifact as a template were struggling. An abstract retelling would have skipped it. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Notice what the artifact doesn't record.** A retrospective walkthrough can recover some of the reasoning behind the artifact, but not all of it. If the "why" behind a decision or a number was never captured, name that as a finding rather than filling the gap from memory. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Protect the high-value segments.** If you're running long, cut depth from the parts you can reconstruct later from the artifact. Never cut from the segments only the participant can give you. `[field-tested, n=1]`

**Let silences run.** Pauses are where people reach past the rehearsed answer. `[reconstructed]`

## Variations

### Live contextual inquiry
The classic form: in the participant's environment while real work happens. Richest data; hardest access. `[reconstructed]`

### Retrospective walkthrough (artifact-anchored)
For work already complete. The participant drives the real artifact; you retrace the real steps on it. See `resources/templates/retrospective-walkthrough-guide.md` for a generalized guide. `[field-tested, n=1]`

### Remote / screen-shared
Either form works over screen share when the work is digital. You lose the physical environment — ask directly about what's around them that isn't on screen. `[reconstructed]`

---

## Outputs

- A **current-state journey** — the real sequence of steps, as shown
- Tagged notes: essential vs instance-specific, friction and workarounds, reusable artifacts
- **Divergences** — specific places where the work or its output disagreed with reality
- A record of **tacit knowledge** surfaced, and of **absent inputs or missing rationale**
