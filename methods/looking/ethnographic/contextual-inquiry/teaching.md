# Contextual Inquiry — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever designed a flow in a specific order — and then watched someone who actually does the job say, 'I never do that first. I always do this first'? Somebody on a drawing board decided the sequence without ever watching the person who does the work. That's the gap this method exists to close."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Story)

In the teaching session, the practitioner didn't explain contextual inquiry first — they showed it. A short recording of a real inquiry: a contact-center agent sharing their screen and narrating how they handle a customer call, step by step, in their real systems.

What the recording shows that no interview would: before the agent ever reaches the tool being studied, they're working through an older system first — logging in, looking things up, generating a ticket. Things pop up. Features that are supposed to be enabled sometimes aren't. None of that was in anyone's picture of "the experience," because the experience everyone had designed started later.

The facilitators in the recording mostly stayed quiet. They let the agent work, and saved their questions. A well-placed clarifying question ("does this happen a lot?") would have been fine — but never one that puts words in the participant's mouth.

**[needs practitioner]** — a first-person story from the practitioner's own experience: a moment where "show me" revealed something that "tell me" never would have.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A plan — what you want to learn, who you'll observe, a short discussion guide. Access to real people doing real work (or, for completed work, the real artifact). A way to capture and record. Ideally, a partner: one to lead, one to document.

**Outputs:** A current-state journey (before, during, after); notes codified as roses, thorns, and buds and placed against the steps they relate to; divergences between how the work was designed and how it's done; referrals to others to observe.

**Connection forward:** Notes feed Rose, Thorn, Bud → Affinity Clustering. Well-titled clusters are close to backlog-ready: "people don't realize financial help is available" is an action waiting to happen; "finance" is not.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full steps.

**Teaching activity used in the recorded session** `[practitioner-taught]`: play a short recording of a real contextual inquiry. Ask learners to take notes in three categories as they watch — **what's working well, what isn't, and what the opportunities are**. Then debrief: what did they see that the participant never said out loud? What happened *before* the main task?

This does two things at once: it demonstrates the method, and it gives learners their first practice at codifying observations — the same roses, thorns, and buds they'll use in synthesis.

**Key mechanics to emphasize:**
- **"Show me," not "tell me."** "Can you just show me how you did that?"
- **Let them work.** Occasional clarifying questions only. Don't interpret, don't lead.
- **Before, during, after.** The context around the task is data.
- **Go in pairs.** One asks, one documents — the second person catches what the first misses.
- **Watch faces, not just screens.** A wince is a question waiting to be asked.

**[needs practitioner]** — an experiential loop: how learners practice running an inquiry (not just watching one), and what the debrief should draw out.

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think of a process you designed or own. Have you ever watched someone do it — really do it, in their setting, from the step before it starts? What do you think you'd see?"*

*"Who is one person you could ask to show you their work this week — even for 20 minutes, even over screen share?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The teaching session closed on a line the practitioner keeps pinned by their desk, often attributed to the tennis player Arthur Ashe: **"Start where you are, use what you have, do what you can."**

You will rarely get the perfect participant, in the perfect setting, with the perfect access. The people available may be a step removed from the real users. The screen share may not work from the field. Do the inquiry anyway — and name what's missing. A step-removed participant is better than no participant, and watching real work beats any amount of assuming about it.

**[needs practitioner]** — confirm this is the close they'd choose.

---

## Step 7 — Supplies

- 30–60 minutes per participant
- A short plan and discussion guide
- A partner to document, or a recording (with permission)
- Screen share or in-person access to the real work — or the real artifact, for a retrospective walkthrough
- A way to capture notes against steps or screens (a whiteboard with screenshots works well)
