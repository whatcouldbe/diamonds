# Interviewing

A structured conversation with someone to understand their experiences, needs, motivations, and context — before you define the problem or design the solution.

---

## Purpose

Use when you need to understand the people you're designing for — what they actually do, what they struggle with, what matters to them, and why. Interviewing is the most common and most accessible ethnographic research method. It's a conversation with a purpose: to learn, not to validate.

**The core question:** What is this person's actual experience, and what do they need that they might not even know how to articulate?

**Why interviewing matters:**
- You can't design great solutions without understanding what people actually need — not what you assume they need
- People's stated behavior and actual behavior are often very different. As Margaret Mead said: "What people say, what people do, and what they say they do are three very different things." This isn't because people lie — it's because recall is genuinely difficult
- Without direct contact with the people you're designing for, you're building on assumptions
- Involving people in the process builds credibility — with customers and with stakeholders
- People have a natural desire to tell stories. A good interview takes advantage of that desire to surface insights you'd never find otherwise

**What makes a research interview different from a casual conversation:**
- It follows a protocol — a planned sequence of questions designed to elicit specific types of information
- It has a defined purpose — you're trying to understand something, not just chatting
- It requires a specific posture — you're there to listen, not to share, advise, or sell
- The balance is deliberate — somewhere between a casual conversation and an interrogation. Too casual and you don't get what you need. Too structured and people shut down.

**Where it sits in the process:**
Interviewing lives in the Discovery phase of the first diamond. You're trying to understand the landscape before defining the problem. It can also be used in the Develop phase to evaluate concepts (though at that point you're moving into evaluative research and the interview takes on a different character — more like a critique or a think-aloud test).

**Where it sits among ethnographic methods:**
Interviewing is one of several ways to do ethnographic research. The spectrum runs from least to most immersive:

| Method | Mode | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| **Try it yourself** | Experience it | Your perspective, not theirs |
| **Observation / Fly on the wall** | "Let me watch" | Behavior without explanation |
| **Interviewing** | "Tell me" | Perspective and motivation, with say-do gap risk |
| **Contextual inquiry** | "Show me" | Behavior + explanation in context |

Each step up requires more access, more coordination, more time — but gives you richer data. Interviewing is the sweet spot for most teams: accessible, structured, and powerful when done well.

## Setup

### Participants
- **Interviewer(s):** One person asks the questions. A second person can take notes, but keep it to two people maximum from your side — more than that and the interviewee feels outnumbered.
- **Interviewee:** The person you're trying to understand. Ideally someone who represents the people you're designing for — a user, a customer, a stakeholder, someone who lives the experience you're investigating.

**Who to interview:**
- Closer to the actual people you're designing for = better insights
- Include a range of perspectives — not just the easy-to-reach or the most enthusiastic
- Look for people who represent different contexts, experience levels, or relationships with the problem space
- After each interview, ask "who else should I talk to?" — let interviewees point you to the next conversation

### What You Need
- **A protocol** — the planned sequence of questions (see Steps below)
- **A way to record** — audio recording (with permission), typed notes, or a dedicated note-taker. Recording is strongly recommended as a backup to notes.
- **A quiet environment** — minimize distractions. If remote, ensure a stable connection.
- **Consent** — permission to interview, to record, and clarity on how the information will be used.

### How Many Interviews
The same saturation principle from evaluative research applies: 5–7 interviews typically reveals approximately 80% of what you need to know. Each additional interview yields diminishing returns. Better to do five interviews and iterate than plan for twenty and never start.

If you're doing ongoing discovery, plan for multiple small rounds of 5–7 interviews across the project rather than one large batch.

### Time
- 30–60 minutes per interview is typical
- 45 minutes is a good default — enough time to get past surface-level answers, short enough to maintain energy and attention
- Budget 15 minutes before and after for setup and debrief

## Steps

### Step 1 — "I am..." (Introduce Yourself and the Purpose)
Introduce yourself, your team, and the purpose of the research. Be specific about what you're trying to understand and why their perspective matters. Keep it brief — you're not here to present.

*Example: "Thanks for taking the time to speak with me today. We're working on [project context], and I'd like to understand your current experience with [topic area]. Your perspective will directly inform how we approach this."*

### Step 2 — "May I..." (Get Permissions)
Ask for permission to record. Confirm the time commitment. Give them a chance to ask questions before you begin. This step establishes consent and puts the interviewee at ease.

*Example: "This should take about 45 minutes — is that still OK? Would you be willing to let me record our conversation so I can refer back to it during synthesis? You may hear me typing notes, but the recording is a helpful backup. Any questions before we start?"*

### Step 3 — "Tell me..." (The Conversation)
This is the bulk of the interview. Ask open-ended questions, follow the thread, and probe for underlying motivations with "why."

**Structure within "Tell me":**

**Start with easy warm-up questions.** Begin with something anyone can answer without thinking hard — their role, their background, how long they've been doing what they do. This builds rapport and gets them talking before you go deep.

**Move from general to specific.** Start with landscape questions (how has this changed for you?), then pain points (what's frustrating?), then specific recent instances (tell me about the last time you...).

**Ask about specific moments, not generalities.** "Walk me through your morning yesterday" not "tell me about your morning." "Tell me about the last trip you planned" not "how do you plan a vacation." Specificity helps with recall and gets past the say-do gap.

**Get people to tell stories.** The most useful information comes in narrative form — a sequence of events with context, decisions, emotions, and outcomes. "Tell me about a time when..." is the prompt that unlocks this.

**Use "why" to get to underlying motivations.** When someone tells you what they do, ask why. When they tell you what's frustrating, ask why. "Why" is the probe that turns a surface observation into an insight about what actually matters to them.

**Cover three dimensions:**
- What's working well? (happy face) — what do they value, what would they want to keep?
- What's not working? What are they struggling with? (sad face) — where is the pain, the friction, the workaround?
- If they could push a magic button, what would it do? (magic button) — what's the desired future state? Follow up with "why is that important?" to get past the surface answer.

**Use section transitions to signal topic shifts.** When moving between topic areas, name the transition: "Now I'd like to talk about [next topic]..." This gives the interviewee a sense of movement through the conversation and resets their attention.

### Step 4 — "Is there anything else?"
Before closing, ask if there's anything you haven't touched on. This catches the things your protocol missed — the things the interviewee has been wanting to say but didn't have an opening for.

Also ask: "If I could only speak with one other person to gain better insight, who would that be?"

### Step 5 — "Thank you"
Close with genuine gratitude. Let them know what happens next — how their input will be used, whether you might follow up. Ask if it's okay to reach out again if you have follow-up questions.

## Facilitation Notes

### The Do's

**Ask open-ended questions.** "Tell me about..." and "Walk me through..." are your primary tools. If you catch yourself asking a yes/no question, follow up with "why."

**Listen more than you talk.** Two ears, two eyes, one mouth — use them in that proportion. The interviewee should be talking 80% of the time or more.

**Ask about specific instances, not generalities.** "Tell me about the last time you..." not "How do you usually..."

**Get comfortable with silence.** When someone pauses, don't rush to fill it. The most honest, reflective answers often come after an uncomfortable silence. One technique: take a drink of water after asking a question — it naturally prevents you from jumping in.

**Follow the thread.** When something unexpected comes up, leave the script and pursue it. The most valuable insights are often the ones you didn't think to ask about. You can always return to the protocol.

**Start with easy questions.** Build rapport before going deep. "Tell me about your role" costs nothing and gets them talking.

**Look for verbal and visual cues.** What their face says might contradict what their mouth says. A pause, a grimace, a laugh — these are signals to probe further.

**Take good notes.** Even if you're recording, capture key quotes, observations, and your own reactions in real time. You'll need them for synthesis.

**Thank people.** Feedback and participation are gifts. People who feel valued will be more open and may connect you to others.

### The Don'ts

**Don't lead the witness.** Keep your assumptions out of the question. "How was that experience?" not "That must have been frustrating, right?" Don't put words in people's mouths.

**Don't ask double-barreled questions.** "How do you feel about the onboarding process and the support you got from your manager?" is two questions. Pick one.

**Don't interrupt.** Even when — especially when — they're going somewhere unexpected. Let them finish the thought.

**Don't project your own experience.** "Oh yeah, I had the same problem" shifts the conversation to be about you. You're not the subject.

**Don't ask people to predict their future behavior.** "Would you use this?" or "How much would you pay?" — people are terrible at predicting what they'll do. Ask about what they've actually done. You can ask about a desired future state (the magic button question), but that's different from predicting behavior.

**Don't pitch during discovery.** The interview is for understanding, not for validating your solution. The moment you start sharing your ideas, you've changed from learning to selling.

## Variations

### Remote / Video Interview
Same protocol, different medium. Works well for geographically distributed research. Ensure stable connection, ask for camera on if possible (to read visual cues), and be aware that some rapport-building cues are lost over video.

### Phone Interview
Lighter-weight than video. Useful when the interviewee is more comfortable with a phone call or when video isn't practical. You lose visual cues entirely — listen more carefully for tone, pauses, and hesitation.

### Paired Interview (Two Interviewees)
Interview two people together — such as a manager and a direct report, or two colleagues who collaborate. The dynamic between them reveals things a solo interview wouldn't. Be aware of power dynamics that might suppress honest answers.

### Contextual Interview
A hybrid between interviewing and observation. Conduct the interview in the interviewee's environment — at their desk, in their workspace, on the factory floor. Ask them to show you how they do things rather than just describing them. This bridges the say-do gap more directly than a standard interview. More resource-intensive but produces richer data.

### Simulated Interview
An AI agent inhabits a described persona and responds to interview questions as that persona would. Useful for:
- Testing and refining a protocol before using it with real people
- Generating initial hypotheses when access to real users is limited
- Training interviewers on technique and follow-up probing

The agent must be transparent: simulated interviews surface likely patterns and reactions but are not a substitute for real human interviews. The data is synthetic, not validated.

### Expert / Stakeholder Interview
Same method, different subject. Instead of interviewing end users, interview internal stakeholders, domain experts, or people adjacent to the problem space. Useful in early discovery when you're mapping the landscape. The protocol may be more targeted and the conversation more peer-to-peer.

## Outputs

- **Raw notes and/or recordings** — the primary data. Capture quotes, observations, and your own reactions.
- **Key quotes** — verbatim statements that capture an insight, a pain point, or a surprising perspective. These become the evidence base for design decisions.
- **Themes and patterns** — emerging patterns across interviews. These are raw material for synthesis, not conclusions yet.
- **Questions for further research** — things you didn't expect that need deeper investigation.
- **Referrals** — names of other people to interview, surfaced through "who else should I talk to?"
- **Empathy and credibility** — intangible but real. Having spoken to the people you're designing for changes how you make decisions and how stakeholders trust your recommendations.

The notes from interviews are raw material for synthesis — they feed into affinity clustering, journey mapping, persona development, and problem framing.

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
Interviewing lives in **Looking / Ethnographic** and is used primarily in the **Discovery** phase of the first diamond. You're gathering raw data about people's experiences before defining the problem. It can extend into the Develop phase for evaluative purposes, but at that point the interview takes on a different character.

### Upstream (what informs the interview)
- **Project brief or research questions** — what you're trying to understand
- **Stakeholder interviews** — understanding the organizational context before talking to users
- **Secondary research** — desk research, analytics, existing data that shapes your protocol
- **Prior synthesis** — if this isn't the first round, previous affinity clusters or insights inform new questions

### Downstream Methods (interview data feeds into)
- **Affinity Clustering** — group interview notes to find themes across conversations
- **Journey Mapping / Experience Diagramming** — map the experiences described in interviews into a visual flow
- **Persona Development** — synthesize patterns across interviews into representative archetypes
- **Problem Framing** — use insights from interviews to define the right problem to solve
- **Importance / Difficulty Matrix** — prioritize the themes or opportunities surfaced through interviews
- **Critique** — present synthesized findings to stakeholders for feedback

### Related Methods
- **Observation / Fly on the Wall** — watch behavior without intervening. Complements interviewing by revealing what people do versus what they say they do.
- **Contextual Inquiry** — interviewing plus observation, in the interviewee's environment. "Show me" instead of "tell me."
- **Try It Yourself / Walk a Mile** — experience the product or service yourself. No access to people required, but you get your perspective rather than theirs.
- **Focus Groups** — group discussion rather than one-on-one. Faster but susceptible to social dynamics: loudest voice, pile-on, people presenting a better version of themselves. Interviewing produces more honest individual perspectives.
- **Surveys** — quantitative complement to interviews. Surveys tell you how many; interviews tell you why.
- **Critique** — related posture (receiving information through a structured protocol) but different purpose: Critique evaluates an artifact, interviewing discovers experiences.
