# Interviewing — FAQ

Questions that come up repeatedly when teams learn or run research interviews for the first time.

---

**How many people do I need to interview?**
Five to seven interviews per round typically reveals approximately 80% of what you need to know. This is the saturation point — each additional interview yields diminishing returns. Plan for multiple small rounds across the project rather than one large batch. Better to do five and iterate than plan for twenty and never start.

**How long should an interview be?**
45 minutes is a good default. Enough time to get past surface-level answers, short enough to maintain energy and attention. Budget 15 minutes before and after for setup and debrief.

**Can I interview with two people from my team?**
Yes, but keep it to two maximum — one interviewer, one note-taker. More than that and the interviewee feels outnumbered. The note-taker should be mostly silent.

**What if the interviewee only gives short answers?**
Follow up with "tell me more about that" or "why is that?" Ask about a specific recent instance: "Think about the last time that happened — walk me through it." Silence also helps — resist the urge to fill it. If they're still guarded, the rapport-building may need more time, or the topic may be sensitive in ways you hadn't anticipated.

**What if they go off-topic?**
Follow the thread for a bit — off-topic tangents often reveal unexpected insights. If it's genuinely unproductive, gently redirect: "That's really interesting. Going back to [topic], I'm curious about..." Don't cut people off sharply.

**Can I do interviews remotely?**
Yes. Remote/video interviews work well. Ensure a stable connection, ask for cameras on if possible (to read visual cues), and be aware that some rapport-building cues are lost. Phone interviews are even lighter-weight but you lose visual cues entirely.

**Should I send questions in advance?**
Generally no. Spontaneous answers are more authentic than prepared ones. However, you can share the general topic area ("I'd like to talk about your experience with [topic]") so they know what to expect. Don't share the specific questions.

**What do I do with the notes after?**
Interview notes are raw material for synthesis. The most common next step is affinity clustering — grouping notes across interviews to find themes. Notes can also feed into journey mapping, persona development, or problem framing. Don't treat individual interview notes as conclusions — synthesis across multiple interviews is where the insights live.

**Is it OK to record the interview?**
Yes, with explicit permission. Always ask. Explain that it's for your reference during synthesis, not for publication or sharing. Even with recording, take notes in real time — you'll review notes far more often than you'll re-listen to a recording.

**What's the difference between an interview and a focus group?**
An interview is one-on-one (or two-on-one with a note-taker). A focus group is a group discussion. Interviews produce more honest individual perspectives because there's no social pressure. Focus groups are faster but susceptible to group dynamics — loudest voice, pile-on, people presenting a better version of themselves.

**Can AI run a simulated interview?**
Yes — see `ai-usage.md`. Simulated interviews are useful for testing protocols, generating initial hypotheses, and training interviewers. They are not a substitute for talking to real people. The data is synthetic, not validated.

**What if I don't have access to actual users?**
Try other ethnographic methods that require less access: observe how people interact with similar products, try the product or service yourself, or run simulated interviews to generate hypotheses. But push hard to get access — even one real conversation is more valuable than extensive internal assumption.
