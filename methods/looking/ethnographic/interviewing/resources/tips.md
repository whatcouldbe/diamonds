# Interviewing — Tips & Advice

The complete do's and don'ts for research interviews. These are the things that separate a productive interview from a wasted conversation.

---

## The Do's

**Ask open-ended questions.**
"Tell me about..." and "Walk me through..." are your primary tools. "Tell me" is the single best way to open a research question. If you catch yourself asking a yes/no question, follow up with "why."

**Listen more than you talk.**
Two ears, two eyes, one mouth — use them in that proportion. The interviewee should be talking 80% of the time or more. Your job is to create space for them, not to fill it.

**Ask about specific instances, not generalities.**
"Walk me through your morning yesterday" not "tell me about your morning." "Tell me about the last trip you planned" not "how do you plan a vacation." Specificity helps with recall and gets past the say-do gap. This is the single most effective interviewing technique.

**Get people to tell stories.**
The most useful information comes in narrative form. "Tell me about a time when..." unlocks stories with context, decisions, emotions, and outcomes — all of which are useful data.

**Get comfortable with silence.**
When someone pauses, don't rush to fill it. The most honest, reflective answers come after an uncomfortable silence. Technique: take a drink of water after asking a question — it naturally prevents you from jumping in.

**Follow the thread.**
When something unexpected comes up, leave the script and pursue it. You can always return to the protocol. The most valuable insights are often the ones you didn't think to ask about.

**Start with easy questions.**
"Tell me about your role" costs nothing and gets them talking. Build rapport before going deep. This eases off the inquisition feeling and establishes a conversational tone.

**Look for verbal and visual cues.**
What their face says might contradict what their mouth says. A pause, a grimace, a laugh — these are signals to probe further: "I noticed you hesitated — what's behind that?"

**Ask follow-up questions to get to underlying motivations.**
Even if someone answers yes or no, follow up with "why." The magic wand question ("if you could push a button, what would it do?") surfaces desired future state. Always follow with "why is that important?" to get past the surface answer.

**Take good notes.**
Even if you're recording, capture key quotes, observations, and your own reactions in real time. You'll need them for synthesis and they're much faster to review than a full recording.

**Thank people.**
Close with genuine gratitude. People who feel valued will be more open, may agree to follow-up conversations, and may connect you to others.

**When interviewing a domain expert, give them the floor first.**
If the person you're interviewing has done their own research or has deep expertise in the topic — they've been running experiments, synthesizing findings, or thinking hard about the problem — don't open with your warm-up questions. Open with "What have you been finding?" or "What's your read on this right now?" Let them share what they know before you start asking questions. This acknowledges their work, reduces defensiveness, and often surfaces something your protocol didn't cover. You can transition into the protocol once they've had the floor: "That's useful context — I want to make sure I explore some of this more deeply."

**Ask who else to talk to.**
"If I could only speak with one other person to gain better insight, who would that be?" Constraining to one person forces the best lead. Let interviewees point you to the next conversation.

**Ask if there's anything else.**
Before closing: "Is there anything we haven't touched on that you'd like to share?" This catches the things your protocol missed.

## The Don'ts

**Don't lead the witness.**
Keep your assumptions out of the question. "How was that experience?" not "That must have been frustrating, right?" Don't put words in people's mouths.

**Don't ask double-barreled questions.**
"How do you feel about the onboarding process and the support you got from your manager?" is two questions. The interviewee answers whichever one they grab onto and the other gets lost. Ask one question at a time.

**Don't interrupt.**
Even when — especially when — they're going somewhere unexpected. Let them finish the thought. Interrupting breaks their train of thought and signals that your agenda matters more than their experience.

**Don't project your own experience.**
"Oh yeah, I had the same problem" shifts the conversation to be about you. You're not the subject. Resist the urge to relate, empathize aloud, or share your own story.

**Don't ask people to predict their future behavior.**
"Would you use this?" or "How much would you pay for that?" — people are terrible at predicting what they'll do. Ask about what they've actually done. You can ask about a desired future state (magic button), but that's different from predicting behavior.

**Don't pitch during discovery.**
The interview is for understanding, not for validating your solution. The moment you start sharing your ideas, you've changed the dynamic from learning to selling. Save it for evaluative research.

---

## Before and After

**Debrief immediately.**
Within 15 minutes of the interview, capture your top-of-mind observations, surprising moments, and gut reactions. These fade fast and they're often the most valuable notes.

**Iterate the protocol.**
If early interviews reveal new topics, better questions, or dead-end questions, update the protocol. The first interview should inform the second. Don't run the same flawed protocol five times.

**You need fewer interviews than you think.**
5–7 interviews typically reveals approximately 80% of what you need to know. Each additional interview yields diminishing returns. Better to do five interviews and iterate than plan for twenty and never start.
