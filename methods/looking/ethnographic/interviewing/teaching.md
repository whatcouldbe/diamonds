# Interviewing — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever built something — a product, a feature, a process — and when it got in front of real people, they didn't use it the way you expected? Or didn't use it at all? And you thought: how did we miss that? We probably missed it because we never asked. We designed based on what we assumed people needed, not what they actually needed."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

Ethnographic research exists because of a fundamental gap between what people say, what they do, and what they say they do. Margaret Mead captured this perfectly. It's not that people lie — it's that recall is genuinely hard, and people present a better version of their behavior than what actually happens.

Justin's Crayola story illustrates this perfectly: ask parents how often they do creative activities with their kids and they'll say "all the time." Go and actually watch, and you find creativity is mostly a solitary, keep-busy activity — heavily constrained by rules about mess. No good clothes, no carpet, no dining room table, no car. That gap between the stated experience ("we're creative all the time") and the observed reality (constrained, solitary, rule-bound) became the insight that led to Color Wonder — a $60M product built on the question "what if we could guarantee no mess?"

That insight didn't come from a survey. It didn't come from a brainstorm. It came from spending time with real people and paying attention to the gap between what they said and what they did.

Interviewing is the most accessible way to start closing that gap. It's a structured conversation — not a casual chat, not an interrogation — with a protocol designed to help people tell you about their actual experience rather than their idealized version of it.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A protocol (planned sequence of open-ended questions), an interviewee (someone who represents the people you're designing for), a way to record and capture notes, and a clear understanding of what you're trying to learn.

**Outputs:** Raw notes and key quotes, emerging themes across interviews, questions for further research, referrals to other people to talk to, and — intangibly but critically — empathy and credibility that changes how the team makes decisions.

**Connection forward:** Interview notes are raw material for synthesis. They feed into affinity clustering (find the themes), journey mapping (map the experience), persona development (identify the archetypes), and problem framing (define the right problem to solve). Interviewing is the beginning of the sense-making pipeline, not the end.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full protocol and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **The protocol drawing** — "I am" (introduction), "May I" (permissions), "Tell me" (the bulk of the interview with happy face / sad face / magic button), "Why?" (the underlying motivation probe), "Is there anything else?" (catch what you missed), "Thank you" (close with gratitude). The proportions matter — the "Tell me" block is the majority of the time.
- **Open-ended questions are the primary tool** — "Tell me about..." and "Walk me through..." are the workhorses. If you catch yourself asking a yes/no question, follow up with "why."
- **Specific instances over generalities** — "Tell me about the last time you..." not "How do you usually..." This is the single most effective technique for getting past the say-do gap.
- **Listen more than you talk** — two ears, two eyes, one mouth. Use them in that proportion. The interviewee should be talking 80% of the time.
- **Get comfortable with silence** — don't fill pauses. The most reflective, honest answers come after the uncomfortable silence. Technique: take a drink of water after asking a question.
- **The magic button question** — "If you could push a button, what would it do?" surfaces desired future state, not predicted behavior. Follow up with "Why is that important?" to get past surface answers. Even throwaway answers can lead to real insights (the food safety story: "print money" → need for recognition → system design change).

**When teaching someone to interview:** Use the experiential loop. Have the learner write a short protocol (5–7 questions on a topic they're curious about), then conduct a practice interview with you or a colleague. Debrief: what worked? Where did they lead the witness? Where did they interrupt? Where did silence produce a better answer? Then have them revise the protocol based on what they learned and run a second interview. The skill develops through practice, not instruction.

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about the last product or project decision you made. What was it based on? Internal assumptions? Data? Or actual conversations with the people affected by that decision?"*

*"Where in your current work are you making assumptions about what people need that you haven't actually validated? What's one question you could ask a real person this week that might challenge those assumptions?"*

**If the experiential loop was used:** *"What surprised you about the conversation? Where did you learn something you didn't expect? What was hard — was it the silence, the urge to share your own experience, the temptation to solve their problem?"*

Close with a commitment: *"Who is one person you could interview this week — even informally, even for 15 minutes — about what they actually experience?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

Interviewing looks simple — you're just talking to someone. But the skill is in what you don't do. You don't lead. You don't interrupt. You don't project your own experience. You don't pitch. You don't fill the silence. You ask a question and you sit with whatever comes back, even when it's uncomfortable or surprising or contradicts what you assumed.

The protocol — "I am, May I, Tell me, Why, Anything else, Thank you" — fits on an index card. But it represents a fundamentally different posture toward the people you're designing for: curiosity instead of assumption, listening instead of telling, discovering instead of confirming.

The Crayola team didn't invent Color Wonder by being clever in a conference room. They invented it by watching real families and noticing what nobody had asked about. The insight was there the whole time. They just had to go look.

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- 30–60 minutes per interview (45 minutes is a good default)
- A written protocol — printed, on screen, or on an index card
- Recording device or video call recording (with permission)
- Note-taking method — laptop, notebook, or dedicated note-taker
- One interviewee per session
- The interview protocol drawing (optional — useful when explaining the structure)
