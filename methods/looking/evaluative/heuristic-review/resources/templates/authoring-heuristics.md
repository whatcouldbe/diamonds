# Authoring Heuristics

*Grounded in What Could Be client kits. Client names and client heuristics are omitted; the example below is illustrative.*

Use when the thing being evaluated isn't an interface: a learning experience, a program, a policy, a proposal. The team writes its own rules of thumb for what good looks like, then applies them.

---

## Part 1: Write the heuristics

**Start from a shared definition of success.** What does "good" mean in this domain? If the team can't agree, stop here; that's the real work.

**Draft 4 to 6 heuristics.** For each:

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Short, Verb + Outcome style (e.g. "Builds Autonomy") |
| **Guiding question** | One plain-language question a reviewer can ask of any example |
| **Why it matters** | 2 to 3 sentences |
| **Look-fors** | 3 to 5 bullets: what good looks like in practice |

Write plainly, no jargon. Each heuristic should be self-contained, like a guide card.

**Heuristics are not a rubric.** They are mental shortcuts: easier to recall, less precise. Resist adding levels and sub-criteria.

**Test them.** Walk a real example (or a generated scenario, see `../ai-usage.md`) through the heuristics. Note where it's strong, where it falls short, and where the heuristics themselves don't discriminate. Refine.

### Illustrative example (not client material)

> **Invites Participation**
> *Guiding question:* Does every person in the room have a real way to contribute, not just the confident ones?
> *Why it matters:* Workshops that rely on open discussion hear from the same few voices. The quieter people often hold the most useful observations. If the format doesn't create a way in, their knowledge is lost.
> *Look-fors:*
> - Silent individual work before group discussion
> - More than one way to contribute (writing, sketching, speaking)
> - The facilitator actively draws in people who haven't spoken
> - Contributions are captured where everyone can see them

---

## Part 2 (optional): Scored self-assessment

For a person evaluating their own work before submitting it.

1. **Read your work** as a reviewer would. Note which heuristics feel strong and which feel weak.
2. **Score each heuristic:**
   - **Green:** clearly meets it; evident in the work
   - **Yellow:** partially; lacks clarity or evidence
   - **Red:** missing
3. **Readiness** = share of heuristics scored green. Yellow counts as not yet.
4. **Get a second opinion.** Another reviewer, or an AI prompted to ask clarifying questions first, then score each heuristic with strengths, gaps, and suggestions.
5. **Revise** the reds and yellows. **Re-score.** Repeat until ready.

Self-assessment carries the author's blind spot. Where the stakes are real, have someone else score it too.
