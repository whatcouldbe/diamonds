# Heuristic Review: Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

## Step 1: Context (the hook) `[reconstructed]`

*"You already use heuristics every day. Look both ways before you cross. Measure twice, cut once. They're rules of thumb: not perfect, but right often enough that they keep you out of trouble without having to think hard. Design has rules of thumb too. People who've watched thousands of people use thousands of things have learned that whether something succeeds can often be predicted by how well it follows a handful of principles. This method lets anyone on your team carry those principles into a review."*

## Step 2: Content (the story) `[needs practitioner]`

The LUMA module opens with an evaluator operating a piece of industrial equipment who found many places where the rules of thumb for good design were broken. That example belongs to LUMA's materials.

What's missing is a What Could Be story: a time a heuristic review caught something early, or a time a team trusted a clean heuristic review and real users still struggled. The second kind is especially valuable for Diamonds, because it teaches the limit of the method.

---

## Step 3: Inputs and outputs

- **In:** something to review, a heuristic set, three key tasks, 3 to 5 reviewers.
- **Out:** a pooled list of issues, each tied to a heuristic and a task, and a short watch-list of things to confirm with real people.

Key insight to land: *a heuristic review finds the problems you can predict. Real people find the ones you can't.*

---

## Step 4: Activity (Eye-Spy) *grounded*

A practice activity from the LUMA materials. It teaches the heuristics by spotting them in the wild before applying them to your own work.

1. Show a handful of familiar designs (a phone's email reply screen, a parking sign, a checkout form, a remote control).
2. For each, ask: *"Which heuristics are at play here?"*
3. Participants place a sticky next to a heuristic: one colour for **good** (the design follows it), another for **bad** (the design breaks it).
4. Each sticky says **why**: what a person would experience.
5. Debrief: which heuristics were easy to spot, which were hard, where people disagreed.

The good/bad split matters. Spotting where a design *follows* a heuristic teaches what the heuristic means, not only how to find faults.

---

## Step 5: Reflection

Questions to ask after the activity:
- Which heuristic did you find yourself using most? Which did you never use?
- Where did two people cite different heuristics for the same thing? Does it matter?
- What problems could this method never find? *(Draw out: whether people want it at all, whether it fits their real context, whether it solves the right problem.)*
