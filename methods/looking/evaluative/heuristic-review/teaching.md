# Heuristic Review: Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`). Captured from a teaching session with Justin Knecht, who teaches this method. Quoted lines are close to how he says them; use them as a guide to tone, not a script.

---

## Step 1: Context (the hook)

Open with evaluation as a family of methods, and the gap this one fills:

*"There are a lot of ways we can evaluate good design. One we turn to a lot is Think-Aloud Testing, where we walk people through a design or an existing experience and have them tell us what they're experiencing. But we don't always have access to users."*

Then the idea that makes the method possible:

*"There's been real work done on what good design actually is, especially good user experience and interaction design. Those rules of design, sometimes called rules of thumb, or heuristics, or design principles, are things we can expertly evaluate a design against."*

---

## Step 2: Content (lineage, the walk, and the story)

**Lineage, briefly.** The most famous published set is Jakob Nielsen's ten heuristics. The ten you'll use were adapted from Nielsen's by LUMA Institute. Teams and companies also write their own. Name which set you're teaching before anything else: LUMA's ten for people who use LUMA methods, Nielsen's ten otherwise, or the team's own if it has one.

**What you actually do.** *"We take a series of screens or an experience and we, as designers, walk through it. It's a bit like a walk a mile, with rules. For each screen or step we evaluate whether it shows good design, or is breaking a heuristic, or falling short of it."*

**Name the hard part up front.** *"Probably the most difficult part is knowing the ten heuristics and internalizing them."* That's why everyone gets a reference to keep beside them, and why you practice (Step 4) before reviewing real work.

**The story: make your own heuristics.** The story Justin tells is about authoring heuristics, not just applying published ones:

- Published heuristics describe what's objectively good in experience design. But on many projects, you need design principles that define what good means *for that project*, so you can evaluate against your own design values.
- On one project, a principle was **"less but better."** You could call it the simplicity heuristic. But as the project's own principle, it forced the team to remove things.
- **Heuristics aren't values.** Team principles or brand behaviours guide how you act. A heuristic is evaluative: you hold a design up against it and judge whether it's good. They're closely related and often confused.
- **Heuristics can come out of other methods.** On a transportation system redesign (getting things from A to B), the team used Alternative Worlds to study other systems that move things: network packets, communicable diseases, logistics, electrical transmission. From those came principles like using transfer stations near the urban core that hand off to different modes for the last mile, the way electricity changes from long-distance transmission to local distribution, or logistics goes plane to truck to conveyor.

---

## Step 3: Inputs and outputs

- **In:** something to review (screens, a flow, an experience), a heuristic set with a reference sheet, a few key tasks, one or more reviewers.
- **Out:** notes tagged by heuristic, each saying met or not met and why; a score per heuristic and a total; a prioritized list of fixes.

---

## Step 4: Activity (Eye-Spy)

Right after previewing the heuristics, practice them before reviewing anything real.

1. Hand out the heuristics reference.
2. *"I'm going to walk through some examples. I want you to spot which heuristic you're seeing, whether it's being met or not, and why."*
3. Walk through 10 to 12 images. Examples Justin uses:
   - **A lit keyboard:** accounts for the environment it's used in (Constraints).
   - **A map with the land and sea colours swapped:** breaks a mental model everyone holds (Match).
   - **A phone email reply screen** (from the LUMA activity board).
4. On a board, one sticky colour for **good**, another for **bad**, each with the **why**.

**Alternative: the lunch line.** A colleague's version for workshops and conferences: hand out the heuristics and send people to review the lunch line. Have they anticipated the needs of people with dietary restrictions? Is the line set up counter-intuitively, for example where the plates are versus the cutlery? It's playful, and it shows the heuristics work on everyday, non-screen experiences.

---

## Step 5: Reflection

- Which heuristics were easy to spot? Which were hard?
- Where did two people cite different heuristics for the same thing, or disagree on good versus bad?
- If you were designing principles for your own project, which of these would you keep, and what's missing?
- What might a heuristic review miss that watching a real person would catch?

---

## Step 6: Final moment (conviction close)

**You can measure good design.**

*"This is a way to look at an existing system and make it better. And when you're building something new, it's a way to evaluate it against the rules of good design before you share it with people. You do a review, you see how severe the problems are, you prioritize the fixes that will have the most effect, and you can score it, so you can measure the difference in design quality. Good experience design can be qualitative, but there are clever ways to turn it into a score. If you know a design needs certain affordances, why wouldn't you use them as a guide, and as a way to evaluate and measure improvement?"*

And the stance on evaluation in general: *"You need ways, as part of a good looking and research practice, to evaluate your designs. There are lots of ways to do that, and ideally you combine them. The most important thing is that you do it."*

## Step 7: Supplies

- Heuristics reference for the chosen set (LUMA, Nielsen, or the team's own), one per person
- Eye-Spy slides or board: 10 to 12 example images
- Two colours of sticky notes (good / bad), pens, or a virtual whiteboard
- A scorecard for the chosen set (`resources/templates/scorecard-template.md`)
