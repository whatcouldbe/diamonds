# Worked Example — The "What Could Be" Design-Readiness Diagnostic

A complete worked example: eight think-aloud sessions on a real product, carried through the full synthesis pipeline to a prioritised, owned backlog. This is the canonical illustration of the method *and* its downstream flow — and it's the teaching story in `../../teaching.md`.

---

## The product

A design-readiness diagnostic — a clean online assessment built in Typeform. Four quadrants (Vision, Impact, People, Challenges), one question at a time, yes / no / somewhat answers, an encouraging tone, a forthcoming report. Simple. Under ten minutes to complete.

## The "before" — why this story matters

The team were already advocates of think-aloud testing. The product seemed simple, and they thought it was *probably close*. They weren't being naive — they ran the test precisely *because* believing in the method means you check rather than assume, especially when you think you're nearly there. The point was never to confirm they were right; it was that you always learn new material.

This is the most important part of the example: it isn't a story about an overconfident team being humbled. It's a story about a disciplined team testing something they thought was nearly done — which is exactly the situation where everyone else skips the test.

## The test

- **Cohort of eight** respondents.
- **Mixed mode:** some moderated over Zoom (watching the participant share their screen), some moderated in person (sitting alongside).
- Every session recorded; transcripts extracted afterwards.

---

## What the narration revealed

On the surface, people *loved* it:
- *"Design is clean and simple."*
- *"I quite like this model — it's neat, it's precise."*
- *"Super clean interface."*

A satisfaction rating, or a quick demo, would have shipped it. But watching people *use* it exposed a layer of failure that no rating would ever catch:

| What broke | In their own words |
|---|---|
| **Links were invisible** — underlined text wasn't recognised as clickable | *"Didn't realise these were links until Impact."* · *"If these were links, I blew right by them."* · *"A different colour, you would have realised."* |
| **"WE" confused everyone** — the inclusive language backfired, repeatedly | *"Who is we?"* · *"I don't know who 'we' is."* · *"Assuming the we is me again."* |
| **The progress bar destroyed its own trust** | *"Crikey — now my completion's gone backwards. I was on 86%."* |
| **People lost their place** relative to the four-quadrant model they'd admired up front | *"I lost track of my journey… I'm very visual, I wanted to see which section I was in."* |
| **The ending was a cliff** | *"Now what?"* · *"I don't know what else to do now."* · *"I was expecting to see my report."* |

And — a gift no script could write — the method narrated its *own* value from inside the data:
- *"It's helping me think aloud how much I know."*
- *"It helped me to think out loud and consider things I might not otherwise have done."*

## The downstream pipeline

The team didn't stop at a pile of observations. They carried the data all the way to a plan:

```
8 think-aloud sessions → recordings → transcripts
    ↓
Rose / Thorn / Bud — every screen of the experience coded
    ↓
Affinity Clustering — the coded data grouped into themes
    ↓
Bullseye diagram — the cluster headings prioritised by proximity to centre
    ↓
MoSCoW backlog (Must / Should / Could / Won't) — with named owners
    ↓
Next iteration
```

A flavour of the resulting backlog, straight from the board:

- **Must:** ensure people aren't discouraged by their level of readiness; emphasise the *project* over "design thinking"; set expectations at the beginning (why / what / when / how / who); remove how-to text from the standard responses; make the called-out text tweaks; make links obviously clickable.
- **Should:** let people name their project; remind people the report is coming so they don't try to take notes; show them their score with a quick interpretation; reinforce place and wayfinding with section imagery.
- **Could / Won't:** further report reminders; customised action-item text; deferred items parked honestly rather than silently dropped.

---

## What to take from it

- **Eight people and a sub-ten-minute assessment produced an entire prioritised roadmap.** That's the answer to "how many people?" and "is it worth the time?" in one example: tiny input, rich actionable output.
- **The surface said "clean and simple"; the behaviour said "I can't find the links, I don't know who 'we' is, and I've hit a dead-end."** Only watching real use bridges that gap.
- **Coding the *whole* experience, not just the headline moments, is what generates the volume of data** — and clustering before prioritising is what turns that volume into something a team can act on.
- **Prioritise the cluster headings, not the individual stickies.** The Bullseye and MoSCoW operate on themes, not raw notes.
