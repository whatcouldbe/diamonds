# Think-Aloud Testing — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever built something you were sure was clear — simple, clean, basically done — and then watched one real person try to use it and quietly fall apart? They can't find the link you thought was obvious. They don't know who 'we' refers to. They get to the end and say 'now what?' None of that shows up in a satisfaction score. You only see it when you watch someone use the thing and hear what's going through their head."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The "What Could Be" Story)

Think-aloud testing is the discipline of putting someone's brain on speakerphone — asking them to narrate their experience out loud while they perform a task — so you can hear, in the moment, where it works and where it breaks. It comes out of usability research, and it's behavioural: you're watching what people *do*, not collecting what they *think*.

The story that makes it land is the **"What Could Be" design-readiness diagnostic** — a clean, simple online assessment (four quadrants: Vision, Impact, People, Challenges; one question at a time; an encouraging tone). The team were already advocates of this method. The product seemed simple, and they thought it was *probably close*. They weren't being naive — they ran the test precisely because believing in the method means you check rather than assume, especially when you think you're nearly there. As we always do, they expected to learn something new.

They tested with a cohort of **eight** people, some over Zoom, some in person, recording each session.

On the surface, people loved it: *"design is clean and simple,"* *"I quite like this model, it's neat, it's precise,"* *"super clean interface."* A thumbs-up rating would have shipped it. But the narration exposed a whole layer underneath that no rating would ever catch:
- **The links were invisible.** Almost nobody realised the underlined text was clickable — *"didn't realise these were links until Impact,"* *"if these were links, I blew right by them."*
- **"WE" broke people.** *"Who is we?"* *"I don't know who 'we' is."* *"Assuming the we is me again."* The inclusive language quietly backfired, over and over.
- **The progress bar destroyed its own trust.** *"Crikey — now my completion's gone backwards. I was on 86%."*
- **People lost their place** in the four-quadrant model they'd admired up front — *"I lost track of my journey… I'm very visual, I wanted to see which section I was in."*
- **The ending was a cliff.** *"Now what?"* *"I don't know what else to do now."* *"I was expecting to see my report."*

And the method narrated its own value from inside the data — one participant: *"it's helping me think aloud how much I know,"* another: *"it helped me to think out loud and consider things I might not otherwise have done."*

Then the team carried it downstream: every screen coded as Rose / Thorn / Bud, affinity-clustered, run through a Bullseye to prioritise, sorted into a Must / Should / Could / Won't backlog with named owners. Eight people, a sub-ten-minute assessment — and out came a complete, prioritised roadmap for the next iteration.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** The thing being tested (a rough prototype, an existing experience, or a real product), a few key tasks framed as goals to accomplish, 6–8 respondents as close to the target user as you can get, a way to record screen and voice (with permission), and a quiet space.

**Outputs:** Recordings and transcripts; observations coded across the whole experience (commonly Rose/Thorn/Bud); patterns across respondents once clustered; and — carried downstream — a prioritised backlog for the next iteration. Plus a team that has *watched* real use, which changes how they decide.

**Connection forward:** The transcript is the raw signal. Rose/Thorn/Bud codes it. Affinity Clustering finds the themes. A Bullseye or a vote prioritises them. MoSCoW turns them into a backlog. Then you iterate and test again.

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full run order and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **"Brain on speakerphone"** — the whole method in three words. Narrate everything. Some facilitators mimic it with a hand on the head, thoughts streaming out.
- **"We're not testing you" / "I didn't make this, so you can't offend me"** — the moves that unlock honesty. Borrow distance from the work even when it's yours.
- **This is a test, not a demonstration** — the hardest discipline, and it's the *facilitator's* trap. When a participant hesitates or asks a question, the instinct is to jump in and explain. Don't. Hand over the task and get out of the way.
- **Reflect questions back** — *"how do you think you should proceed?"* / *"what do you think should be here?"* Every question is data about what's unclear; answering it destroys the data.
- **Fight silence in the setup, not the moment** — set it up so well that silence rarely happens; when it does, re-enter small (*"can you tell me what you're thinking right now?"*) and withdraw again.
- **The unfinished-part move** — *"what did you expect to find here?"* turns a gap in the prototype into a finding.

**When teaching someone to facilitate think-aloud testing:** Use the experiential loop (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`, Step 4). Have the learner moderate a short test while you play the participant — and deliberately ask them a question mid-task so they have to practise *not* answering it. Then debrief on how hard it was to stay quiet and resist demonstrating. That instinct to explain is the single thing most worth rehearsing.

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about something you or your team is working on that seems simple and feels close to done. That's exactly the kind of thing you're most tempted to ship without testing. What would you learn if you watched three real people try to use it?"*

*"When you've gathered feedback before, was it opinion ('do you like it?') or behaviour (watching someone use it)? What did each one actually tell you?"*

**If the experiential loop was used:** *"How hard was it to stay quiet? What happened in you when I asked you a question and you knew you weren't supposed to answer it?"*

Close with a commitment: *"What could you put in front of a few real people this week?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The "What Could Be" team weren't overconfident. They were experienced, they believed in the method, and they still thought their simple product was probably close. They tested anyway — and watching eight people use it surfaced invisible links, a confusing "we," a sense of place that evaporated, and a dead-end ending, every one of which "probably close" would have shipped straight past.

That's the whole argument. The objection you'll hear is always *"it's simple, I think it basically works — do I really need to test it?"* And the answer is: that's exactly the moment you're most tempted to skip the test, and exactly where it still pays. You don't run a think-aloud test to confirm you're right. You run it because you always learn new material — and because the cheapest place to discover you were wrong is in front of one person, early, not in front of the market, late.
