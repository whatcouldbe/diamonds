# Critique

A structured protocol for getting balanced, constructive feedback on work-in-progress from people outside your team — or from within your team when working individually and coming together.

---

## Purpose

Use when you need evaluative feedback on something you've been working on — a concept, a design, a plan, a process, an artifact at any fidelity. Critique provides a protocol that ensures feedback is balanced (not just positive, not just negative), constructive (tied to specific dimensions), and safe (the structure gives people permission to be honest).

**The core question:** What's working, what's not, and how might we make it better?

**Why critique matters:**
- Without structure, feedback is usually bad — too vague ("what do you think?"), too defensive, or unbalanced
- People default to either rosy politeness or unconstructive criticism
- The presenter often becomes defensive, which shuts down honest input
- Good critique surfaces blind spots that the team can't see from inside the work

**What makes critique different from informal feedback:**
- A deliberate protocol that sequences the conversation
- Language designed to reduce attachment and invite honesty
- Equal attention to strengths, challenges, and suggestions
- The reviewer's job is to give feedback; the presenter's job is to collect it — not defend

**Where it sits in the process:**
Critique is used throughout the process — not phase-locked. Any time you have something in any state of readiness that could benefit from outside perspective, critique applies. The worst number of critiques is zero. Early, frequent, small-group critique is more valuable than one large review at the end.

## Setup

### Participants
- **Presenter(s):** The person or team sharing work for feedback. Also often the note-taker.
- **Note-taker:** Can be the presenter or a separate person. Captures feedback as it's given — on paper, sticky notes, or digitally.
- **Reviewers:** The people providing critique. 5–7 reviewers per session is the sweet spot — research on evaluative methods suggests this produces about 80% of what you need to know. One reviewer risks bias. More than 7, and you should be iterating on the work before showing it to the next group.

**Who should review:**
- Closer to actual users or people affected by the work = better feedback
- Further from the presenting team = more honest and less biased
- Internal critique (team members who worked individually, then present to each other) is valid and common
- People who know the challenge or are the people you're designing for are ideal
- The spectrum runs from "warm body" to "targeted user" — get as close to the latter as you can

### What You Need
- **A protocol** — the structured sequence of questions (see Steps below)
- **Something to share** — an artifact in some state: a concept, wireframe, poster, journey map, architectural layout, plan, prototype. Any fidelity works — the point is to get feedback, not to present polished work
- **A way to capture feedback** — sticky notes (color-coded by type), a shared document, or a note-taker

### Time
- 20–45 minutes for a typical session, depending on complexity and number of reviewers
- Budget more time if using spatial feedback (sticky note placement on artifacts)

## Steps

### Step 1 — Set Context
*"The challenge we were trying to solve was..."*

Explain where you are in the process and what problem or opportunity you're addressing. This grounds the reviewers in the same starting point as the team.

### Step 2 — Present the Work
*"One of the ideas we had was..."* or *"What we'd like your feedback on is..."*

Present the artifact, concept, or work-in-progress. Walk reviewers through it.

**The language matters:** "One of the ideas we had" is deliberately softening. It does double duty:
- For the **reviewer**, it signals this is a working idea, not a finished product — making it safer to give honest feedback
- For the **presenter**, it reduces attachment to the work. The Time/Love Continuum principle: the longer you spend with an idea, the more attached you get, and the less receptive you become to feedback. Framing it as "one idea" creates healthy distance.

In non-design contexts — plans, proposals, policies, strategies — *"What we'd like your feedback on is..."* carries the same softening intent without implying an idea or concept.

### Step 3 — Clarifying Questions
*"Is there anything unclear about what I just presented?"*

Give reviewers a chance to ask questions before providing feedback. This ensures everyone has shared understanding of what they're evaluating.

**Key principle:** The presenter does not need to have answers to every clarifying question. You are collecting information at this point, not defending the work. It's okay to say "I don't know" or "that's a great question — we haven't figured that out yet."

### Step 4 — What's Working (Strengths)
*"What do you like about this concept?"* or *"What is working well?"*

Reviewers share positive feedback — what resonates, what's strong, what they'd want to keep. Starting with strengths before challenges is a deliberate sequence — it establishes a constructive tone and ensures good things don't get lost in the rush to critique.

### Step 5 — Challenges
*"What are some challenges we might face?"* or *"What isn't working as well?"*

Reviewers surface concerns, risks, weaknesses, and obstacles. The framing "challenges we might face" is intentionally forward-looking and collaborative rather than accusatory.

### Step 6 — Build On It
*"How might we build on this idea?"* or *"Do you have any suggestions on how to make this better?"*

Reviewers contribute ideas for improvement. This is the generative step — it moves beyond identifying problems to proposing solutions. Everyone has suggestions; giving them a structured moment to share prevents unsolicited advice from bleeding into other steps.

### Step 7 — Close
*"Thank you for your feedback."*

Close with gratitude. Feedback is a gift. This isn't just politeness — it reinforces the behavior you want. People who feel thanked for honest feedback will give it again.

## Facilitation Notes

### The Presenter's Job Is to Collect, Not Defend
This is the single most important facilitation principle. The presenter is there to receive feedback, not to justify decisions or explain why something is the way it is. When presenters become defensive, reviewers stop being honest. Name this at the start of the session and hold the line if it happens.

### Post the Protocol Visibly
Make the four rounds visible to everyone throughout the session — a poster in the room, a slide, or a digital equivalent in your shared workspace. This reduces anxiety ("when do I get to say the negative stuff?"), helps reviewers know what's coming, and helps the facilitator manage transitions between steps. In digital sessions, a simple visible key in the Miro or FigJam board serves the same purpose as a physical poster.

### Silent Write Before Group Discussion
When working in a group, give reviewers individual time to capture their feedback before sharing aloud. This:
- Ensures all voices are heard — protects quieter participants
- Reduces anchoring bias — the first speaker doesn't set the frame
- Equalizes voice — each sticky note gets discussed regardless of who wrote it. The janitor's note gets the same airtime as the CEO's.

Even in teams with high psychological safety, individual-first is the safer default.

### Color-Code Feedback by Type
When using sticky notes, assign colors to feedback categories:
- **Pink** — strengths / what's working (connects to the "roses" in rose/thorn/bud)
- **Blue** — challenges / concerns
- **Green** — suggestions / ideas to build on

Post the color key visibly so reviewers know which color to use.

### Spatial Feedback — Placing Stickies on Artifacts
A powerful variation: instead of collecting feedback in a pile, have reviewers place their sticky notes directly on or near the specific part of the artifact they're responding to. This works especially well for:
- Architectural drawings or floor plans
- Concept posters
- Experience diagrams or customer journey maps (feedback layered onto stages)
- Screen designs or wireframes (feedback near the relevant screen)
- Any large visual artifact where location matters

The proximity of feedback to the thing it addresses adds a layer of specificity that discussion alone can't match.

### Multiple Rounds Over Time
Critique is not a one-time event. Plan for multiple rounds at multiple points in the process with small groups. After each round, react to the feedback — iterate on the work — then show it to the next group. Don't show the same version to more people; iterate between rounds. This mirrors the natural rhythm of evaluative research.

### Don't Over-Prepare the Artifact
The point of critique is to get feedback, not to present polished work. Showing rough work early invites more honest feedback and catches problems before investment deepens. The Time/Love Continuum applies: the more polished the work, the more attached the team, the harder it is to hear criticism.

### The Time/Love Continuum
A core concept that supports critique: the longer you spend with an idea, the more attached you become to it, and the less receptive you are to feedback. This is why early, frequent critique matters — it catches the work before attachment hardens. Name this concept when training teams on critique. It normalizes the emotional difficulty of hearing feedback and motivates getting it sooner rather than later.

## Variations

### Synchronous Group Critique
The standard format — presenter walks through the protocol with reviewers in the room (physical or virtual). Discussion happens in real time.

### Async / Written Critique
Send the artifact with the protocol questions. Reviewers respond in writing at their own pace. Works well for distributed teams or when the artifact is self-explanatory. The protocol questions are the same — the format of capture changes.

### Spatial / Board-Based Critique
Reviewers write feedback on color-coded sticky notes and physically place them on the artifact. Can be done individually (silent write, then place) or after group discussion. Powerful for visual artifacts and journey maps.

### Peer Critique (Internal Team)
Team members who worked individually come together and present to each other. Uses the same protocol but with peers rather than external reviewers. Common in studio/design environments. Good for early-stage work that isn't ready for external feedback.

### Simulated Critique
An AI agent or role-player inhabits a described persona (user, stakeholder, domain expert) and provides feedback through the protocol. Produces real value for hypothesis-testing and identifying blind spots, but the agent must be transparent about what synthetic feedback can and cannot do — it surfaces likely reactions and patterns but is not a substitute for feedback from real people.

### In-Person vs. Digital
Both work. Physical sessions allow spatial feedback on wall-mounted artifacts. Digital sessions (Miro, FigJam, Mural) allow async sticky note placement and broader participation. Digital also produces a built-in record of the feedback.

## Outputs

- **Clarifying questions** — identified gaps in understanding or communication, some answered, some flagged for future work
- **Strengths identified** — what's working, what to preserve, what resonates
- **Challenges surfaced** — risks, weaknesses, obstacles, blind spots the team couldn't see
- **Suggestions for improvement** — concrete ideas for how to make the work better
- **Unstructured notes** — the raw capture from the session, organized by feedback type (clarity, strengths, challenges, ideas)
- **Organizational alignment** — people who participate in critique become invested in and aligned with the work, even if they're outside the team
- The notes from critique are raw material for synthesis — they can feed into affinity clustering, importance/difficulty prioritization, or direct iteration on the work

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
Critique lives in **Looking / Evaluative Research** but is used throughout both diamonds. Any time you have an artifact — from early sketches to refined prototypes — critique applies. It's most commonly used during the Develop and Deliver phases, but early use in Discover (critiquing research plans, interview guides) is equally valid.

### Upstream Methods (critique receives from)
- Any method that produces an artifact: concept ideation, prototyping, journey mapping, experience diagramming
- Research planning — critique the research plan before executing it
- Strategy work — critique the strategy before committing to it

### Downstream Methods (critique feeds into)
- **Affinity Clustering** — cluster the feedback to identify themes
- **Importance/Difficulty Matrix** — prioritize which feedback to act on
- Direct iteration on the artifact
- Business case development (when feedback identifies critical gaps)

### Related Methods
- **Rose/Thorn/Bud** — similar positive/negative/opportunity structure; critique adds a protocol and clarifying step
- **Think-Aloud / Usability Testing** — also evaluative, but observes behavior rather than soliciting structured opinion; less simulatable, more behavioral
- **Interviewing** — critique is closer to interviewing than to usability testing in that it follows a protocol and collects perspectives through structured conversation
