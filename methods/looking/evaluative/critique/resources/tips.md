# Critique — Tips & Advice

Collected practitioner wisdom for running Critique well. These are the things that trip people up, the things that make the difference between a productive session and a frustrating one.

---

## Before the Session

**The worst number of critiques is zero.**
Getting feedback from no one isn't a strategy — it's avoidance. Even one rough, informal critique session early is better than a polished presentation to a large group late. The moment you have something to show, you have something to critique.

**Don't over-prepare the artifact.**
The point of critique is to get feedback, not to present polished work. Rough work shown early invites more honest feedback and catches problems before investment deepens. A polished artifact signals to reviewers that decisions are already made, which makes honest critique harder to give and receive.

**Get outside your team as early as you can.**
Internal critique is valid and useful. But the further you get from people who worked on the thing, the more honest the feedback. Internal reviewers carry shared assumptions that are invisible to them — outsiders will surface those immediately.

**Know the Time/Love Continuum.**
The longer you spend with an idea, the more attached you get to it — and the less receptive you become to feedback. This is why early critique matters. Name this concept when preparing your team. It normalizes the emotional difficulty of hearing criticism and motivates showing work sooner.

---

## During the Session

**The presenter's job is to collect, not defend.**
This is the single most important principle. When presenters become defensive — explaining why something is the way it is, justifying decisions — reviewers stop being honest. Name this at the start and hold the line if it happens. A presenter who says "great point, thank you" to every piece of feedback (even the uncomfortable ones) creates a much better session than one who debates every observation.

**You don't need answers to every clarifying question.**
Step 3 (clarifying questions) is not a test of the presenter's knowledge. It's a chance for reviewers to establish shared understanding. When you don't know the answer, say so: "That's a great question — we haven't figured that out yet." Unanswered questions are often the most valuable output.

**Post the protocol visibly.**
In a physical space, post the protocol poster so everyone can see what's coming. This reduces reviewer anxiety ("when do I get to talk about the problems?") and helps the facilitator hold the structure.

**Silent write before group discussion.**
In a group setting, give reviewers individual time to capture their thoughts before discussing aloud. This protects quieter participants, reduces anchoring bias (the first speaker doesn't set the frame), and equalizes voice — each sticky note gets the same airtime regardless of who wrote it. The janitor's note and the CEO's note deserve equal attention.

**Color-code feedback by type.**
When using sticky notes, assign colors to the three feedback types:
- **Pink** — what's working / strengths
- **Blue** — challenges / concerns
- **Green** — suggestions / ideas to build on

Post the key visibly so reviewers don't have to remember. This makes patterns visible at a glance and keeps the session organized.

**Manage the sequence deliberately.**
Don't let reviewers skip ahead to challenges before you've finished strengths. The sequence is deliberate — it builds a constructive tone and ensures good things aren't lost in the rush to critique. Hold each step long enough to get real feedback before moving on.

## After the Session

**Iterate before your next round.**
Critique is not a one-time event. After each round, react to the feedback — make changes to the work — then bring it back. Don't show the same version to the next group. The rhythm is: critique → iterate → critique → iterate. Each round should show progress.

**The notes are raw material, not a to-do list.**
Critique produces unstructured feedback. The next step is synthesis — affinity clustering to find themes, importance/difficulty matrix to prioritize which feedback to act on. Not every piece of feedback requires a change, but every piece deserves consideration.

**Thank reviewers genuinely.**
Feedback is a gift. People who feel thanked for honest, critical feedback will give it again. People who feel their feedback was ignored or dismissed won't. Closing with real gratitude is not a formality — it's an investment in future feedback.
