# Critique — FAQ

Questions that come up repeatedly when teams learn or run Critique for the first time. This file grows over time as new questions surface in practice.

---

**Does "one of the ideas we had" do double duty?**
Yes, intentionally. The phrasing softens both sides of the room. For reviewers, it signals this is a working idea — not a finished product — making it safer to be honest. For the presenter, it creates distance from the work and reduces attachment. The more attached you are to something, the harder it is to hear criticism. The language "one of the ideas" helps the presenter hold the work more loosely. See the Time/Love Continuum in `visuals/`.

**Can the presenter and note-taker be the same person?**
Yes. Think of presenter and note-taker as roles, not people. One person can do both, though it's harder. If the presenter is also capturing feedback, it's worth slowing the pace slightly and asking reviewers to hold one thought at a time. If there are two people from the presenting team, one presenting and one capturing is the cleaner setup.

**How many reviewers should we have?**
Five to seven is the sweet spot. Research on evaluative methods suggests this produces approximately 80% of what you need to know — the point of saturation where additional reviewers start repeating feedback you've already heard. One reviewer risks high bias. Zero reviewers produces no data. More than seven without iterating first means you're likely getting diminishing returns.

**Can critique be done asynchronously?**
Yes. Share the artifact with the protocol questions and ask reviewers to respond in writing at their own pace. The questions are the same — the format of capture changes. Async works well for distributed teams or when the artifact is complex enough to require time to review. Digital boards (Miro, FigJam) allow reviewers to drop sticky notes asynchronously, which produces a visual record similar to an in-person spatial critique.

**Who should be a reviewer?**
The spectrum runs from "warm body" to "targeted user" — closer to the actual person you're designing for produces better feedback. The further from your team, the more honest and less biased. Internal critique (team members working individually, then presenting to each other) is valid, especially early. Over time, move toward people who know the challenge or are the people you're designing for.

**Can you run critique within your own team?**
Yes, especially in contexts where critique is embedded in ways of working. Studio environments, design teams, and research groups often run internal critique regularly — individuals work separately, then present their work to each other for feedback. This is most effective when psychological safety is high and people are accustomed to the protocol.

**What if the presenter gets defensive?**
Name it early, before the session starts: "Your job is to collect feedback, not to defend the work. If you find yourself wanting to explain why something is the way it is, that's a signal to listen harder." If defensiveness happens during the session, redirect: "Let's just note that and keep moving." Don't embarrass the presenter — just hold the structure.

**What happens to the feedback after the session?**
The notes from critique are raw material, not a finished plan. The most useful next steps are: (1) affinity cluster the feedback to find themes across responses, (2) use the importance/difficulty matrix to prioritize which feedback to act on, and (3) iterate on the work before the next round of critique.

**Do you have to run all seven steps every time?**
The full protocol is the standard. But in a quick, informal setting — especially when critique is already embedded in a team's culture — you can run an abbreviated version. The minimum useful version includes: presenting the work, asking what's working, asking what's not, and thanking people for their input. The clarifying questions step (step 3) and the build-on-it step (step 6) are the ones most commonly dropped in informal sessions, and they're also the most often missed.
