# Critique — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever shown something you've been working on to someone and asked 'what do you think?' — and gotten back something completely unhelpful? Either they said 'looks good' and you learned nothing, or they tore it apart and you felt defensive, or they gave you feedback on things you didn't ask about? Everyone's a critic, but almost no one knows how to give — or receive — feedback well."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

Critique has roots in studio culture — art school, architecture, photography, design programs. In a studio crit, you present your work and receive structured feedback from instructors and peers. It's built into the way those disciplines work.

Justin uses critique throughout the HCD process — any time there's something in any state that could benefit from outside perspective. A real example: when WCB was moving offices, the person managing the build-out came in and presented several architectural layout options. The team — the people who would actually work in the space — provided critique using the protocol. The reviewers were the users.

The key insight is the Time/Love Continuum: the longer you spend with an idea, the more attached you get to it, and the less receptive you become to feedback. This is why the protocol matters — it creates structure that makes it safe for reviewers to be honest and helps the presenter stay in collection mode rather than defense mode. Critique is how you counteract the natural tendency to fall in love with your own work.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** Something to share (an artifact at any fidelity), a protocol (the structured question sequence), 5–7 reviewers who know the challenge or are the people you're designing for, and a way to capture feedback (sticky notes, shared doc, note-taker).

**Outputs:** Clarifying questions surfaced, strengths identified, challenges flagged, suggestions for improvement, and increased organizational alignment from people who now understand and are invested in the work. The raw feedback is material for synthesis — it can feed into affinity clustering or prioritization.

**Connection forward:** Critique produces the raw signal. Affinity clustering finds the themes. Importance/difficulty helps you prioritize which feedback to act on first. Then you iterate and critique again. It's a feedback loop, not a one-time event.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full 7-step protocol and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **The protocol is the method** — without the structured sequence (context → present → clarity → strengths → challenges → suggestions → close), feedback reverts to the usual unstructured mess
- **"One of the ideas we had"** — this language does double duty: softens it for reviewers to be honest AND reduces the presenter's attachment
- **Collect, don't defend** — the presenter's job during feedback is to listen and capture. Not to justify, explain, or argue. This is the hardest thing to do and the most important thing to practice.
- **Silent write before sharing** — when in a group, give individual time first. This prevents anchoring and ensures every voice is represented. Each sticky gets discussed regardless of who wrote it.
- **Color-coded stickies** — pink (strengths), blue (challenges), green (suggestions). Post the key visibly.

**When teaching someone to facilitate critique:** Use the experiential loop (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`, Step 4). Run a critique with the learner as presenter — have them present real work and receive feedback through the protocol. Then swap: have them facilitate a critique where you present something. Debrief on how it felt to receive feedback through the structure vs. informally. Then debrief on how it felt to facilitate — what was hard about holding the protocol when discussion wanted to go sideways.

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about the last time you got feedback on something you'd been working on. How did it go? Was it structured? Did you learn something useful? Or did you leave feeling either falsely reassured or unnecessarily deflated?"*

*"Where could you use critique in the next two weeks? Think about something you or your team is working on that would benefit from outside perspective — especially if you've been working on it long enough that the Time/Love Continuum might be kicking in."*

**If the experiential loop was used:** *"What was it like to receive feedback through the protocol versus how you normally get feedback? What shifted when you had the structure? What was hard about not defending your work?"*

Close with a commitment: *"Is there something you could run a critique on this week?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The protocol looks simple — seven questions on a poster. But it solves a problem that derails teams constantly: feedback that's unstructured, unbalanced, or defensive. The structure gives people permission to be honest about what's not working, while also making sure they name what is. And "one of the ideas we had" — five words — changes the entire dynamic of a presentation by signaling that this is a working idea that belongs to the conversation, not a finished product that belongs to the presenter.

The hardest part isn't the protocol. It's the discipline of collecting feedback without defending. That's the skill. And the more you practice it, the earlier you'll seek feedback — because you'll stop being afraid of what you'll hear.
