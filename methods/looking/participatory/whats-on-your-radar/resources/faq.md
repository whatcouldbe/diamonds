# What's on Your Radar — FAQ

**What's the difference between the individual radar and the group radar?**
The individual radar represents one person's genuine priorities — what matters most to them, expressed without group influence. The group radar ("What's on Our Radar") is a negotiated artifact: the result of a group deciding together which single item sits at the center of each segment. The individual radar is data; the group radar is alignment.

**Why can only one item sit at the center?**
Because the center represents what is most important — and if everything is equally important, nothing is. The constraint forces a genuine choice. It's uncomfortable by design. The discomfort surfaces real priorities and real differences that a more permissive structure would paper over.

**Can we skip the individual step if we're short on time?**
No. The individual step is the mechanism that makes the method work. Without it, the group phase is just a discussion with a radar drawn on it. If time is short, reduce the number of categories rather than skipping the individual phase.

**How many people can participate in a group radar?**
3–5 is the practical range. Below 3, there isn't enough diversity of perspective. Above 5, negotiation becomes unwieldy and quieter participants get pushed out. For larger groups, run multiple parallel group radars and compare the outputs.

**What if two items from different people feel equally important?**
The group chooses one. The facilitator's job is to hold the constraint, not resolve the tension. Ask: *"If one of these didn't happen, which would make the other harder?"* or *"Which one, if it were missing, would you notice first?"*

**Can the categories be anything?**
Yes. The four defaults (Vision, Impact, People, Challenges) are well-tested for project kick-offs, but the method works with any 2–6 categories. Trigger questions should be open-ended and in plain language.

**What do the coloured tabs mean?**
They serve two purposes — both are valid, and sometimes both are used simultaneously:
1. **Category attribution:** A color per category, so items carry their segment of origin when moved during the group phase.
2. **Authorship:** A color per person, so items on the group radar can be traced back to who generated them.

**Can we use this method digitally?**
Yes. Digital tools like Miro and FigJam support the radar structure well. The individual-before-group principle still applies — use separate frames or pages for individual radars and a shared frame for the group radar. Prevent participants from seeing each other's individual radars before the comparison step.

**How is this different from dot voting?**
Dot voting asks people to distribute preference across a set of options that have already been surfaced, usually by the group. What's on Your Radar asks people to surface their own options individually before any group input, then prioritizes through structured choice rather than a vote. The result is individual perspective preserved within a collective artifact — not a majority view.

**Does an item's ring placement carry over from the individual radar to the group radar?**
No. An item's placement on the group radar is determined by the group's negotiation — not by where it sat on the individual's radar. An item that was a center ring item for one person may land in the middle or outer ring of the group radar. Ring placement on the group radar is determined after negotiation is complete.

**What do we do with the divergence?**
Divergence — segments where people couldn't agree on the center, or where the most important items varied widely across individual radars — is the most interesting output of the method. Use it to identify where deeper research is needed, where assumptions differ, or where alignment work needs to happen before moving forward.

**Should the rings be labeled?**
In live facilitation: no. The rings are intentionally unlabeled — importance is expressed through placement, and the absence of labels keeps the cognitive task clean. When teaching the method for the first time, labeling the rings Most/Some/Least on the artifact is appropriate scaffolding.

A common substitution to avoid: labeling the rings Must/Should/Could/Won't (MoSCoW prioritization). This changes the cognitive task from an importance judgment ("what matters most to me?") to a necessity or feasibility judgment ("what has to happen vs. what won't?"). The two questions produce different thinking and different outputs. If labels are needed, use Most/Some/Least — never MoSCoW.
