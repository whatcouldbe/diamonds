# What's on Your Radar — Tips

## The individual step is non-negotiable
The most common facilitation mistake is allowing group conversation before individuals have generated and prioritized on their own. Even a brief discussion before the generate step biases what people write. Hold the line: no talking until everyone has placed their items on their personal radar.

## Generate before you show the radar
Reveal the radar template only after participants have finished generating their items. If they see the radar first, they generate with placement in mind — narrowing what they express to what feels placeable. Let the thinking happen before the structure is introduced.

## Two items is the floor for the generate step
If someone generates only one item for a category, they have nothing to compare and therefore nothing to prioritize. Prompt them to generate a second: *"If there were a second thing — even a less obvious one — what might it be?"*

## The constraint is the point — enforce it
When groups resist choosing a single item for the center ("they're both equally important"), the facilitation move is to hold the constraint, not relax it. Say: *"I know both feel important — that's exactly why we're making the choice. Which one, if it didn't happen, would make everything else harder?"* The discomfort of choosing is where the real conversation happens.

## Coloured tabs prevent chaos in the group phase
When items are moved from individual to group radars, they can lose their segment context. A coloured tab or dot per category ensures each item retains its origin. Set this up during the prioritize step — not after items are already scattered across the group radar.

## Consider marking authorship when the output has a life beyond the room
If the group radar will be shared with people who weren't in the session, or if you want to trace priorities back to individuals later, mark each item with the author's name or color. This is optional during the session itself but often worth the overhead when the artifact will be analyzed or presented.

## The group negotiation should go segment by segment
Don't ask everyone to share everything at once. Work through one segment at a time: share the Must from that segment, negotiate, place on the group radar, then move to the next. This keeps the conversation focused and prevents participants from jumping ahead to items they feel strongly about.

## Divergence is data, not a problem
When participants can't agree on what belongs at the center of a segment, that disagreement is valuable information. Name it explicitly: *"It's interesting that we're not aligned here — that tells us something about this category that we should probably explore further."* Don't rush to resolution just to complete the artifact.

## Apply MoSCoW labels to the group radar after negotiation
The ring placement on the group radar is determined by the group's negotiation — not carried over from individual radars. An item that was a Must for one person may land in the Should ring of the group radar. Apply labels after each segment is resolved.

## The radar is not the output — the conversation is
Participants and stakeholders sometimes fixate on the artifact. Remind them that the group radar is a record of a conversation, not a strategy document. Its value is in what it took to produce it: the choices made, the assumptions surfaced, the divergence revealed.
