# What's on Your Radar — Teaching

---

## Hook

Ask the group: *"When was the last time you were in a meeting where someone asked 'what matters most to us?' — and the first person who spoke ended up defining the answer for everyone?"*

Let that land. Then: *"What's on Your Radar is designed to prevent that from happening."*

---

## The Problem It Solves

Group discussions about priorities are almost always shaped by who speaks first, who speaks loudest, and what feels socially safe to say. The result isn't a collective view — it's a dominant view with compliance around it.

This method separates two things that usually happen simultaneously: *thinking* and *discussing*. By asking people to generate and prioritize individually before any group conversation happens, you get genuine individual perspectives — not social consensus dressed up as collective agreement.

---

## Key Insight

The constraint is the method.

Most priority-setting exercises ask people to rate or rank things. What's on Your Radar does something different: it forces a choice. Only one item can sit at the center of each segment. When two people each believe their item belongs at the center, and only one can go there, the conversation that follows is where the real priorities emerge.

You can't find that in a spreadsheet or a survey. You have to put people in the room — or the digital equivalent — and make them choose.

---

## The Structure

A radar with two dimensions:
- **Segments (wedges):** One per category. The number of categories determines the shape of the radar. Default is four; range is two to six.
- **Rings:** Must (center) → Should → Could → Won't (outer edge). Based on MoSCoW prioritization.

**Default four categories for project kick-offs:**

| Category | Trigger Question |
|---|---|
| Vision | What would the future look like if this initiative succeeds? |
| Impact | How would success be measured for this initiative? |
| People | Who is this for? |
| Challenges | What problems need solving? |

The categories and trigger questions can be customized to fit the context. What doesn't change: the segment/ring structure, the MoSCoW framework, the individual-before-group sequence.

---

## The Process

Three phases:

**1. Generate individually**
Respond to each trigger question with sticky-note–style phrases. Two to four per category. No prioritizing yet — just get thinking on paper before the radar is revealed.

**2. Prioritize individually**
Place items on your personal radar. One item at the center per segment. The MoSCoW rings provide the vocabulary: Must, Should, Could, Won't.

**3. Compare and build together** (workshop only)
Share your most important item per segment — not everything, just the one at the center. The group negotiates which single item goes at the center of the collective radar. The MoSCoW labels are applied to the group radar based on final placement, not carried over from individual radars.

The individual phase is non-negotiable. Skip it and you've converted the method into a group discussion with a visual prop.

---

## What It Produces

Two distinct artifacts:

- **Individual radars** — the primary data. In a research context, these are what you analyze. In a workshop, they're the starting point for group conversation.
- **Group radar ("What's on Our Radar")** — a collaboratively negotiated artifact. Not an average, not a vote. The result of people being required to choose.

And a third output that doesn't appear on any artifact: **the conversation itself**. The discussion that happens when three people each believe their item belongs at the center — and only one can go there — surfaces assumptions and genuine priorities that the radar alone cannot capture.

---

## When to Use It

- Opening a new project or initiative — to surface what different people believe matters before any definition work begins
- Starting a workshop — to establish individual context before moving into synthesis or design
- Research — to gather individual perspectives at scale and analyze for patterns
- Any moment when you suspect the group has assumed alignment that may not actually exist

---

## Common Misconceptions

**"It's just a prioritization exercise."**
It's a participation structure. The radar is the artifact — the *conversation* it generates is the output.

**"We can skip the individual step if we're short on time."**
No. The individual step is the mechanism. Without it, you have a group discussion with a radar drawn on it.

**"Everyone agrees, so we don't need to negotiate."**
Surface agreement is not the same as genuine alignment. The method is designed to test whether the agreement is real. If everyone genuinely has the same Must item, that itself is valuable information — and rare.

**"The coloured tabs are just for aesthetics."**
They serve two functional purposes: category attribution (so items don't lose their origin when moved) and optionally authorship attribution (so the group radar preserves who generated what). Both matter.

## Supplies

- Radar template — printed or digital (Miro, FigJam, or equivalent)
- Sticky notes and markers (physical) or digital sticky equivalents
- Coloured tabs or dots — for category attribution; optionally for authorship
- The directive — written and visible throughout
- Timer
