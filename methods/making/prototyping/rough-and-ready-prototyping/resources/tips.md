# Rough & Ready Prototyping — Tips

---

## Facilitation Tips

**Frame the roughness before people start building.**
Teams — especially those with design or engineering backgrounds — feel embarrassed about rough work. Say explicitly: "You are not building something to be proud of. You are building something to learn from. Rough is the goal." If you don't name it, they'll polish. The Time/Love Continuum kicks in faster than you expect.

**Start from the assumption, not the interface.**
The most common mistake: groups start designing screens before articulating what they're testing. Hold them on the planning step. Don't release them to build until they can complete this sentence: *"We want to find out whether ______."* Everything else follows from that.

**Give groups a scenario list, not a blank.**
In a time-limited workshop, open-ended scenario generation burns 15–20 minutes. Prepare 4–6 realistic scenarios in advance and let groups choose. If they want to adapt one, that's fine — but don't start from nothing.

**Set a hard time box and hold it.**
The constraint is what keeps the prototype rough. If groups know they have 90 minutes, they'll use 90 minutes. If they know they have 30, they'll find the essential screens. Name the time box, post it visibly, and call it when time is up. "Good enough to test" is always achievable in 30–40 minutes.

**Watch for over-polishing mid-build.**
Look for groups spending time on visual refinement — fonts, colors, spacing. Interrupt gently: *"Will someone be able to react to this if it's rougher? Then leave it rougher."* Send them back to content and structure.

**Divide and conquer works — but needs a brief.**
Before groups split up to build individual screens, they need to agree on: terminology, the overall flow, and which screen each person is responsible for. Five minutes of alignment before splitting up saves 20 minutes of inconsistency fixes after.

**Mark the gaps.**
Remind groups repeatedly: anything that isn't built needs a label. *"This section not available yet." "Coming soon."* Unmarked dead ends confuse testers and derail the test session. Check for them in the consistency pass before testing.

**Realistic content is not optional.**
Placeholder text — "Lorem ipsum," "Product Name," "Description here" — makes testers focus on the placeholder rather than the concept. Use real words, real (or realistic) prices, real images. Google image search + "icon" finds usable visual shorthand for almost anything. Unsplash has free realistic photography. The content doing real work in the prototype is what makes the test real.

## Common Pitfalls

**Prototyping the solution, not testing the assumption.**
The prototype should exist to answer a specific question. If you can't name the question, you're building, not learning. Ask: *"If a tester tells you X, what will you do differently? If they tell you Y?"* If the answers are the same, the prototype isn't testing anything.

**Testing usability before testing desirability.**
A usability test asks: can people use this? A desirability test asks: do people want this? Most early-stage prototypes should be testing desirability first. Don't invest in making something navigable until you've validated that people want the thing at all.

**Skipping the scenario and task definition.**
Without a scenario, testers have no context for what they're doing. Without tasks, the test has no structure. Both are required before any testing can happen. Build them before you build the screens.

**Showing polished work to get honest feedback.**
The more polished the prototype, the less honest the feedback — reviewers assume effort has been invested and soften their response accordingly. This is the Time/Love Continuum operating on the tester, not just the builder. If you want honest feedback, show rough work. Rough is the correct signal.

**Not pairing the prototype with a test.**
A prototype that never gets tested is a prop. The build phase and the test phase are inseparable. Every Rough & Ready session should end with a clear plan for how and when the prototype will be put in front of real people.

**One person building the whole thing.**
In a group, building in isolation concentrates assumptions into one person's mental model. Divide and conquer exposes the different mental models across the team — a team member who builds a different screen than the one they imagined is learning something about where the concept isn't fully shared.

## Things That Work Well

**The phone form factor as a discipline.**
Building a mobile prototype on an actual phone-sized frame (even a paper one) forces ruthless prioritization. There's no room for everything. The constraint reveals what actually matters.

**The "Google + icon" trick.**
Almost any concept can be represented visually by typing the thing into Google Image Search followed by "icon." Simple, flat, readable. Faster than Unsplash for conceptual representations and good enough to make a prototype feel real.

**One Van, One Stand as the business model prototype story.**
When teaching prototype types, this example lands every time. A branded van and a pop-up tent to test distributing fresh produce into food deserts. $0 software, $0 tooling, real-world data. It shows that prototyping isn't a design tool — it's a learning tool at every scale.

**The pharmacy service prototype story.**
Cardboard signage and wayfinding elements placed in a real pharmacy to observe whether the redesign actually changed traffic patterns. The prototype was rough (cardboard and Play-Doh); the learning was real (actual people, actual behavior, actual space).
