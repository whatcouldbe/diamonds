# Reference — Houde & Hill (1997), "What Do Prototypes Prototype?"

**Full citation:**
Houde, S., and Hill, C. (1997). *What Do Prototypes Prototype?* In M. Helander, T. Landauer, and P. Prabhu (eds.), *Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction* (2nd Ed.). Elsevier Science B.V: Amsterdam.

---

## Why This Paper Matters for This Method

The method already answers the question *"what human or business question is your prototype testing?"* (desirability, usability, viability, feasibility). Houde & Hill add a complementary lens: *"what design question is your prototype investigating?"* Their model gives teams vocabulary to name the focus of a prototype before building it — which prevents the most common failure mode: a team that agrees they're testing desirability but builds a prototype that answers a completely different question.

---

## The Model

Houde & Hill define three dimensions of any design:

- **Role** — what function the artifact serves in a user's life; the way it will be useful to them. Role prototypes explore whether the idea is the right thing: *"What would this do for people?"*
- **Look and Feel** — the concrete sensory experience of using the artifact; what the user sees, hears, and touches. Look & Feel prototypes explore *"What would this be like to use?"*
- **Implementation** — the techniques and components through which the artifact performs its function; how it actually works. Implementation prototypes explore *"Can this actually be built?"*

These three form a triangle. Any prototype sits somewhere on it — usually weighted toward one or two corners, and it's rare (and expensive) to explore all three at once. Making separate prototypes for separate dimensions is almost always more efficient than trying to build one that covers everything.

**The key implication:** a rough prototype doesn't have to simulate everything. It only has to simulate what it's *actually testing.* A storyboard is a valid role prototype. A pizza box is a valid look-and-feel prototype. A working script is a valid implementation prototype.

---

## The Pizza-Box Example

One of the clearest demonstrations of the model in practice. A design team was developing a portable computer for architects who needed to gather information on building site visits. One of the first questions was: what physical form would work for this user?

Rather than designing anything, they weighted a pizza box to match the expected weight of the computer, and gave it to an architect to carry on an actual site visit. They watched how he carried it, what else he had with him, what tasks he needed to do. They saw that the rectilinear form and weight were awkward given everything else he was carrying — and immediately started considering softer, bag-like form factors instead.

The prototype cost virtually nothing to build. It answered a real design question in an afternoon. It is a look-and-feel prototype in its most stripped-down form: no screens, no software, no simulation — just the right question materialized in the cheapest possible way.

## Connection to the Method Name

The term "rough and ready" in the context of prototyping traces to:

Wong, Y.Y. (1992). *Rough and Ready Prototypes: Lessons from Graphic Design.* Human Factors in Computing Systems: Proc. CHI'92, Posters and Short-Talks, New York: ACM, pp. 83–84.

Wong is also cited in this paper in the context of using hand-drawn, rough interfaces intentionally to keep design discussion focused on overall direction rather than specific visual details — precisely the rationale behind the Time/Love Continuum.

## How to Use This in Practice

When a team is stuck on what to build, ask: *"Are you trying to answer a Role question, a Look & Feel question, or an Implementation question?"*

- If **Role**: the prototype doesn't need to look like anything. A storyboard, a video, or an acted scenario is enough.
- If **Look & Feel**: build only the sensory surface — screens, forms, textures, flows. Implementation is irrelevant at this stage.
- If **Implementation**: build the technical mechanism. The interface and role are placeholders.

This lens also helps teams avoid a common mistake: building an implementation prototype when they actually need a role prototype, because the implementation prototype "looks more legitimate" to their organization. Houde & Hill name this directly — organizations develop "prototyping cultures" that privilege certain kinds of prototypes. Knowing the model lets you argue for the right prototype for the question, not just the prototype that passes internal review.
