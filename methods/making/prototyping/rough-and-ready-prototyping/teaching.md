# Rough & Ready Prototyping — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The Hook)

*"Have you ever worked on something for weeks — maybe months — then showed it to someone for the first time and realized in the first 30 seconds that something was fundamentally wrong? Something that you would have caught immediately if you'd just sketched it out and shown it to someone much, much earlier?"*

The follow-up question for groups: *"What's a prototype? When you hear that word, what comes to mind — what examples can you give me?"*

This surfaces the wide range of prototype types before you define anything. People always know more examples than they expect. Common answers: paper sketches, cardboard models, wireframes, role-playing a service, Figma mocks. They're all right. The point is that prototypes don't require sophisticated tools, and they're not just for digital products.

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story and Key Concepts)

**The name itself is the first lesson.** *Rough* means low effort — an approximation, not a finished product. *Ready* means it has a purpose: it's built to test something, not to demonstrate polish. The method is a discipline against over-investing in an idea before you know it's worth investing in.

**The 1x/10x/100x principle.** Tom Gilb's development cost pyramid: problems caught at the concept stage cost 1x to fix. The same problem in active development costs 10x. After release: 100x. Draw the pyramid — concept at the top, build in the middle, release at the bottom. This is why we prototype. It's not about process purity; it's about managing the cost of learning.

Frank Lloyd Wright: *"You can use an eraser on the drafting table or a sledgehammer on the construction site."* Same idea, better imagery.

**The Time/Love Continuum.** The longer you spend on something, the more attached you become to it, and the less receptive you are to feedback. This runs in both directions: you become less likely to change the work, and reviewers become less likely to be honest because the effort is visible and they don't want to hurt you. This is why rough prototypes get better feedback than polished ones. The roughness is not a limitation — it's an invitation.

**Little loops of learning vs. giant leaps of faith.** A common failure mode: you write a spec, spend months building, show it to people at the very end, and find out it doesn't work. Wouldn't you have wanted to know that sooner? The alternative is small, frequent loops: build something rough, test it, learn, adjust, repeat. Each loop is cheap. The leap is expensive.

**What can you test?** Desirability (do people want this?), usability (can people use it?), viability (will people pay for it?), feasibility (can we build it?). Early in development, desirability is almost always the first gate — don't invest in making something beautiful or easy to use before you know people want it at all.

**Prototype types.** The range is wider than most people expect:
- Physical products: cardboard models, foam form factors, index card screen mockups at real scale
- Screen-based: paper prototypes, wireframes, digital tools, PowerPoint screen flows
- Service: cardboard signage in a real pharmacy to observe traffic patterns; a pop-up food stand to test desirability and feasibility of distributing fresh produce to food deserts
- Business model: one van, one branded stand — the simplest possible test of whether the real-world delivery works

The right format is whatever lets you test your critical assumption using the least effort. Fidelity should be proportional to confidence: the more validated the concept, the higher the fidelity you can justify.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A concept (typically from a concept poster or value proposition poster), a named critical assumption (what must be true for this concept to work?), a scenario, a task list, and materials appropriate to the format.

**Outputs:** A testable artifact at an appropriate fidelity, a defined scenario and task list for testing, and a decision: iterate, validate further, or eliminate.

**Connection forward:** The prototype is the input to Think-Aloud Testing (observing people use it) or Critique (collecting structured feedback on it). Those methods produce findings that feed back into the next iteration of the prototype.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full steps and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **Start from the assumption, not the interface** — the first question is always "what are we testing?" not "what should the screens look like?" Everything about the prototype follows from the assumption.
- **4–8 screens is usually enough** — for a task-based test, you need enough screens to walk through the scenario; you don't need to prototype the entire product. More than 8 screens in a workshop setting usually means the group is building more than they need.
- **Mark incomplete areas explicitly** — anything not built should be labeled. Testers shouldn't hit dead ends without understanding why.
- **Use realistic content** — placeholder text reduces the realism of the test. Testers respond to actual words, prices, and images, not to "Lorem ipsum."
- **Divide and conquer** — once the screen structure is agreed on, split the build. Reconvene for a consistency pass before testing.
- **The Time/Love Continuum is the reason** — always come back to this. The reason we build rough is not because we can't do better; it's because rough produces honest feedback.

**When teaching someone to build a screen-based prototype:** Use the four planning questions from the workshop structure — what scenario will you test? what do you want to learn? what format will you use? what tasks will you build out? — and walk through an example from a concept poster to a completed 4-screen prototype. The demonstration should be quick and visibly rough.

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about a project you're working on right now — or one from the recent past. When did you first put it in front of real people? Was there something you could have tested earlier with a rough sketch or a cardboard model?"*

*"Where in your process do you currently have a long loop — a lot of work happening before anyone outside the team sees it? What would you need to test first to make that loop shorter?"*

If the group built and tested prototypes: *"What did you learn from the test that surprised you? Would you have learned that from more time building, or only from the test?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

Here is the honest version of why this matters: most teams already believe in prototyping in the abstract. The failure isn't ignorance — it's the Time/Love Continuum in action. They get excited about the concept. They start building. The idea gets better in their minds. The prototype gets more polished. Showing it rough feels like a risk because it might not impress people. So they wait until it's impressive.

And then they find out it doesn't work.

The discipline of Rough & Ready Prototyping isn't technical. It's emotional. It's the practice of showing work before you're ready, on purpose, because you know that the longer you wait, the more it will cost you — in money, in time, and in the hardening of attachment that makes honest feedback hard to hear.

The eraser is always available. The sledgehammer is expensive. Use the eraser.

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- 10 min planning + 10 min structure + 30–40 min build
- A concept to prototype (concept poster or value proposition poster)
- Device frame templates (phone, tablet, browser)
- Wireframe component library
- Icon Finder / Unsplash / Google image search for visual assets
- Scenario list (pre-written, for workshop use)
- Connection to Think-Aloud Testing or Critique for the test phase
