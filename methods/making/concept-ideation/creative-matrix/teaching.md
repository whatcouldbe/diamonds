# Creative Matrix — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

**This arc is a conversation, not a script.** Deliver one step at a time. After each step, pause — check for resonance, invite a question, or ask the person to connect it to something real in their own work. Let their response shape what comes next.

---

## Step 1 — Context (The Hook)

*"Has anyone here been in a brainstorming session? Put your hand up."*

Most hands go up. Then: *"How did it go?"*

Let people answer. Acknowledge the positives — energy, quantity, building on ideas. Then give space for the honest ones: the session that ran long and produced the same ideas as last time. The loudest voice in the room. The quiet people who never quite found the moment to contribute. The feeling of being put on the spot.

*"We've got an alternative. It's an antidote to all of those things — structured, silent, and fast. You can fill a grid with a hundred ideas in under ten minutes, and the quietest person in the room will contribute just as much as the loudest one."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

The most common moment this method earns its place: a team that's done the hard work — research, interviews, synthesis, problem framing — and now has to make the leap into solutions. They know the problem. They know their customers. And they're about to do what teams always do: they'll go into a breakout room, talk for forty minutes, and come out with five ideas, three of which they had before the session started.

Creative Matrix changes that. Instead of talking their way to ideas, they look at a grid. The challenge sits top-left. The columns name the people — maybe five How Might We questions from Statement Starters. The rows name the enablers — technology, cultural drivers, internal levers. And at every intersection, there's a cell waiting for an idea.

The sprint starts. The room goes silent. And for eight minutes, everyone just makes. No waiting for a gap in the conversation. No ideas lost because someone more confident said something first. Just thinking, writing, sticking.

At the end of eight minutes, a team of four has typically filled a large part of a 5×5 grid. That's up to twenty cells with one or more ideas each. Some will be ordinary. Some will be wild. And a few — usually the ones at the intersections that nobody would have thought to put together out loud — will be genuinely new.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A clearly framed overarching challenge (top-left cell), column headings that relate to people (market segments, personas, How Might We statements, service touchpoints), row headings that name enablers (technology, trends, cultural drivers, policies, behaviours), a Wild Card row at the bottom, sticky notes, pens, and a timer. If the team has run Statement Starters upstream, those HMW questions are ready-made column headings.

**Outputs:** A populated grid — a large volume of ideas visible to the whole team. From that, a shortlist of 8–12 selected ideas, typically through individual nomination or dot voting, ready for prioritisation.

**Connection back:** Creative Matrix often follows Statement Starters (which provides the column headings) and feeds into Importance/Difficulty Matrix (which prioritises the shortlist) or Critique (which evaluates the most promising concepts before development).

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full steps and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasise when teaching:**

- **The grid must be populated before the sprint starts.** Debating column and row headings mid-session kills momentum. Column construction is its own step — do it before the clock starts.
- **Silence is the mechanic, not a preference.** The method only works the way it's supposed to when people work simultaneously without talking. Hold the silence.
- **Coach for sticky quality before the sprint, not during it.** Show the contrast: a sticky that says "app" vs. one that says "smart watch notification with a pill emoji reminding you it's time for your medication." The rule of thumb: if someone who wasn't in the room would understand the idea without asking, it's good enough.
- **Wild Card row is always the bottom row.** It gives people somewhere to put ideas that don't fit the named enablers. Without it, good ideas get discarded because they don't map cleanly.
- **Alternative Worlds is a mid-sprint injection, not a separate session.** If the team is generating safe ideas, naming an inspiring organisation ("For three minutes, imagine you're Patagonia — what would they generate?") creates a burst of more unusual thinking without stopping the sprint.

**When teaching someone to facilitate Creative Matrix:** Build a grid together on a real challenge before the sprint — work through column and row construction so they feel what good headings produce. Then run a short live sprint. Debrief on what the grid structure did that a blank whiteboard wouldn't have.

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about the last time your team generated ideas together. How much did the same few people speak? Who didn't get a chance?"*

*"If you'd had a populated grid and eight minutes of silence instead, how might the output have been different — not just in volume, but in whose ideas made it onto the table?"*

If the activity was run live: *"Which intersections surprised you? Which ideas came from a column-row pairing that you wouldn't have thought to combine on your own?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

Most ideation sessions are won by the people who talk most confidently and lose by everyone else.

Creative Matrix doesn't fix that with better facilitation norms or ground rules about equal participation. It fixes it by changing the structure. When the room works in silence, there's no conversation to dominate. When every intersection is a prompt, there's no blank page to freeze in front of. When everyone writes simultaneously, there's no queue to wait in.

The introverts in the room — the ones who think before they speak, who need a moment before they're ready to contribute — they don't get a moment in most brainstorms. In a Creative Matrix sprint, they have eight minutes. And in that time they typically generate as many ideas as anyone else.

That's not a side effect. That's the design.

---

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- The grid — large format, 5×5, columns and rows populated before the sprint
- Sticky notes — plenty; one idea per sticky
- Thick pens or markers — ideas need to be legible across the grid
- Timer — enforce the sprint length
- Optional: Alternative Worlds prompt list for a mid-sprint injection
- Digital: Miro, Mural, or FigJam with emoji palette available
