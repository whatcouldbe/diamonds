# Creative Matrix

A structured ideation method that generates a large volume of new and unusual ideas quickly by sparking thinking at the intersections of two distinct categories — people-related columns and enabling-solution rows — arranged in a grid. Silent, structured, and democratic: everyone contributes simultaneously, no voice dominates, and teams regularly fill a grid in under 10 minutes.

---

## Purpose

Use when a team needs to generate a large number of diverse ideas quickly, especially when previous ideation sessions have been dominated by louder voices, produced familiar ideas, or left quieter members feeling unable to contribute. Creative Matrix is a structured antidote to the common failure modes of unstructured brainstorming.

**The core question:** How might we create the best concepts to solve the customer problem?

**Why Creative Matrix works:**
- The grid structure provides a systematic prompt at every intersection — teams don't stare at a blank page waiting for inspiration. Each cell is a concrete invitation to ideate.
- Ideas are generated simultaneously and silently. No one waits for permission to contribute, no voice crowds out others, and no one feels put on the spot mid-conversation.
- The column/row pairing forces unusual combinations. An enabler row you wouldn't naturally connect to a problem statement column produces ideas that wouldn't emerge from free brainstorming.
- Quantity is the explicit goal. The pressure to fill cells overrides perfectionism — teams let go of filtering and keep generating.
- Reading other people's stickies mid-session is encouraged, not forbidden. This stimulates further ideas without requiring the verbal negotiation of traditional brainstorming.

**What makes Creative Matrix different from standard brainstorming:**
- Structure replaces open space — every intersection is a prompt, not a blank slate
- Silent working removes the social dynamics that distort group ideation
- The grid makes contribution visible and roughly equal — quiet people participate as fully as loud ones
- The method deliberately prioritises quantity; evaluation comes later, with a different method

## The Grid

### Anatomy

A Creative Matrix is a five-by-five (or similar) grid with:

- **Top-left cell — Main challenge:** The overarching challenge or How Might We statement from which all columns connect. Keeps the session anchored even as individual cells get specific.
- **Columns — People:** Each column title relates to the people dimension of the challenge. This is the human-centred spine of the method.
- **Rows — Enablers:** Each row title names a category that could enable a solution — a type of technology, a trend, a cultural driver, an organisational lever.
- **Bottom row — Wild card:** Always reserved as a catch-all for ideas that don't fit any named enabler but clearly relate to a column. No idea gets left homeless.
- **Cells — Ideas:** One idea per sticky note, placed at the intersection of the column and row that inspired it.

### Populating the Columns

Columns are always about people. Common column types:

- **Market segments** — the distinct customer groups the challenge affects
- **Personas or customer profiles** — named archetypes built from research
- **Service touchpoints** — the moments in a customer journey where the challenge shows up
- **How Might We statements** — individual problem statements, often generated upstream through Statement Starters; the overarching challenge stays top-left, and individual HMW statements become the column headings

### Populating the Rows

Rows are enablers — categories that inspire solution thinking. Common row types:

- Emerging technologies or digital media
- Cultural drivers or social trends
- Market trends
- Internal policies and procedures
- Games and competitions
- Facilities and environments
- Lifestyle and behaviour tracking

The bottom row is always Wild Card.

Pre-established enabler sets (such as Luma's) can be used as a starting point, especially with teams new to the method.

### The Most Frequently Asked Question

How do you decide what goes in the columns and rows? The core principle: **columns are always people, rows are always enablers.** Beyond that, use what you know from upstream research. If the team has completed Statement Starters, those HMW statements are strong column candidates. If the team has identified distinct customer segments or personas, those become columns. Rows should inspire — choose categories that create genuine tension with the columns, not ones so familiar they produce predictable ideas.

## Setup

### Participants
- **Facilitator:** Sets up the grid, manages time, runs the debrief
- **Team members:** Ideate simultaneously on the same grid; ideally 4–5 per grid
- **Multiple grids:** With larger groups, run parallel grids (same challenge, same structure) and share out at the end

### What You Need
- **A populated grid** — the main challenge (top-left), column and row titles agreed before the session starts
- **Sticky notes** — one idea per sticky
- **Pens or markers** — thick enough for ideas to be legible at a distance
- **Timer** — the sprint is time-boxed
- **Digital alternative:** Miro, Mural, FigJam, or similar — emojis can supplement drawings

### Time
- **Setup:** Populate column and row titles before the session
- **Introduction and instructions:** 5–10 minutes
- **Ideation sprint:** Up to 10 minutes; sometimes 8 "movie minutes" to add urgency
- **Read-through:** Approximately 2 minutes per column
- **Selection or prioritisation:** 5–10 minutes depending on method used
- **Full session:** 45–60 minutes including debrief

## Steps

### Step 1 — Frame the Challenge

Display the main challenge in the top-left cell. Read it aloud. This is the anchor — everything generated should connect back to it.

Walk the team through the column and row titles so everyone understands what each heading means before the clock starts. This is orientation, not discussion — save debate about the headings for before the session.

### Step 2 — Introduce the Method

Ask the room: *"Has anyone been in a brainstorming session? How did it go?"* Let people respond. Acknowledge the positives (quantity, energy) and then name the common failure modes — the loud voice that dominates, the quiet people who don't get a chance to contribute, the session that runs long and yields the same ideas it always does.

Name the method as an antidote: structured, silent, fast, and democratic.

### Step 3 — Explain the Mechanic

- One idea per sticky note
- Work at the intersections — find a column and a row, generate the idea that lives where they meet
- Write enough that someone who wasn't in the room could understand the idea without asking you
- If you're stuck on a cell, move to another one and come back
- Reading other people's stickies is encouraged — it sparks new ideas
- Drawings welcome; digital teams can use emojis alongside text
- Go for quantity; there are no bad ideas; wild ideas welcome — *you can tame a wild duck later, but you can't make a tame duck wild*

Useful in-the-moment prompts: *think it, write it, stick it.*

### Step 4 — Run the Ideation Sprint

Start the clock. The room works in silence — this is intentional. There is no discussion, no breakout rooms, no verbal coordination. People are generating, writing, and placing stickies.

At the midpoint, introduce an energy prompt if needed: invite people to look at other stickies and let them spark further ideas.

Optionally introduce Alternative Worlds partway through: ask the team to imagine they work for a different organisation (an innovator, an ethical brand, a company known for bold thinking) and generate a further burst of ideas as that organisation would.

### Step 5 — Read-Through

After the sprint, allow the team to read through the matrix quietly as a group — approximately 2 minutes per column. The only permitted conversation is clarification: if someone doesn't understand what an idea means, ask. Do not critique, debate, or evaluate at this stage. The goal is shared understanding of what's on the grid.

### Step 6 — Select and Prioritise

Choose an approach based on time and what comes next:

**Individual selection:** Each person nominates two ideas — guided by criteria such as boldness, connection to strategy, or ideas they'd be willing to champion. A team of four or five produces eight to ten ideas this way, which is a strong input size for Importance/Difficulty Matrix.

**Dot voting:** Each person receives three dots to place on any ideas, including their own or someone else's. A quick visual signal of collective preference.

**Affinity clustering:** Group related ideas regardless of their position in the grid, then name the overarching idea each cluster represents. Useful when ideas are numerous and many are related. Can produce 6–12 clusters to take into prioritisation.

**Roses/Eyes diagram or other visual prioritisation:** Use when the group needs a richer evaluation before committing to a shortlist.

## Facilitation Notes

### Silence Is the Method
The silent working is not a preference — it's what makes Creative Matrix work. Don't let it slip into conversation. If energy drops during the sprint, prompt with a time check or an Alternative Worlds injection, not discussion.

### The Grid Must Be Populated Before the Sprint
Column and row titles should be agreed before the ideation sprint starts. Debating headings mid-session kills momentum. If the team hasn't done upstream work that produces clear column candidates (such as Statement Starters), build the grid with the team in a separate step before the sprint.

### Coach for Specificity on Stickies
The most common problem is stickies that are too sparse — a single word like "app" or "survey" that no one can interpret later. Actively coach: *"Write enough that someone who wasn't in the room today could understand your idea."* Show examples of weak versus strong stickies before the sprint.

### Wild Ideas Are an Asset
Explicitly give permission for wild, bold, unusual ideas. The tame-duck line lands well: *"You can tame a wild duck, but you can't make a tame duck wild."* Wilder ideas during the sprint often spark the most interesting combinations; evaluation is for later.

### Alternative Worlds as a Mid-Sprint Injection
If the team is generating safe or predictable ideas, introducing Alternative Worlds partway through adds a forcing function. Name an inspiring organisation (Patagonia, Apple, SpaceX) or a bold individual and ask: *"For the next three minutes, imagine you're them — what ideas would they generate?"* This doesn't require setting up Alternative Worlds as a standalone method; it works as an in-session prompt.

### Tool Choices and Shared Visibility
The ideation surface has one non-negotiable requirement: **shared visibility** — everyone seeing what others are generating in real time. That cross-pollination is what makes Creative Matrix structurally different from individual brainstorming. Any tool that provides it can work; any tool that doesn't produces a different exercise.

**Whiteboard tools** (Miro, Mural, FigJam) are the natural fit for distributed sessions. Pre-build the grid before the sprint — columns, rows, challenge top-left — so participants arrive at a surface ready to use.

**When whiteboard tools aren't available**, shared live documents work. Google Sheets or Excel Online pre-built to the grid structure give genuine co-authoring with visible simultaneous edits. Google Slides or PowerPoint in co-authoring mode with a table-format grid are viable alternatives; use normal view (not presentation mode) and distribute the shared file link before the session. Verbal timing replaces any auto-advance mechanic.

**The auto-advance PowerPoint format** — a timed deck that cycles through challenge/enabler pairings automatically — is a different approach worth naming separately. It works when shared visibility isn't achievable and simplicity matters: participants work individually against a shared prompt displayed on screen, writing ideas on paper or their own notes. The structured cadence is preserved; the cross-pollination is not. What remains is structured individual ideation with a common challenge, which is useful but distinct from the collaborative version of the method. Use it knowingly rather than as a default.

## Variations

### Statement Starters as Columns
After a research phase, if the team has run Statement Starters and generated a set of How Might We questions, those HMW statements become the column headings. The overarching challenge still sits top-left. This is the tightest possible link from discovery to ideation — the columns are grounded in validated research.

### Pre-Assigned Enabler Sets
For teams new to the method, or in contexts where column and row construction is too much to manage before a session, use a pre-established set of enablers as row headings. Luma has published a standard set. This reduces friction in setup without changing how the sprint works.

### Multiple Grids for Larger Groups
With groups larger than five or six, run parallel grids — same challenge, same structure, different teams. Each grid generates its own pool of ideas. Share out after the sprint; similar ideas across grids signal strong themes, divergent ones signal the breadth of possibility.

### Digital Execution
Miro, Mural, and FigJam all support the method. Emojis replace drawings for visual thinking. The silence mechanic holds in digital sessions — participants work on the same board without speaking. The main facilitation adjustment is ensuring everyone can navigate to the right cell; pre-building the grid with clear frames helps.

## Outputs

- **A populated grid** — a large volume of ideas at the intersections, visible across the whole team
- **A shortlist** — selected through individual nomination, dot voting, or clustering; typically 8–12 ideas to carry forward
- **Material for prioritisation** — the shortlist feeds directly into Importance/Difficulty Matrix, Critique, or prototyping

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
Creative Matrix sits in **Making / Concept Ideation** — early in the Develop phase, when the question shifts from what the problem is to what the solution might be. It is a divergent method: the goal is to open up possibility before evaluation narrows it.

### Upstream Methods (Creative Matrix receives from)
- **Statement Starters** — HMW questions generated from research become strong column headings
- **Affinity Clustering** — research themes or insight clusters can inform both column and row choices
- **Contextual Inquiry / Interviewing** — provides the customer understanding that makes column categories meaningful
- Any method that produces a clearly framed challenge or set of problem statements

### Downstream Methods (Creative Matrix feeds into)
- **Importance/Difficulty Matrix** — evaluate the shortlisted ideas for feasibility and priority
- **Critique** — structured evaluation of the most promising concepts before committing to development
- **Value Proposition Poster** — develop a shortlisted idea into a concept ready for testing
- **Prototyping** — the strongest concepts become briefs for a first prototype

### Related Methods
- **Round Robin** — also in Concept Ideation; produces fewer, more developed ideas through a generate-critique-resolve sequence. Use Round Robin when quality and pressure-testing matter more than volume; use Creative Matrix when volume and speed matter more.
- **Alternative Worlds** — a method that can be inserted mid-Creative Matrix sprint as a creative forcing function; also a standalone ideation method with its own selection logic
- **Brainstorming** — Creative Matrix is a structured antidote to unstructured brainstorming; shares the quantity-over-quality principle but provides the grid structure and silent working mechanic that address brainstorming's common failure modes
