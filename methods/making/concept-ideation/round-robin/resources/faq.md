# Round Robin — FAQ

---

**What if the idea I inherit is really underdeveloped — can I add to it before critiquing?**

Two things help here. First, before passing, the facilitator should prompt everyone to make sure their idea is legible to someone who wasn't in their head — a quick pre-flight check. Second, an underdeveloped idea isn't necessarily a problem. Misinterpretation is generative; the next person's interpretation of an incomplete idea can produce something more interesting than a fully specified one would have. If you inherit something underdeveloped, flesh it out as part of the critique pass. You're the owner of that idea for this round — develop it enough to critique it honestly.

---

**What if someone's critique is too vague to resolve?**

Do your best with what you have. If the critique is genuinely unusable, generate your own — identify the failure modes you see in the idea — and resolve that instead. The resolve pass still happens. You're never blocked.

---

**Who presents the final concept — the person who resolved it or the person who generated it?**

Usually the person who resolved it, not the originator. The more distance between the person who generated the idea and the person who presents it, the more the idea belongs to the room rather than to an individual. Some facilitators go a step further and have a fourth person present — someone who touched neither the generate nor the resolve pass. Both approaches work. What's rare, and worth avoiding, is having the originator present their own idea. It undermines the shared ownership the method was designed to create.

---

**What comes after this — how do we decide which concept to move forward with?**

You'll need a selection method. The default is presentation followed by a vote. In smaller groups, everyone presents their resolved concept and the group votes. In larger groups, a bracketed tournament works well — pairs present to each other to narrow the field, then the strongest concepts are presented to the full room. Voting can be holistic (best overall concept) or detailed (voting on the strongest elements across concepts, then synthesizing). Importance/Difficulty Matrix is a natural downstream method for evaluating resolved concepts against feasibility and priority. Pattern and priority methods generally are the right category to reach for after Round Robin produces its output.

---

**What do we do with ideas that didn't make it through all three rounds?**

This shouldn't happen if you have at least three people — the method is designed so every idea completes all three passes. Below three people, you have a facilitation constraint, not a Round Robin problem. If an idea does end up incomplete, it simply doesn't get presented. An incomplete idea isn't a failure; the room naturally focuses on the resolved concepts.

---

**Can one person do more than one round on the same idea?**

In smaller groups it can happen, but it changes the nature of the method. Below three people, Round Robin starts to feel less like a passing mechanic and more like a critique-and-revise loop — which is useful, but different. The method's value comes from the separation between generator, critic, and resolver. Two people gets you some of that; one person gets you almost none of it. In a two-person session, the pattern is essentially: I generate, you critique, I resolve — which is closer to a straight critique than a Round Robin. With Diamonds, AI fills the missing seats so the full three-pass structure runs even in a solo session.

---

**How many ideas will we end up with?**

Exactly as many as you have participants. Everyone generates one idea, so the number of ideas always equals the number of people in the room. Twenty people produces twenty ideas — all of them touched by three people, all of them resolved. This is also why Round Robin produces a manageable output even in large groups: a room of a hundred people doesn't produce a thousand ideas, it produces a hundred — each one pressure-tested.
