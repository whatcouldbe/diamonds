# Round Robin

A structured ideation method in which ideas evolve as they are passed from person to person through three rounds: generate, critique, resolve. Every participant contributes to every idea that touches their hands — and no one leaves owning the idea they started with.

---

## Purpose

Use when a team needs to generate and pressure-test ideas while building shared ownership of the output. Round Robin is especially powerful in large groups, in situations where seniority or attachment might distort a traditional brainstorm, or when the goal is a small number of robust ideas rather than a large volume of undeveloped ones.

**The core question:** How might we generate ideas that are stronger because they've already survived critique?

**Why Round Robin works:**
- Traditional brainstorming produces quantity; Round Robin produces quality. The critique pass surfaces failure modes before anyone has invested in a direction.
- Ideas that have been through generate, critique, and resolve are structurally more robust than a flat list — the failure modes have already been named and addressed.
- Passing the idea breaks attachment. You can't fall in love with something you gave away. This makes critique easier to receive and easier to give.
- Shared ownership is built into the mechanic. By the end, no idea belongs to the person who started it — which makes the team more likely to commit to implementing what they've created together.
- In large groups, traditional brainstorming produces noise. Round Robin gives every person guaranteed contribution: one idea generated, one critiqued, one resolved.

**What makes Round Robin different from standard brainstorming:**
- It deliberately breaks two brainstorming rules — deferring judgment and going for quantity — for good reason
- The critique pass is structural, not optional; judgment is built in
- Every idea goes through the same three-round process, regardless of group size
- The paper fold or template mechanic enforces both the sequence and the anonymity

## Setup

### Participants
- **Facilitator:** Manages the rounds, timekeeping, and passing mechanics
- **Team members:** Each generates, critiques, and resolves — ideally 3 or more
- **Minimum:** 3 participants (one per round). Works well up to large groups; scale by running parallel sets of templates

### What You Need
- **A problem statement or prompt** — a How Might We question, statement starter, or framing the team is working against. Everyone should be solving for the same thing.
- **A template per participant** — paper (folded), digital (Miro/Mural), or large-format poster. The template has three sections, revealed in sequence: generate, critique, resolve.
- **Timer** — rounds are timed separately; ratio guidance below

### Time
- **Generation:** Longest round. Allow enough time to develop and sketch an idea fully.
- **Critique:** Roughly half the time of generation.
- **Resolve:** Roughly double the time of generation — this is the most cognitively demanding pass.
- **Example:** 10 min generate / 5 min critique / 10 min resolve = 25 minutes + transitions
- A full session with six participants and ten templates ran as a two-hour session (see story in `resources/`)

## Steps

### Step 1 — Frame the Prompt
Share the problem statement or prompt with the whole group. If using a paper template, ask everyone to write the prompt at the top before unfolding anything. This creates the hand-brain connection — writing it down starts the thinking and ensures everyone is solving for the same thing.

### Step 2 — Generate (Round 1)
Each person generates their best idea in response to the prompt. Encourage unusual, ambitious, or unexpected ideas — this is not the time for safe answers. The idea should be developed enough to be critiqued: give it a name, sketch it, explain the key elements.

*On paper:* Participants work in the first section of the folded template, then fold it closed before passing.
*In Miro/Mural:* Each person works on their own template frame, then rotates to the next one.
*On posters:* Each person drafts their poster, then physically moves to the next station.

### Step 3 — Why the Solution Will Fail (Round 2)
Each person inherits someone else's idea and lists all the ways it could fail. This is the critique — not a gentle feedback session, but an honest inventory of risks, weaknesses, and gaps. The goal is to surface every significant failure mode before anyone invests in resolving them. The template calls this "Why the Solution Will Fail" — that framing is intentional. Be the armchair critic.

Structured critique frameworks work well here. Rose/Thorn/Bud is one option: what's strong (Rose), what could fail (Thorn), what's promising but undeveloped (Bud).

*Important:* The person critiquing should not know whose idea they have if possible. Physical separation and the folded template help maintain this. With seniority in the room, anonymity makes critique more honest.

### Step 4 — Final Concept (Round 3)
Each person inherits an idea with its critique attached. Their job is to address the critique: fix the failures, reinforce the weaknesses, and develop the bud. The final concept should be stronger than the original — not a new idea, but an evolved version of the one they inherited.

This is the hardest round and needs the most time. The person resolving has to hold the original intent of the idea while responding to critique they didn't write about work they didn't generate. That tension is where the real development happens.

### Step 5 — Share Out
Present the resolved ideas to the group. Because the ideas have passed through multiple hands, the presenter is usually not the originator — and that's the point. The ideas belong to the room.

From here, use an evaluative method (such as Importance/Difficulty Matrix or a dot vote) to prioritize which resolved concepts to carry forward.

## Facilitation Notes

### Anonymity Is an Asset, Not a Requirement
Anonymity makes critique easier to give and receive — especially when seniority is in the room. If it's easy to maintain (folded paper, separated digital frames, physical rotation to a different table), do it. If it's not practical, don't let it block you. The mechanic still works; you just may need to be more explicit about the expectation that critique is of the idea, not the person.

### The Critique Pass Is the Method
The generate pass is setup. The resolve pass is payoff. But the critique pass is what makes Round Robin distinct. Coach participants to be honest and specific — vague critique ("this seems risky") produces vague resolution. Name the specific failure modes.

### Watch the Time Ratio
The resolve pass needs more time than most facilitators give it. If participants are rushing through resolution, they're not doing the hard work. Protect the resolve time.

### Async Works
Round Robin can run asynchronously. Assign the generate pass as pre-work, then have participants critique and resolve in separate sessions or via email/document handoff. The three-round structure holds; only the pace changes.

### It's Not Just for Ideas
Round Robin works for any artifact that benefits from group development and shared ownership: role definitions, mission statements, concept posters, job descriptions, design principles. The prompt changes; the mechanic doesn't.

## Variations

### Role Definitions / Shared Authorship
Instead of a concept idea, participants draft a definition — of a role, a term, a principle — and pass it through the same three rounds. Each person inherits a definition, critiques it, and the third person refines it. Useful for building shared vocabulary or jointly authored documents where every voice should be represented.

### Large Group (Parallel Sets)
With more participants than three, run multiple simultaneous sets of templates. Divide participants into groups of three, run the rounds in parallel, then share out across groups. With six participants and ten templates, split into two groups of three, each running five templates in two rounds.

### Station Rotation
Post large-format templates on the wall. Participants draft at their station, then physically rotate to the next station for critique, then rotate again for resolution. Good for energizing a room and creating visible, spatial work product.

### AI-Assisted Execution
When running solo or in a one-on-one session, the agent can run all three passes sequentially: generate a set of ideas, critique each one for failure modes, then resolve the critique for each. The output is more robust than a flat list because the failure modes have been surfaced and addressed. See `resources/ai-usage.md`.

## Outputs

- **Resolved concepts** — ideas that have been generated, pressure-tested, and developed by multiple people
- **A critique record** — the failure modes identified, which can inform evaluation criteria even if not fully resolved
- **Shared ownership** — no one leaves having only touched their own idea; the output belongs to the room

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
Round Robin sits in **Making / Concept Ideation** — early in the Develop phase, when a team is generating and developing solution concepts before committing to one direction. It can also be used earlier in the diamond to generate and pressure-test problem framings or opportunity statements.

### Upstream Methods (Round Robin receives from)
- **Abstraction Laddering** — provides the chosen framing or How Might We that becomes the Round Robin prompt
- **How Might We** — converts research insights into design challenges that seed the generate pass
- **What's on Your Radar** — can surface the categories or areas that each Round Robin template addresses (as in the role definitions example)
- Any method that produces a problem statement, opportunity, or prompt worth developing

### Downstream Methods (Round Robin feeds into)
- **Importance/Difficulty Matrix** — evaluate resolved concepts for feasibility and priority
- **Critique** — a more structured evaluative session on the resolved concepts before committing to one
- **Prototyping** — the resolved concept becomes the brief for a first prototype

### Related Methods
- **Brainstorming** — generates quantity; Round Robin generates quality. Use brainstorming when volume and divergence are the goal; use Round Robin when you need ideas that can survive scrutiny.
- **Critique** — the Critique method is a standalone evaluative session; Round Robin embeds a lighter critique pass inside an ideation flow
- **Rose/Thorn/Bud** — a structured feedback framework that works well as the format for the Round Robin critique pass
