# Round Robin — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

**This arc is a conversation, not a script.** Deliver one step at a time. After each step, pause — check for resonance, invite a question, or ask the person to connect it to something real in their own work. Let their response shape what comes next.

---

## Step 1 — Context (The Hook)

*"What are the rules of brainstorming you've heard?"*

Let them answer. They'll say: defer judgment, go for quantity, build on others' ideas, encourage wild ideas. Then:

*"We're going to break two of those rules — deliberately, and for good reason. We're not going to defer judgment. And we're not going for quantity. We're each going to work with a single idea. By the end of this method, that idea will have been touched by three people — and it won't belong to any of them."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

The example: using Round Robin to collaboratively draft role definitions for a distributed engineering team.

At the time, the team had one engineer in Pittsburgh and five more spread across different continents. The challenge was to define roles and responsibilities in a way that the whole team felt ownership over — not handed down, but built together.

The session started with What's on Your Radar, which surfaced 10 categories of responsibility: project and work management, supplier management, data management, and more. Each category became a mini role definition poster — a structured template with fields for the role name, category (people/process/tech), why it matters, a quick definition, what success looks like internally, what success looks like externally, and key tasks.

Six people. Ten templates. Two rounds of Round Robin — one for each row of five templates.

Round 1: Everyone drafted a role definition (10 minutes).
Round 2: Everyone rotated and critiqued using Rose/Thorn/Bud (10 minutes).
Round 3: Everyone rotated and resolved the critique (10 minutes).

Two hours later, the team had initial role definitions for all 10 areas — collaboratively authored, pressure-tested, and owned by the whole group. No one left having only touched their own work.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A problem statement or prompt (often a How Might We), one template per participant, a timer, and a structure for the critique pass (Rose/Thorn/Bud works well).

**Outputs:** Resolved concepts — ideas that have been generated, critiqued, and evolved by three different people. A critique record. And shared ownership of the output.

**Connection back:** Round Robin often follows Abstraction Laddering or How Might We, which provide the framing that becomes the prompt. The resolved concepts often feed into Importance/Difficulty Matrix for prioritization, or directly into prototyping.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full steps and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **The time ratio matters.** Generation gets the most time; critique gets roughly half; resolution gets roughly double. Most facilitators underprotect the resolve round — that's where the real development happens.
- **Pass the idea, not just the paper.** The passing mechanic is the whole method. Whatever format you use — paper, Miro frame, physical poster — the idea has to move and the person has to let it go.
- **Anonymity is an asset.** When possible, prevent people from knowing whose idea they're critiquing. The folded paper template does this naturally. Seniority in the room makes this more important, not less.
- **The critique pass is honest, not gentle.** Coach participants to name specific failure modes. "This seems risky" is not a critique. "This fails when the team is distributed and can't coordinate in real time" is.
- **It's not just for ideas.** The mechanic works for any artifact worth group development: role definitions, mission statements, concept posters, design principles. The prompt changes; the structure doesn't.

**When teaching someone to facilitate Round Robin:** Run a short live version with a real prompt. Debrief on the critique pass — was it honest? Was it specific? What happened to the idea between round one and round three?

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about a situation where your team generated a lot of ideas but couldn't agree on which one to move forward with. How might three rounds of generate, critique, resolve have changed that?"*

*"Where in your current work would shared ownership of the output actually matter — where you need the whole team committed, not just the people who came up with the idea?"*

If the activity was run live: *"What happened to the idea between the time you passed it and the time you saw the resolved version? What did three passes do that you couldn't have done alone?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

Here's what's true about most ideation sessions: the people who generated the ideas love them, the people who didn't feel less ownership, and everyone ends up having to evaluate work they're not equally invested in.

Round Robin changes that. By the time you're done, no one owns the idea they started with — and everyone has touched something they didn't create. That's not a side effect. That's the point.

The ideas are stronger because they survived critique. The team is more committed because they built it together. And the person who had to let go of their idea? They usually end up prouder of what it became than what it was.

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- Timer — enforce the round lengths
- One template per participant — paper (folded), Miro/Mural frame, or large-format poster
- A structured critique framework for round two — Rose/Thorn/Bud recommended
- The prompt written down and visible to everyone throughout
