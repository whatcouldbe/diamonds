# Worked Example — FreshMeals meal kit service

*Fresh Market Grocery · "Development team hypotheses as of April 27" · synthetic teaching sample, transcribed verbatim from the supplied artifact.*

Frame: **We believe** *[new feature]* **will** *[desired outcome]* **for** *[target persona]*. **We'll know this is true when** *[quantitative impact]*. **We believe this will ultimately help improve** *[high-level goal]*.

| # | New feature | Desired outcome | Target persona | Quantitative impact | High-level goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | introducing quick-prep meal kits | save time | busy parents | we see a 30% increase in orders from households with children | overall sales growth in a key demographic |
| 2 | providing organic meal options | expand our brand's appeal | eco-friendly and health-conscious consumers | we observe at least 20% of customers ordering organic meal kits in our pilot | our brand appeal among people we've historically struggled to reach |
| 3 | a referral program | provide new avenues for customer acquisition | markets where we already have some market saturation | referrals account for 15% of new subscriptions | our social proof and create viral feedback loops |
| 4 | introducing cooking tutorials with meal kits | provide a sense of empowerment | novice cooks | tutorial engagement rates go up by 30% | product satisfaction and stickiness |
| 5 | featuring seasonal meal kits | generate excitement and delight | regular customers | seasonal meal kits are more popular than our typical meal kit selections by 10% or more | long-term product satisfaction |

**What this example teaches:**
- Every *quantitative impact* has a number and a direction — each one could come back negative. That is the disproof condition working.
- Every *target persona* is specific enough to go find and talk to.
- The *high-level goal* is broader than the metric and always answers "so what."
- The *desired outcome* is a change ("save time," "expand appeal"), never a restatement of the feature.
