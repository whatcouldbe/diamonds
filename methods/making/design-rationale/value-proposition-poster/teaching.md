# Value Proposition Poster — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

**This arc is a conversation, not a script.** Deliver one step at a time. After each step, pause — check for resonance, invite a question, or ask the person to connect it to something real in their own work. Let their response shape what comes next.

---

## Step 1 — Context (The Hook)

*"You've had a great idea. You're excited about it. You walk into a room of people who could back it — and you have everything: the research, the slides, the detail. How do you bring it to them?"*

Let them answer. Most people reach for a deck, or "I'd talk them through it."

*"Here's the trap. You can't pitch an idea with a single sticky note — that's too little. But you also can't carry in acres of research and a forty-slide deck — that's too much. There's a page that lives in between: one page that makes the idea understandable and exciting in about three minutes. That's the Value Proposition Poster."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Before / After Story)

The story is a picture of two rooms. Paint both.

**The first room.** An enthusiastic person with a genuinely good idea is presenting. They're sharing a lot — referencing documents, going deep, talking for too long. Around the table, three or four people are trying to follow. One is asking a question to get oriented. One looks confused. One looks bored. And one — honestly — looks like they've fallen asleep. It's not that the idea is bad. It's that there's too much of it, for too long, and the room has checked out.

**The second room.** Same idea — but now it's on a single poster. The presenter pitches it in three minutes and stops. And the room changes. People lean *in*. "That's awesome." "I've got a build on that." "Have you thought about—?" And the one that every idea-author wants to hear: *"Do you want some money?"*

The difference isn't the quality of the idea. It's the format — and the discipline the format imposes. (This is the WCB evolution of Luma's Concept Poster, built precisely because busy executives in large enterprises needed the second room, not the first.)

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A developed idea worth backing — usually one that's already been generated (Round Robin, Creative Matrix) and prioritised (Importance/Difficulty, bullseye). A template. A named customer. Sticky notes. Optionally, an AI assistant as a second pair of eyes.

**Outputs:** A one-page poster pitchable in three minutes; a prioritised list of the assumptions that would make or break the idea; a set of feedback from reviewers; and — often the most useful — a clear sense of the *one element to prototype first*.

**Connection back:** the poster receives a chosen concept and hands off to Critique (feedback), Rough & Ready Prototyping (build the first element), and validation methods (test the assumptions).

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for full steps and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet. The poster has three zones, built in layers:

- **The statement** — *Our [X] helps [who] who want to [job] by [removing pain] and [creating gain].* Coach for specificity: a named segment, a measurable outcome. The legible cousin of Strategyzer's Value Proposition Canvas.
- **The storyboard** — six panels, one named customer, problem to transformation. Emphasise: words before icons, icons before images. A real character, not "the user."
- **The assumptions** — desirability, feasibility, viability. The deal-breakers get promoted onto the poster. This is the integrity of the method: it says out loud what would have to be true.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **Pull back from saying everything.** This is the whole discipline. Pitch in three minutes; trust the format to make them ask.
- **Build in layers.** Rough version of all three zones first, then improve. Never polish one zone while the others sit empty.
- **Assumptions are a feature.** Naming what you don't know is more credible than false confidence — and it's what sets up real feedback.
- **After critique, find the first thing to build.** The idea is too big to prototype whole; point at one element and make the minimal version.

**When teaching someone to facilitate this:** have them build a poster for a real idea, pitch it to the group in three minutes, then run a live Critique round on it. Debrief on what the three-minute limit forced them to cut — and whether the room leaned in.

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think of the last time you watched someone present an idea and lose the room. What was the problem — the idea, or how much of it they tried to convey at once?"*

*"Take an idea you're working on now. Could a stranger read one page and know who it's for and why it matters — without you standing next to it?"*

*"What's the most uncomfortable assumption underneath your idea — the one that, if it's wrong, the whole thing falls over? Would you put it on the poster?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

Here's the thing most people get backwards. They believe the way to win support is to *say more* — more evidence, more detail, more slides. So they fill the room until everyone drowns.

The Value Proposition Poster works on the opposite belief: the way to win support is to say the essential thing clearly, show it in a real person's hands, be honest about what you don't know — and then *stop*, and let the room come to you.

Three minutes. One page. A vision of the future, made concrete enough to back. Done well, you don't just get a polite nod — you get "I've got a build on that," and sometimes you get "do you want some money?" That's not because the idea got better. It's because, for the first time, the room could actually see it.

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- The poster template — statement frame, six-panel storyboard, assumptions area
- A named, believable customer — chosen before the storyboard starts
- Icons and simple images for the storyboard
- Sticky notes — for assumptions (the team) and feedback (reviewers, initialled)
