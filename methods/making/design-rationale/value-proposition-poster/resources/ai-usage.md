# Value Proposition Poster — AI Usage

An AI assistant is a strong second pair of eyes at every stage of the poster. It doesn't replace the team's judgement, but it catches the generic, the vague, and the unstated fast. Below are stage prompts (genericised from real workshop use) and guidance for building a poster solo.

The pattern at each stage is the same: **draft → ask the AI to question and critique → revise.** Always have the AI ask clarifying questions *before* it gives feedback — it produces sharper input when it understands the idea first.

---

## Stage 1 — Strengthen the value proposition statement

> I'm working on a value proposition for an idea I want to put forward inside my organisation.
> The format is:
> "Our [products and services] help(s) [customer segment] who want to [job to be done] by [verb] [customer pain] and [verb] [customer gain] unlike [competing value proposition]"
> Here is my draft:
> [PASTE YOUR DRAFT]
> Please help me strengthen this by:
> 1. First, asking me 3–5 clarifying questions to better understand my idea, and awaiting my answers
> 2. Then providing feedback in this structure:
>    - What's strong about this value proposition and why
>    - What's weak or missing and why
>    - Specific suggestions to improve it
> Focus especially on whether the customer segment is specific enough, whether the impact/outcome is clear and compelling, and whether the differentiation is distinctive.

---

## Stage 2 — Refine the storyboard

> I'm creating a storyboard to show how someone experiences my value proposition. I've drafted answers to six steps and need help refining them.
> Here's my value proposition:
> [PASTE YOUR VALUE PROPOSITION]
> Here are my draft answers to the six steps:
> Step 1 (Who is the character? What problem are they facing?): [YOUR DRAFT]
> Step 2 (What is needed to help them solve their problem?): [YOUR DRAFT]
> Step 3 (How do they get started?): [YOUR DRAFT]
> Step 4 (What happens next?): [YOUR DRAFT]
> Step 5 (What happens at the end?): [YOUR DRAFT]
> Step 6 (What is the result? How does that make things better?): [YOUR DRAFT]
> Please help me refine this storyboard by:
> 1. Identifying which steps are strong and why
> 2. Pointing out what's unclear, vague, or missing in each step
> 3. Suggesting specific improvements to make each step more concrete and compelling
> 4. Recommending what kind of icon or visual would represent each step well
> 5. Ensuring the story flows naturally from problem to transformation
> Keep the story human-centred and focused on the customer's experience, not the technical details of how we build the solution.

---

## Stage 2b — Turn the finished storyboard text into visuals

Once the six captions are final, this stage turns them into the storyboard itself — the panels a busy audience actually reads. It assumes the words are done: **words before icons, icons before images.** Pick the version that matches your tools and time. Either way, a generated storyboard is still a *hypothesis about experience*, not evidence — its job is to be tested with real people, not to look finished.

### Option A — Panel-by-panel art direction (tool-agnostic)

Use this when a person will illustrate the panels, or when icons are enough. It produces a visual brief per panel that works whether you draw it, hand it to an image tool, or stay at icon level.

> I have the finished text for a six-step customer storyboard and need a clear visual brief for each panel, so the six panels tell one customer's story from problem to transformation and hold together as a set.
> Value proposition: [PASTE YOUR VALUE PROPOSITION]
> Named character: [NAME + ONE LINE ON WHO THEY ARE]
> My six finished captions:
> Panel 1 (the character and the problem they face): [CAPTION]
> Panel 2 (what's needed to help them): [CAPTION]
> Panel 3 (how they get started): [CAPTION]
> Panel 4 (what happens next): [CAPTION]
> Panel 5 (what happens at the end): [CAPTION]
> Panel 6 (the result — how things are better): [CAPTION]
> First, ask me 3–5 clarifying questions so the panels stay consistent — at least: what the character looks like and their setting, and how they should visibly feel at the start versus the end.
> Then, for each of the six panels, give me:
> 1. The single moment to depict — one clear action or feeling, not a busy scene
> 2. The character's expression and body language at that moment, carrying the emotional arc (the problem should read as a problem in Panel 1; the transformation should land in Panel 6)
> 3. A simple icon option and a richer illustration option, so I can choose by how much time I have
> 4. What to leave out — anything that shows the engineering rather than the customer's experience
> Flag any caption that can't be told in one clear image — that usually means the step is doing too much and the text needs splitting before we draw it.

### Option B — Generate the panels directly (image generation)

Use this when the assistant can generate images and you want the storyboard produced now.

> I have the finished text for a six-step customer storyboard and want to turn it into the actual visual storyboard — six panels, read left to right, telling one customer's story from problem to transformation.
> Value proposition: [PASTE YOUR VALUE PROPOSITION]
> Named character: [NAME + ONE LINE ON WHO THEY ARE]
> My six finished captions:
> Panel 1 (the character and the problem they face): [CAPTION]
> Panel 2 (what's needed to help them): [CAPTION]
> Panel 3 (how they get started): [CAPTION]
> Panel 4 (what happens next): [CAPTION]
> Panel 5 (what happens at the end): [CAPTION]
> Panel 6 (the result — how things are better): [CAPTION]
> Before you generate anything, ask me 3–5 clarifying questions so the panels stay consistent and believable — covering at least: what the character looks like and their setting (so they're recognisably the same person in every panel); the visual style I want (simple line illustration, flat colour, sketch, etc.); and the emotional arc — how the character should visibly feel at the start versus the end.
> Then produce the storyboard as six panels, in order, and for each panel:
> 1. Show one clear moment or action — not a busy scene. The single most important thing the character is doing or feeling at that step.
> 2. Keep the same character, style, and world across all six panels.
> 3. Make the experience visible — what the customer does and feels — not the engineering of how the solution works.
> 4. Make the emotional shift legible: the problem should look like a problem in Panel 1, and the transformation should land in Panel 6.
> 5. Place the one-line caption with its panel; let the picture carry the moment and the words carry the meaning.
> Keep it simple enough to read in about three minutes with no one standing beside it to explain. If any caption can't be told in a single clear image, tell me — that usually means the step is doing too much and the text needs splitting before we draw it.

## Stage 3 — Surface key questions and assumptions

> I'm identifying critical questions and assumptions for my value proposition.
> Here's my value proposition and how it works:
> VALUE PROPOSITION: [PASTE YOUR VALUE PROPOSITION]
> STORYBOARD (6 steps): [PASTE YOUR SIX STEPS]
> Please help me identify critical questions and assumptions by analysing three dimensions:
> 1. DESIRABILITY: What assumptions are we making about whether customers actually want this? What questions remain about real customer pain/gain? Will they change their behaviour?
> 2. FEASIBILITY: What assumptions are we making about our technical ability to build this? What questions remain about available technology, required capabilities, or expertise?
> 3. VIABILITY: What assumptions are we making about business value? What questions remain about costs, investment justification, timeline, or organisational impact?
> For each dimension, provide:
> - 3–5 critical questions or assumptions
> - Why each one matters (what breaks if it's wrong?)
> - How we might test or validate it
> Prioritise assumptions that, if proven false, would invalidate the entire concept.

## When AI builds the poster solo or one-on-one

When working with a single author (or autonomously), the agent runs the passes a workshop would distribute across a team:

1. **Statement** — draft from what the author tells you, then run the Stage 1 critique on your own draft and revise. Push for a specific segment and a measurable outcome.
2. **Storyboard** — generate the six-step story for one named, believable customer, then run the Stage 2 refinement. Keep it experiential, not technical. Once the text is final, use Stage 2b to turn it into the visual storyboard (or a panel-by-panel art-direction brief if images aren't the right call yet).
3. **Assumptions** — run the Stage 3 analysis across all three dimensions, then prioritise to five to seven deal-breakers and pair each with a test.
4. **Choose the first prototype** — point at one storyboard panel or one promoted assumption and name the minimal element to build and test first. This is the handoff to Rough & Ready Prototyping.

**Transparency:** an AI-built poster is a strong draft, not a validated artifact. The statement is a claim, the storyboard is a hypothesis about experience, and the assumptions are explicitly unverified. Say so — the poster's job is to be tested with real people, not to stand in for that testing.
