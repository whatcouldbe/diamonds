# Value Proposition Poster — Tips & Gotchas

## On the statement

- **Be specific, not generic.** "Oncologists treating rare cancers," not "healthcare providers." The segment is where most statements go soft.
- **Make the impact measurable.** "Reduce waiting time from 30 minutes to 2," not "save time." A number is what makes a gain believable.
- **Connect to a real pain.** If you can't name the genuine frustration the customer feels today, the proposition is built on a guess.
- **Sharpen differentiation.** If you use the *unlike* clause, name what's actually different — not "unlike traditional approaches."
- **Don't polish forever.** The statement is the field teams most love to perfect. Get it specific, then move on — an unfinished storyboard costs you more than an imperfect sentence.

## On the storyboard

- **A real, named character.** "The user" is not a character. Choose someone the value proposition clearly helps, give them a name, and make their problem one people care about.
- **Words before icons; icons before images.** Finish the story text first. Add icons second. Only upgrade to images if there's time and it improves understanding.
- **Show the experience, not the engineering.** The story is about what the customer feels and does, not how the thing is built.
- **Read it out loud.** The fastest way to find a weak panel is to narrate the six steps aloud. If it only makes sense when you explain it, it isn't finished.

## On assumptions

- **Surface the uncomfortable ones.** The deal-breakers are the point. "Obviously people want this" is the assumption most likely to sink you — write it down and plan to test it.
- **Check all three dimensions.** Technical feasibility isn't enough. Desirability and viability matter equally — a flaw in any one kills the concept.
- **Make them testable.** Convert a vague worry into a specific question, and pair it with the cheapest, fastest way to answer it.
- **Prioritise ruthlessly.** Five to seven critical assumptions, ranked by risk — not fifty unranked ones.

## On the pitch

- **Three minutes, then stop.** The hardest discipline and the most important. The instinct to explain everything is exactly what loses the room. Trust the format; let them ask.
- **Let the poster carry context.** If reviewers can't understand it without you narrating, the poster isn't done — fix the poster, don't add more talking.

## On feedback

- **Receive, don't defend.** The assumptions zone has already given everyone permission to be honest. The author's job in the Critique round is to listen and capture.
- **Place feedback where it applies.** Stickies go on the part of the poster they're about, initialled, so the author can act on them precisely.

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ **One zone polished, the rest empty.** → Build a rough version of all three zones first, then improve in layers.
- ❌ **An unrealistic customer.** → Choose a customer the proposition clearly helps; make them believable.
- ❌ **Abstract language that needs narration.** → Use accessible language a stranger understands unaided.
- ❌ **Assuming desire.** → Name the specific pain and who you'll validate it with.
- ❌ **A wall of unprioritised assumptions.** → Deal-breakers only, ranked.
- ❌ **Trying to prototype the whole idea.** → Use the poster to pick the one minimal element to build first.
