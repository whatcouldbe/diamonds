# Value Proposition Poster — FAQ

## How is this different from a Concept Poster?
The Concept Poster (Luma) is a general "present the main points of an idea on one page" format. The Value Proposition Poster is a more opinionated descendant: it fixes the structure around a value proposition *statement*, a customer *storyboard*, and an explicit *assumptions* zone — and it's built specifically to be pitched in three minutes to a busy audience.

## How is it different from the Value Proposition Canvas?
Strategyzer's Value Proposition Canvas is rigorous but hard for many people to read on sight. The poster's single-sentence statement does a more legible job of conveying value to a non-specialist, while keeping the canvas's underlying logic of jobs, pains, and gains.

## We have several ideas — should each get a poster?
No. The poster is for an idea that's already been chosen. Diverge and prioritise first (e.g. Importance/Difficulty Matrix); poster the bet, not the whole set.

## What if we haven't done any research yet?
Then the poster will look polished but rest on assumptions. It will still surface those assumptions honestly in zone three — but a confident poster on an unvalidated problem is the exact trap to avoid. Do some Looking first, or at minimum be explicit that the statement is a claim, not a finding.

## How long should the storyboard be?
Six panels is the standard — it forces a problem-to-transformation arc without sprawling. Fewer and you lose the journey; more and it stops being scannable in three minutes.

## Do we need icons or images?
Words first, always. Icons make it scannable; images make it clearer still — but only add them once the story text is finished, and only upgrade to images if there's time. A complete story in plain text beats a half-finished one with beautiful art.

## Who fills in the assumptions — won't it look weak to a backer?
The team does, and no — naming what you don't yet know is more credible than false certainty. It turns a pitch into a working session and invites help rather than a verdict. Push for the deal-breakers, not the safe questions.

## How does feedback get captured?
Through a Critique round: reviewers add initialled stickies using four prompts (clarifying / strong / challenges / build-on), placed on the part of the poster they refer to. In a multi-team workshop, teams review each other's posters in a shared space.

## What do we do after the poster?
Fold the feedback in, then take one of two routes forward — often both:

- **Build something:** pick the single element worth prototyping first — the idea is almost always too big to build whole. That's the handoff into Rough & Ready Prototyping.
- **Learn something:** do further research. The poster and the Critique round frequently point you at *who to go and talk to* — a reviewer says "you need to speak to so-and-so," or a promoted assumption can only be settled by hearing from real customers. That's a handoff into Interviewing or Think-Aloud Testing.

Either way, the promoted assumptions become the agenda — they can be formalised as Hypothesis Statements, then answered by the cheapest, fastest mix of building and researching that gives good-enough evidence.
