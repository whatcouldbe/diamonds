# Value Proposition Poster

A single-page format that makes a new idea understandable and compelling to people who weren't in the room when it was born. It carries three things at the right altitude for a busy audience: a sharp **value proposition statement**, a **storyboard** showing the idea in a customer's hands, and the **key questions and assumptions** that admit what isn't known yet. It is built to be pitched in three minutes — and to earn the conversation that follows.

---

## Purpose

Use the Value Proposition Poster when an idea has survived selection and now needs to **travel**. The idea has been generated and developed — often through Round Robin or a Creative Matrix — and prioritised — often through an Importance/Difficulty Matrix or bullseye diagramming. Now it has to be shown to people who weren't there: colleagues, stakeholders, the executives who might back it.

**The core problem it resolves:** a post-it note is too little, and the research pile is too much. You can't carry a sticky into the room and expect it to land, and you can't carry acres of printed documentation and expect a busy person to read it. The poster is the right altitude — succinct enough to grasp in minutes, complete enough to convey what the idea is, the problem it solves, who it's for, and why it creates value.

**The core question:** Can someone who has never heard of this idea understand it, get excited about it, and ask a good question about it — in three minutes?

**Why the Value Proposition Poster works:**
- It forces the team to pull back from saying everything. The discipline is the value: when you stop trying to convey every detail, the idea becomes legible — and people lean in and ask, rather than glaze over.
- It puts a value proposition *in context*. A statement alone is abstract; the storyboard shows the idea come to life in a real customer's hands, which is what makes people remember it and support it.
- It builds in honesty. The "key questions and assumptions" zone demonstrates the team doesn't know everything yet — which is more credible to a backer than false certainty, and which sets up structured feedback rather than applause.
- It hands off cleanly to Critique. Because the poster already names its own open questions, it invites constructive feedback instead of a yes/no verdict.

**What makes it different from a pitch deck:** a deck tends to grow until it says everything; the poster is one page that says the essential thing. A deck is presented *at* an audience; the poster is built to be pitched briefly and then *talked around*. The format does the editing the presenter often won't.

**What a finished poster gives you:**
- A way to **promote a vision of the future** — making an abstract idea concrete enough that others can see it
- The raw material for a **business case** and for **gaining support from decision-makers**
- A **roadmap for moving forward** — the assumptions and feedback surface what to work on next
- A means of identifying **which element to prototype first** (see *Identifying the first thing to prototype*, below)

### Lineage

The Value Proposition Poster is an evolution of three lineages, and it's worth naming them honestly:
- **Luma's Concept Poster** — a presentation format illustrating the main points of a new idea. The Value Proposition Poster is a more opinionated, value-led descendant of it.
- **Strategyzer's Value Proposition Canvas** — a rigorous tool for articulating value, but one many people find hard to interpret on sight. The poster's statement does a more legible job of presenting a value proposition to a non-specialist audience.
- **Field practice (What Could Be)** — working with large enterprises, WCB found that busy audiences (especially executives deciding what to back) needed a format that conveyed the proposition faster and more memorably. The Value Proposition Poster is the result. It has been taught many times and is embedded in some organisations' own ways of working.

## Anatomy

The poster is **one page, three zones**, read left to right. Feedback is layered on top as a fourth, temporary state.

### Zone 1 — Value Proposition (the statement)
A single structured sentence, filled in like a mad-lib:

> **Our** *[product / service]* **help(s)** *[customer segment]* **who want to** *[job to be done]* **by** *[verb] [customer pain]* **and** *[verb] [customer gain]*

Optionally extended with **unlike** *[competing value proposition]* to sharpen differentiation.

The statement names, in order: what the idea is, who it's for, the job they're trying to get done, the pain it removes, and the gain it creates. Specificity is everything — "oncologists treating rare cancers," not "healthcare providers"; "reduce waiting time from 30 minutes to 2," not "save time."

### Zone 2 — How it will work (the storyboard)
A short visual story — typically six panels — showing one named customer experiencing the value proposition. It answers six questions:

1. Who is the character in your story? What problem are they facing?
2. What is needed to help them solve their problem?
3. How do they get started?
4. What happens next?
5. What happens at the end?
6. What is the result? How does that make things better for your character?

The story is human-centred, not technical — it shows the *experience* of the value, not the engineering of the solution. A named, believable character (not "the user") is what makes it land.

### Zone 3 — Key questions and assumptions
Stickies capturing what the team doesn't yet know, sorted across three dimensions:
- **Desirability** — will customers actually want this? What's assumed about the real pain and gain?
- **Feasibility** — can we build it? What's assumed about technology, capability, expertise?
- **Viability** — does it make business sense? What's assumed about cost, market, timeline, organisational impact?

The most critical questions — the deal-breakers — are promoted onto the poster itself. This zone is the heart of the method's integrity: it is the team saying, in public, "here is what would have to be true, and here is what we still need to find out."

### Layer 4 — Feedback (temporary)
When the poster is shared, reviewers add feedback stickies directly onto it, initialled and placed where they apply. The prompts come from **Critique** and **Rose, Thorn, Bud**:
- What clarifying questions do you have?
- What is strong about this work, and why? *(Rose)*
- What challenges might this work face, and why? *(Thorn)*
- How might we build on or strengthen this work? *(Bud)*

## Setup

### Participants
- **The idea's author / team** — builds the poster. Works well solo or in a small team that developed the concept.
- **A facilitator** — if run as a workshop across multiple teams, manages timing and the feedback round.
- **Reviewers** — colleagues or stakeholders who give feedback. They don't need prior context; the poster is the context.

### What you need
- The idea — already generated and ideally prioritised (a winning concept, not a raw brainstorm)
- The poster template (statement frame, six-panel storyboard, assumptions area)
- Icons / simple imagery for the storyboard
- Sticky notes for assumptions and for feedback
- Optional but recommended: an AI assistant to pressure-test the statement, refine the storyboard, and surface assumptions (see `resources/ai-usage.md`)

### Time
The method builds in layers; each can be done quickly and improved if time allows. A guide for a facilitated build:
- **Statement:** draft (~10 min) → pressure-test (~15 min) → revise (~10 min)
- **Storyboard:** answer the six questions (~15 min) → add icons (~10 min) → refine (~15 min) → improve imagery if time (~10 min)
- **Assumptions:** generate across desirability/feasibility/viability (~10 min) → prioritise and promote (~5 min)
- **Feedback:** a Critique round on the finished poster

A poster can be drafted in a focused session and then improved over days as it's shared and feedback comes in.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Craft the value proposition statement
Fill in each part of the statement. Don't aim for perfection on the first pass — get something down, then make every field specific. Be concrete about the segment, the job, the pain, and the gain. Pressure-test it (with a colleague or an AI assistant) for a too-generic segment, a vague outcome, or weak differentiation, then revise. The test of a good statement: someone outside the team reads it once and knows who it's for and why it matters.

### Step 2 — Show how the idea will work
Answer the six storyboard questions as short, plain-language captions — overwrite each question with a one-line answer. Choose a believable customer and tell their story from problem to transformation. Then make it visual: add an icon to each of the six panels; if there's time, upgrade icons to simple images that make the story clearer. **Words before icons; icons before images** — finish the story text before decorating it.

### Step 3 — Capture key questions and assumptions
Review the statement and storyboard and ask, across all three dimensions, "what are we assuming, and what do we still need to know?" Write one question or assumption per sticky. Then decide which are critical — the ones that would *kill the idea if proven wrong* — and promote those onto the poster. Make them testable: convert a vague worry ("this is risky") into a specific question ("can insulin be heated to room temperature in under two minutes, safely?").

### Step 4 — Assemble the poster
Bring the three zones together on one page: statement on the left, storyboard top-right, critical assumptions bottom-right. Check that it reads in three minutes and that nothing on it requires the author standing next to it to explain.

### Step 5 — Gather constructive feedback
Share the poster and run a Critique round. Reviewers add initialled stickies using the four prompts (clarifying / strong / challenges / build-on), placed where they apply. The author's job here is to listen, not defend — the open-assumptions zone has already given everyone permission to be honest.

### Step 6 — Consider, iterate, and choose what to build first
Take the feedback back into the poster. Strengthen weak areas, answer clarifying questions, fold new assumptions into the list. The poster is a living artifact you keep improving as it's shared — and the prioritised assumptions become the agenda for actual validation. Critically, use the feedback to choose how to move forward — usually one or both of two routes: **build something** (identify the one element worth prototyping first — the idea is almost always too big to build whole, so name the smallest, most uncertain piece, and carry it into Rough & Ready Prototyping) or **learn something** (do further research — the poster and the Critique round often point you at *who to go and talk to*, or surface an assumption that only real customers can settle, which hands off to Interviewing or Think-Aloud Testing).

## Facilitation Notes

### The three-minute discipline is the method
The single most important thing to coach: **pull back from saying everything.** The instinct of an excited idea-author is to convey every detail, every piece of research, every nuance — and that instinct is exactly what makes a room glaze over. Teach people to pitch the poster in three minutes and trust the audience to ask. The format earns the questions; the over-explanation kills them.

### Build in layers, never one zone to perfection
The most common failure is spending all the time on one zone — usually polishing the statement — and never finishing the storyboard or the assumptions. Coach teams to get a rough version of all three zones down first, then improve. A complete rough poster beats a beautiful third of one.

### A real, named customer or it doesn't land
"The user" is not a character. The storyboard works because someone in the room can picture a specific person with a genuine problem. If the customer feels generic or unrealistic, the story won't carry — choose a customer the value proposition clearly helps and give them a name.

### Assumptions are a feature, not a confession
Teams sometimes resist filling the assumptions zone because it feels like admitting weakness in front of a backer. The opposite is true: naming what you don't yet know is more credible than false certainty, and it's what turns a pitch into a working session. Push for the *uncomfortable* questions — the deal-breakers — not the safe ones.

### AI is a strong second pair of eyes
At each stage — statement, storyboard, assumptions — an AI assistant can pressure-test for specificity, surface blind spots, and propose tests for assumptions. It doesn't replace the team's judgement, but it catches the generic and the vague fast. See `resources/ai-usage.md` for stage prompts.

### Identifying the first thing to prototype
A poster usually describes an idea too big to prototype whole. One of the most valuable moves — especially right after the Critique round — is to point at a *part* of the poster and ask: "which single element should we make first?" The storyboard panels and the promoted assumptions are the candidates; the goal is the smallest, most uncertain element that, if built rough and tested, would teach the team the most. This is the natural bridge into **Rough & Ready Prototyping** — the poster names the bet, the prototype tests it.

## Variations

### Solo / one-on-one build
The agent and one author build the poster together, with the agent running the pressure-test passes (statement critique, storyboard refinement, assumption surfacing) that a workshop would distribute across a team. See `resources/ai-usage.md`.

### Multi-team workshop with peer feedback
Several teams build posters in parallel, then review each other's work. Each team drops its finished poster into a shared space; reviewers visit, read, and leave initialled feedback stickies using the Critique prompts. This is the format WCB has taught at scale — see `resources/examples/`.

### Time-constrained ("just the statement")
When time is very short, the value proposition statement alone — done well and specifically — is a usable artifact. The storyboard and assumptions can follow. But a statement without the storyboard is abstract; treat statement-only as a starting point, not the finished poster.

### Adding differentiation ("unlike")
For competitive or crowded contexts, extend the statement with *"unlike [competing value proposition]"* and name what's genuinely different — not "unlike traditional approaches" but the specific contrast. Useful when the audience's first question will be "how is this different from what we already do?"

## Outputs

- **A one-page Value Proposition Poster** — statement, storyboard, and critical assumptions — that conveys the idea to a busy audience in roughly three minutes
- **A prioritised set of assumptions and key questions** — the deal-breakers, ready to be validated
- **A set of feedback stickies** — clarifying questions, strengths, challenges, and build-ons from reviewers
- **A pitch** — the author can now present the idea briefly and credibly to stakeholders who weren't in the room

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
The Value Proposition Poster sits in **Making / Design Rationale** — at the point in the Develop/Deliver space where a chosen concept needs to be communicated, pressure-tested, and championed. It is a packaging-and-rationale method, not an ideation method: it assumes you already have an idea worth backing.

### Upstream Methods (the poster receives from)
- **Round Robin** and **Creative Matrix** — generate and develop the concept the poster will carry
- **Importance/Difficulty Matrix** / bullseye diagramming — prioritise which idea earns a poster
- Any synthesis that produced a customer, a job to be done, and a pain/gain worth addressing

### Downstream Methods (the poster feeds into)
- **Critique** — the structured feedback round run on the finished poster; the poster is built to hand off to it
- **Rose, Thorn, Bud** — the feedback framework layered onto the poster (strong / challenges / build-on)
- **Hypothesis Statements** — the prioritised assumptions become hypotheses to test
- **Rough & Ready Prototyping** — after critique, the team points at one element of the poster (a storyboard panel, a promoted assumption) and prototypes that minimal piece first, rather than the whole idea
- **Interviewing**, **Think-Aloud Testing** — the methods that actually validate the promoted assumptions

### Related Methods
- **Concept Poster (Luma)** — the ancestor; a more general "present the main points of an idea" format
- **Value Proposition Canvas (Strategyzer)** — the rigorous tool the statement makes more legible
