# Affinity Clustering

A synthesis method that organizes individual data items into clusters by similarity to surface themes, patterns, and insights from a body of research.

---

## Purpose

Use after gathering data — most often from user research — when you need to make sense of many individual items and identify what they collectively reveal. Affinity clustering transforms raw observations, quotes, and notes into organized themes with named insights.

**The core question:** What is the data telling us?

**What makes this different from sorting by category:**
- Category sorting pre-defines buckets and sorts items into them — themes are assumed before looking at the data
- Affinity clustering starts blank and lets themes emerge from the content — groupings arrive bottom-up, not top-down
- The cluster title is not a category label. It is the beginning of an insight.

**Where it sits in the synthesis progression:**
Codification (Rose, Thorn, Bud or similar) → Affinity Clustering → Prioritization (Bullseye, Importance/Difficulty Matrix) → Framing (How Might We)

---

## Setup

### Participants
- **2–4 people** working in real time is the ideal
- Can be done by one person working individually
- Can work as an asynchronous activity — one person starts, passes to a colleague who adds to or reviews and rearranges the emerging clusters
- More than four people and the session tends to slow down

### Data
- Individual items, each on its own sticky note or digital equivalent
- Typically from user research: interviews, contextual inquiry, observation
- Often pre-codified using Rose, Thorn, Bud — color-coded to indicate what's working (rose/pink), what's not working (thorn/blue), and ideas or opportunities (bud/green)
- Can also come from desk research, stakeholder input, assumptions, or other sources — any body of items you need to make sense of

### Materials
- A blank board (physical wall or digital workspace like Miro or FigJam) — start with empty space, not pre-labeled zones
- Pre-written sticky notes, one item per note
- Optional: color coding from Rose, Thorn, Bud (pink, blue, green)

### Time
- **~45 minutes** for a typical session
- Set a timer — 45 minutes is a productive forcing function
- For large datasets, do one cycle in 45 minutes, then return; or pass the work to a colleague to continue
- Time scales with both the volume of data and the number of people involved

## Steps

### Step 1 — Frame the Purpose
Orient the group to what you've done and what you're trying to learn. Name the research context: where the data came from, who was involved, and what question you were exploring.

If the data has been codified using Rose, Thorn, Bud, explain what the colors mean before starting.

### Step 2 — Start with a Blank Area
The working area should be empty. Do not pre-label sections or create buckets in advance. Themes must emerge from the data, not from assumptions about what the themes will be.

If someone begins pre-labeling areas before clustering starts, pause and redirect. The tendency to pre-define buckets is common — particularly in groups with domain expertise — and it is one of the biggest risks to the quality of the output.

### Step 3 — Describe, Then Place
One person picks an item, reads it aloud, and places it in the blank area. The act of reading aloud matters — it ensures the whole group understands what each item says and what it means.

### Step 4 — Invite Similarity
After the first item is placed, ask: *"Who has something that's a bit like that?"* Participants with similar items place them nearby, reading each one aloud before placing it.

The criterion for proximity is affinity — shared meaning, not shared sentiment. A cluster may contain roses, thorns, and buds together, because items across all three types can speak to the same underlying theme.

### Step 5 — Repeat Until All Items Are Placed
When no more items have affinity with the current cluster, pick up a different item and start again. Keep rotating who places items to keep the group engaged and to prevent any one person from dominating.

Continue until every item has been placed somewhere.

### Step 6 — Discuss and Rearrange
Once all items are placed, step back and look at the emerging picture. Some clusters will feel settled; others will need adjustment. This is the time to:
- Move items between clusters where a better fit becomes apparent
- Split a cluster that has grown too large or contains more than one theme
- Combine clusters that turn out to be saying the same thing
- Promote a solo item to its own cluster, or absorb it into an adjacent one

This is a discussion step — the group should be talking through their reasoning as they rearrange.

### Step 7 — Handle Edge Cases
**Lone items:** A cluster of one is valid. Not every item will group with others. Don't force a lone item into a cluster where it doesn't belong — place it on its own and give it a title.

**Items that fit two clusters:** If an item genuinely belongs in two different clusters, duplicate it and place a copy in each. Also ask whether the item is significant enough to anchor its own cluster.

**Large clusters:** A cluster of more than ~8 items is worth interrogating. Does it contain two themes that could be separated? A large, undifferentiated cluster often means the grouping is too broad — the insight isn't sharp enough yet.

### Step 8 — Label the Clusters
Labeling happens after clustering, not during. Don't commit to titles too early — clusters shift, and premature labels can anchor items in the wrong place.

When labeling:
- **Avoid one-word titles.** A title like "Communication" or "Cost" is a category, not an insight.
- **Write in full sentences.** *"People don't realise there's financial help available"* is a title; *"Finance"* is not.
- **Lead with a verb.** Action-oriented titles connect more directly to what could be done: *"Adapting to constantly changing global regulation"* is closer to an insight than *"Regulation."*
- **Apply the three-part test:** What is the title? What is the insight? What is the action? If you can't get to all three, the title isn't finished.

It's fine to start with rough placeholder titles and refine them as clusters settle. The final title is where the insight lives.

### Step 9 — Read the Heat Map
If the data has been color-coded using Rose, Thorn, Bud, the completed cluster board is also a heat map. Clusters with a high proportion of thorns (challenges) and buds (opportunities) are often the ones worth acting on. Clusters that are predominantly rose (positive) describe things that are working — important to know, but often not where the design work happens.

Use the heat map to orient attention, not to make final decisions.

### Step 10 — Articulate What You Learned
For each cluster you want to carry forward, ask: *"What did we learn about this cluster, and why is that important?"* This question bridges the cluster title to an actionable insight and prepares the work for prioritization or How Might We framing.

## Facilitation Notes

### Never Pre-Define the Buckets
The single most important thing to protect is the blank starting area. Groups with domain expertise are particularly prone to pre-categorizing: they know in advance that there will be finance items, communication items, process items. Let that knowledge inform their reading of what emerges — don't let it determine the structure before the data speaks.

### Keep the Criterion Clear
Affinity means shared meaning — items that are similar because of what they say, not because of where they came from or what valence they carry. When a grouping is being discussed, ask: *"What do these items have in common? What's the shared idea?"* That question should always be answerable.

### Rotate Who Places Items
Don't let one person drive the whole session. When starting a new cluster, walk up to a different person and ask them to read a sticky and place it. Then ask the group: *"Who has one that's a bit like this?"* This spreads ownership and prevents any one person's mental model from dominating the output.

### Read Every Item Aloud
Stickies are often written in shorthand — a quote fragment, a brief observation, a half-formed idea. Reading aloud forces interpretation and surfaces ambiguity early. If the group can't agree on what an item means, that's worth resolving before placing it.

### Label Last, Title Thoughtfully
The temptation to name clusters early is strong, especially in groups that like to organize as they go. Hold the line: rough placeholder labels are fine, but commit to titles only when clusters have settled. The title is doing important work — it carries the insight forward.

## Variations

### Individual Clustering
One person working alone can do affinity clustering. The absence of discussion is a trade-off — you lose the challenge and perspective that comes from multiple people reading the same items differently. Compensate by being slower and more deliberate: read each item aloud, ask the affinity question explicitly, step back frequently to assess the emerging picture.

### Asynchronous Collaborative Clustering
One person starts and builds an initial set of clusters, then hands the board to a colleague who adds items, adjusts groupings, and refines titles. This works well for distributed teams and removes the need for everyone to be present simultaneously. The risk is that early placements anchor subsequent contributors — mitigate by leaving notes explaining the reasoning behind groupings.

### Super-Clusters
Once individual clusters have settled, related clusters can be grouped into higher-order collections with an overarching theme name. This adds a layer of structure above the clusters themselves and can help orient large datasets. Super-clustering is best done after individual clusters are stable — it's a second pass, not a first one.

### Without Color Coding
Affinity clustering works without Rose, Thorn, Bud or any color scheme. The heat map reading in Step 9 isn't available, but all other steps apply. In this case, prioritization happens through a separate method — Bullseye Diagramming or Importance/Difficulty Matrix — rather than through visual cues.

### Physical vs. Digital
Both work. Physical (sticky notes on a wall) tends to be more tactile and embodied — easier to move things around, harder to read small text at a distance. Digital (Miro, FigJam) offers the ability to zoom, align, and share; it also makes async work straightforward. The method is the same in both environments.

---

## Outputs

- **A clustered board** — items organized into thematic groups, each with a descriptive title
- **Cluster titles** — full sentences that name the insight, not category labels
- **A heat map** — visible distribution of RTB colors across clusters (when color coding has been used)
- **"What did we learn" statements** — articulations of what each priority cluster reveals and why it matters
- **Insight seeds** — cluster titles that are close to, or can be directly converted into, How Might We statements

## Connections

### In the Synthesis Progression
- **Upstream:** Rose, Thorn, Bud (codification), Interviewing, Contextual Inquiry, What's on Your Radar
- **Downstream:** Importance/Difficulty Matrix, Bullseye Diagramming, How Might We framing

### In the Double Diamond
Affinity clustering sits at the transition between Discover and Define — it is the primary method for moving from a body of research to a defined set of insights and themes.

### Natural Sequences
- **Immediately before Importance/Difficulty Matrix:** Synthesized cluster titles become the items to prioritize — each cluster is a potential focus area, and the matrix helps the team decide which to pursue
- **After Interviewing or Contextual Inquiry:** The raw notes from field sessions are the input; clustering is how those notes become insights
- **Before How Might We:** Strong cluster titles — verb-led, descriptive — convert directly into HMW statements with minimal additional work
