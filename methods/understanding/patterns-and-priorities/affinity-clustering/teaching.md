# Affinity Clustering — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever come back from a week of fieldwork with hundreds of sticky notes and felt completely overwhelmed? You know there's something important in all that data. You just can't see it yet. Individual observations are interesting — but they don't tell you anything by themselves. What you need is a way to let the data speak."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

We spent time with people living with Type 1 diabetes — interviewing them, observing their routines, understanding the real texture of managing blood sugar day to day. We came out of it with a mass of individual notes: things people said, things we observed, moments that surprised us. We'd codified those notes using Rose, Thorn, Bud — so we knew which ones were positive, which ones captured challenges, and which ones held opportunity.

Then we clustered. We started with a blank board. No buckets, no categories pre-defined. One item at a time, reading aloud, asking: *who has something that's a bit like this?* Patterns emerged that we hadn't anticipated — things about equipment access, about the financial system around test strips, about the cognitive load of knowing when to act.

When we got to the cluster that became *"People don't realise there's financial help available"* — that was the moment. That title is not a category. It's a finding. It tells you something has to change.

**The bucket story:** I once watched someone in a workshop start placing large sticky notes on the blank area before we'd begun — labeling them "Finance," "Communication," "Process." I walked over and asked what he was doing. He said he was getting the buckets ready. He knew from experience what the categories would be. And he was probably right — but that's exactly the problem. We were going to let the themes emerge from the activity. That's a really important thing to protect.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** Individual data items on sticky notes — typically from user research (interviews, contextual inquiry, observation), often pre-codified using Rose, Thorn, Bud. A blank workspace. Two to four people who've been through the research.

**Outputs:** A clustered board with thematic groups. Cluster titles — full sentences, not category labels. A heat map of where challenges and opportunities concentrate. Insight seeds that feed directly into prioritization or How Might We framing.

**Connection forward:** Strong cluster titles — verb-led, descriptive — convert almost directly into How Might We statements. The synthesis work is most of the way to a design brief. Clusters also become the items to prioritize in an Importance/Difficulty Matrix or Bullseye session.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full 10-step process and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**
- **Start blank** — no pre-defined buckets. Ever. The bucket tendency is the most common thing to catch and correct.
- **Read every item aloud** — stickies are shorthand; reading forces interpretation and surfaces ambiguity.
- **Affinity, not sentiment** — clusters can and should contain roses, thorns, and buds together. The grouping criterion is shared meaning.
- **Label last, title thoughtfully** — rough placeholders are fine during clustering; commit to titles only when clusters have settled.
- **The three-part test** — what is the title? what is the insight? what is the action? A title that can't answer all three isn't done.
- **A cluster of one is valid** — don't force items into clusters where they don't belong.

**When teaching someone to facilitate this method:** Run a live clustering session with real (or anonymized) data. Watch for the bucket tendency and interrupt it when it appears — it's the best teaching moment the method offers.

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about a body of data you've collected recently — or one that's sitting in a folder somewhere waiting to be looked at. What would it take to run a clustering session on it this week?"*

Close with a commitment: *"What's one dataset you're willing to bring to a clustering session?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The method is about letting the data speak. When you start with a blank area and let themes emerge, you find patterns you didn't expect — patterns that challenge your assumptions about what the research revealed. And when you arrive at a cluster title that is a full sentence, that starts with a verb, that answers "what did we learn and why does it matter" — you've got an insight. An insight is the beginning of design. And it was always in the data. You just had to stop deciding in advance what you were going to find.

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- ~45 minutes; set a timer
- A blank board — physical or digital (Miro, FigJam)
- Pre-written sticky notes, one item per note
- Color coding if RTB has been used (pink/rose, blue/thorn, green/bud)
- 2–4 participants familiar with the data
