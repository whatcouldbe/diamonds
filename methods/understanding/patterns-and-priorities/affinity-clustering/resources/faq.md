# Affinity Clustering — FAQ

Questions that come up in practice. Grows over time as practitioners contribute from real sessions.

---

**How many items do you need before clustering is worthwhile?**

Even a dozen items can produce two or three meaningful clusters. Less isn't a reason not to try — it just means the session will be shorter and the output more focused. More items produce richer clusters and more nuanced insights, but the method works at small scales too.

---

**What if an item seems to fit in two different clusters?**

Duplicate it and place a copy in each. But also ask whether the item is significant enough to anchor its own cluster. Sometimes the right answer isn't duplication or forced placement — it's recognizing that the item is pointing at something that deserves its own theme.

---

**What do you do with items that don't cluster with anything?**

Give them a cluster of one. A lone item is valid — it may represent something distinct and important that just doesn't share affinity with other items in the dataset. Don't force it into a cluster where it doesn't belong just to avoid having a solo.

---

**How do you know when you're done?**

When every item has been designated a cluster. At that point the board represents the full shape of the data. What comes next — rearranging, refining titles, reading the heat map — is refinement, not completion.

---

**What if the team disagrees about where something belongs?**

Hear the reasoning. If someone has a valid rationale for why an item belongs in a different cluster or deserves its own, respect it — their experience may be leading them somewhere the rest of the group hasn't reached. If the cluster in question already has plenty of items that clearly support its theme, one item moving in or out may not change the insight much. The goal is shared understanding, not consensus by default — and "disagree and commit" is a valid outcome when the group has genuinely heard each other.

---

**Should you keep participant names or sources visible on the stickies while clustering?**

Not as a default. The source document — the interview transcript, the contextual inquiry notes — is more useful than the participant name, because it gives you a way to go back and verify context. But be careful not to confuse source with participant: a sticky note might reference something a participant said, and the source is the session it came from — those are different things. Showing source is an option; showing participant name is optional and worth considering only when the identity of who said something is directly relevant to how the item should be read.

---

**Can you cluster data that isn't from user research?**

Yes. Affinity clustering works on any body of items you're trying to make sense of — desk research findings, stakeholder perspectives, assumptions you want to examine, ideas from a brainstorm. The method most often comes out of user research because that's where the volume of individual items tends to build up, but the technique is agnostic to source.

---

**How do you avoid pre-defining what the themes will be?**

Start with a blank area and resist the urge to label it before clustering begins. The most common way this goes wrong is when someone — usually a domain expert who knows the subject well — begins placing category headers on the board before any items have been placed. They're often right about the categories. But allowing prior knowledge to pre-structure the output means you'll find what you expected to find and miss what the data is actually showing. Let the themes emerge from the activity.

---

**What's the difference between a cluster title and an insight?**

A useful mantra: what is the title? what is the insight? what is the action? A cluster title names what the group of items reveals. An insight articulates why that matters. An action points toward what could be done about it. Strong cluster titles compress all three — they're close enough to an insight that the action almost writes itself. Weak titles (one word, category-level) don't do any of that work.

---

**How do you prioritize which clusters to act on?**

Two approaches work well. If the data has been color-coded using Rose, Thorn, Bud, the heat map is already visible — clusters with high proportions of thorns and buds tend to be where the design work is. For a more structured prioritization, take the cluster titles into a Bullseye Diagramming session using MoSCoW rules (Must, Should, Could, Won't) — place each cluster title on the bullseye and discuss its priority. The clusters that land in the "Must" zone are the ones to act on first.

---

**What if one person dominates and keeps moving other people's stickies?**

A strong facilitator is the answer. If the group can't self-regulate, the facilitator needs to actively rotate who is placing items — walk up to a specific person, ask them to pick an item and read it, ask the group who has something similar, then move to a different person for the next starting item. This keeps the energy moving around the group and prevents any one person's mental model from becoming the organizing logic of the board.

---

**Is there a minimum or maximum number of clusters you should end up with?**

No. The number of clusters is a function of the data — how many items there are, how diverse the themes are, how rich each theme turns out to be. Don't set a target. Let the clusters that belong together find each other, and let the ones that don't stay apart.
