# Affinity Clustering — Tips & Advice

Collected practitioner wisdom for running Affinity Clustering well. These are the things that consistently trip facilitators up and the moves that make the difference between a good session and a great one.

---

## Before the Session

**Start with a blank area. Defend it.**
The working space must be empty before clustering begins. No pre-labeled sections, no buckets waiting to be filled. If someone sets up category headings in advance — even well-intentioned ones — the method is already compromised. Themes must emerge from the data, not from prior assumptions about what the data will say.

**Set a timer for 45 minutes.**
A timed session creates productive pressure. Without it, clustering tends to sprawl. 45 minutes is the forcing function that keeps the group moving — you may not finish, but you'll make real progress and know where to pick up.

**One item per note.**
If a sticky note contains two observations, it needs to be split. Items that carry multiple ideas can fit into two clusters for different reasons — or fit into neither cleanly. Splitting before you start saves arguments later.

---

## During the Session

**Read every item aloud before placing it.**
Stickies are often written in shorthand — fragments, quotes pulled from context, half-formed observations. Reading aloud forces the person placing the item to interpret it, and gives the group a shared understanding of what it means. If the group can't agree on what an item means, that's worth resolving before placing it.

**Keep the affinity question explicit.**
When building a cluster, the question to keep returning to is: *"What do these items have in common in terms of what they say?"* Not: *"Are these all thorns?"* Not: *"Did these all come from the same interview?"* Shared meaning is the criterion. Keep it visible.

**Rotate who places items.**
Don't let one person drive the whole session. When starting a new cluster, walk up to a different person and ask them to pick an item and read it aloud. Then ask the group: *"Who has something that's a bit like this?"* Rotating ownership prevents any one person's mental model from dominating the output — and keeps energy distributed around the group.

**A cluster of one is fine.**
Not every item will group with others. When an item doesn't have natural companions, give it its own space and a title. Don't force it into a cluster where it doesn't belong — a strained cluster title is a sign that items have been grouped by convenience rather than by affinity.

**Interrogate large clusters.**
A cluster of more than ~8 items is a signal. Ask: *"Is this actually one theme, or are there two different ideas here?"* Large clusters often contain sub-themes that are worth separating. A cluster that captures everything tends to reveal nothing.

**Duplicate items that genuinely fit two clusters.**
If an item clearly belongs in two places, copy it and place it in both. But also ask: is this item significant enough to anchor its own cluster? Sometimes the right answer is neither duplication nor forced placement — it's recognizing that the item is pointing to something that deserves its own theme.

## Labeling

**Label late, not early.**
Resist the urge to commit to cluster titles while clustering is still in progress. Premature titles anchor items in the wrong place and make it harder to rearrange. Rough placeholders ("finance stuff," "knowledge gap") are fine during the session — refine them into real titles only when clusters have settled.

**One word is not a title.**
"Communication," "Cost," "Regulation" are categories. They describe a topic, not an insight. A title does more work than a label — it names what the cluster reveals.

**Write in full sentences.**
"People don't realise there's financial help available" is a title. "Finance" is not. The sentence form forces you to articulate what the cluster is actually saying, not just what it's about.

**Lead with a verb.**
Action-oriented titles travel further. "Adapting to constantly changing global regulation" puts the insight in motion. "Regulation" doesn't. Verb-led titles are also easier to convert into How Might We statements — often just a small reframing away.

**Apply the three-part test.**
For each title, ask: What is the title? What is the insight? What is the action? If you can't get to all three, the title isn't finished. This test is most useful for cluster titles that feel complete but aren't quite there yet.

## The Heat Map

**Use color as orientation, not conclusion.**
If the data has been codified using Rose, Thorn, Bud, the completed board is a heat map. Clusters heavy in thorns (blue) and buds (green) are often where the design work lives — they reveal challenges and opportunities. Clusters heavy in roses (pink) describe what's working. Use this to orient attention in prioritization, not to dismiss any cluster outright.

**Don't skip the "what did we learn" question.**
For each cluster you want to carry forward, ask: *"What did we learn about this cluster, and why is that important?"* This is the bridge from a title to an actionable insight — and from an insight to a How Might We statement. It's worth pausing to answer it explicitly rather than assuming the title does enough work on its own.
