# Importance / Difficulty Matrix

A two-axis prioritization method that plots items by relative importance (x-axis) and relative difficulty (y-axis) to surface trade-offs, align stakeholders, and produce an emergent road map.

---

## Purpose

Use when a group needs to prioritize a set of proposed initiatives, ideas, or opportunities against two dimensions — how much something matters and how hard it is to accomplish — to create a sequenced plan everyone can get behind.

**The core question:** Given everything we could do, what should we do first, and why?

**What makes this different from simpler prioritization:**
- Dot voting (Visualize the Vote) is one-dimensional — it captures preference but not trade-offs
- Bullseye diagramming is also one-dimensional — it ranks importance but ignores difficulty
- The Importance/Difficulty Matrix forces trade-off discussions across both dimensions. When you bring together stakeholders with different expertise — someone who understands the difficulty of implementation alongside someone focused on strategic importance — you get richer, more honest conversations

**Where it sits in the prioritization progression:**
Visualize the Vote (simplest) → Bullseye Diagramming → Importance/Difficulty Matrix (most robust)

## Setup

### Participants
- 4–8 people with diverse perspectives on both importance and difficulty
- A facilitator (or co-facilitator pair) who will not influence the content
- Having people from different areas of expertise strengthens the conversation — someone who really understands difficulty alongside someone thinking about importance

### Items to Prioritize
- **8–10 items** is the sweet spot. More than that and the session bogs down; fewer and the relative comparison loses nuance
- Items must exist before the session — generated from research, surveys, brainstorms, discovery work, or prior synthesis (e.g., affinity clustering)
- If you have more than 10 items, use Bullseye Diagramming first to narrow the set

### Materials
- A board (physical wall or digital workspace like Miro/FigJam) with space for a vertical importance line, then the full 2×2 grid
- Sticky notes with the items pre-written
- The quadrant grid should be **hidden at the start** — only revealed after both axes are scored
- Definitions of importance and difficulty written out and visible throughout

### Time
- 45–60 minutes for a full session with 8 items (importance ranking, difficulty ranking, quadrant reveal, sequencing, and next steps)
- 25 minutes is tight for both axes with 8 items — plan accordingly

## Steps

### Step 1 — Frame the Purpose
State the driving question clearly. Something like: *"How might we turn what our people are telling us into a plan everyone is behind?"* or *"Which of these initiatives should we tackle first and why?"*

Confirm the agenda with participants so everyone knows what they're walking into.

### Step 2 — Confirm the Items
Present the pre-existing items. Read through each one so everyone understands what's on the table. Ask: *"Does this sound fair? Is anything missing from your perspective?"*

This is not a brainstorming step — the items should already exist. The group is confirming shared understanding of what they're working with.

### Step 3 — Define Importance
Provide a specific, context-relevant definition of importance. Not "importance in general" — importance relative to your specific goal.

**Example definitions:**
- *"Does this improve employee engagement and retention? Is it visibly impacting how people experience working here?"*
- *"Does it prepare us to achieve team priorities? Does it improve our alignment and effectiveness?"*

Get group agreement on the definition before proceeding. Allow it to be challenged and refined — the conversation about what importance means here is itself valuable.

### Step 4 — Rank by Relative Importance
This is the first axis. Only the importance dimension is visible — no grid, no quadrants, no difficulty axis yet.

1. **Place the first item in the middle.** Don't discuss it — just put it there. It establishes a reference point. *"I'm going to grab one and stick it in the middle. It doesn't matter which one. We're not going to talk about it too much yet."*
2. **Introduce items one at a time.** For each, ask: *"Is this more or less important than what's already placed?"*
3. **Facilitate the discussion.** Call on specific people. Rotate who speaks. When someone is nodding, name it: *"I see you nodding — what are you thinking?"*
4. **Use the one-position rule.** When debate stalls over a minor difference: *"One position difference isn't going to affect this conversation too much. If someone thinks it should move more than one space, let's talk about it."*
5. **Remind the group it's relative.** *"They are all important. It's just relative to one another."*
6. **Refer back to the definition** when discussion gets abstract.

**Facilitation technique — place along the middle line, then adjust:** Some facilitators place each new item along a horizontal middle line and then move it up or down relative to others. This works well and creates visual clarity.

### Step 5 — Define Difficulty
Now introduce the second dimension. Provide a specific definition of difficulty for this context.

**Example definitions:**
- *"Does this require leadership commitment or cross-team coordination? Is it difficult because it demands organizational change beyond our team's control?"*
- *"How much time and effort is required before, during, and after? Does it need additional budget or external support?"*

Again, get group agreement. Allow refinement — participants may want to add dimensions like resource requirements or timeline.

### Step 6 — Rank by Relative Difficulty
Reveal the difficulty axis (vertical). Same process as importance:

1. Place one item in the middle of the difficulty range
2. Compare each subsequent item: more or less difficult than what's placed?
3. Same facilitation principles — call on people, use the one-position rule, refer to the definition

**Capture connections:** If someone notices that two items are related — the difficulty of one depends on the other, or solving one enables the other — draw a connection line. These relationships matter for sequencing later.

**Alignment trick:** At the end, use your tool's alignment function to evenly distribute items along the axis. This creates visual separation even when items were clustered during discussion.

### Step 7 — Reveal the Quadrants
Unlock and reveal the four quadrants. Half the items should fall above the center line and half below; same left to right. If the distribution is lopsided, consider whether items need adjustment.

**Why progressive reveal matters:** *"We don't reveal the quadrants at the start because it can bias people's perspectives. People would immediately start arguing that something is strategic or something is a quick win without having had the conversations."*

The quadrants:

| | Low Difficulty | High Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| **High Importance** | **Quick Wins** — Highest ROI. Start here. | **Strategic** — Important but hard. Stage these. Build toward them. |
| **Low Importance** | **Fill-ins** — Easy but less critical. Do if time allows. | **Deprioritize** — Hard and less important. Not now. |

### Step 8 — Sequence the Road Map
Start with items in the bottom-right (high importance, low difficulty) — highest ROI. Plot a suggested sequence:

- *"Perhaps if we start with career growth clarity, that enables a conversation around workload sustainability, then we pick up strategic clarity..."*
- Draw arrows or lines showing the suggested order
- Consider connections between items — sometimes a quick win enables a strategic initiative
- Each item is a potential business case. The strategic ones are bigger cases to be made, but they should still be made.

**The strategic quadrant trap:** The most common pitfall is that strategic items — important but difficult — never get addressed because the team keeps picking quick wins. Treat strategic items like a horizon model: stage work toward them deliberately while executing on quick wins. Draw a line from the quick wins up to the strategic items so the connection is visible.

### Step 9 — Capture Next Steps
Document the emergent plan. Note who will do what by when. Suggest getting feedback from others not in the room within the next week or two — the matrix reflects a moment in time and a specific group's perspective.

### Step 10 — Capture Feedback
Close with: *"We were trying to prioritize [X] and align on the most impactful things. Did we achieve that outcome? What did you like? What didn't you like? What would you suggest?"*

## Facilitation Notes

### Progressive Disclosure Is Non-Negotiable
The staged reveal — importance only, then difficulty, then quadrants, then sequencing — is core methodology, not a nice-to-have. It prevents anchoring bias and forces genuine trade-off discussion.

### Be the Group's Hands
The facilitator moves stickies on behalf of the group, but the group drives the discussion. *"I'm happy to be your hands here. But I want you to drive the discussion."*

### Separating Importance from Difficulty Is Hard
Even when participants know difficulty is coming next, it's hard to discuss importance in isolation. This is where facilitators need to be firm: *"We'll get to difficulty — right now let's focus on importance relative to [the definition]."*

### Keep Definitions Visible
Move them close to the active workspace. Participants lose track of what "importance" and "difficulty" mean in context, especially partway through. Keep them physically near the working area throughout.

### Know Which Hat You're Wearing
If you're the facilitator, you're neutral — you serve the process. If you're also a team member, you can challenge, but you must still allow the group to have the final say. You can't move a sticky and say "I think this should be here" — you ask the question and let the group decide.

### Managing Disagreement
- Ask the dissenter to state their position
- Ask others to respond
- If it's one position apart, invoke the one-position rule
- If it's a major disagreement, seek a third voice to break the deadlock
- "Disagree and commit" is a valid outcome — name it and move on

### Difficulty Is Often Unknown
Participants may not know the true difficulty of something. That's okay — the method captures *relative qualitative difficulty*, not precise estimates. The conversation about how the group is thinking about difficulty is itself valuable, even when exact numbers don't exist.

### Scope Ambiguity in Items
An item like "improve meeting effectiveness" could mean one meeting or every meeting across 15,000 people. When this surfaces, draw a range line on the board showing the spectrum. Help the group decide which scope they're prioritizing for.

### Timing
Timing is consistently the hardest thing to get right. With 8 items, plan for the importance discussion to take longer than you expect. Co-facilitator pairs can split the axes — one handles importance, one handles difficulty — which helps with pacing and energy.

### Co-Facilitation
Two facilitators work well for this method. Natural split: one facilitates importance ranking, the other facilitates difficulty ranking. Keep handoffs clean and brief.

## Variations

### Axis Label Variations
- **Impact / Effort** — Same structure, different labels. More common in agile/product contexts
- **Eisenhower Matrix** — Uses Important / Urgent instead of Important / Difficult. Different second dimension entirely — urgency is not difficulty
- Choose labels that resonate with your audience. The underlying mechanics are the same

### Scope Variations for Definitions
- More specific topics with expert groups → more specific definitions of both importance and difficulty
- Broad topics with diverse groups → simpler, more accessible definitions
- Definitions can be refined during the session as the group's understanding deepens

### Pairing with Other Methods
- **Bullseye Diagramming as a precursor:** When you have 20+ items, use bullseye to narrow to 8–10, then run the matrix on the survivors
- **Affinity Clustering as a precursor:** When items emerge from research synthesis, cluster first, then prioritize the clusters
- **Business case development as a follow-up:** Each sticky is a potential business case — the matrix output feeds directly into building those cases
- **Retrospectives as follow-up:** Evaluate whether prioritized actions were actually addressed

### In-Person vs. Digital
The method works in both settings. Digital tools (Miro, FigJam, Mural) offer alignment/distribution functions that help with visual clarity. In-person offers easier reading of body language and disagreement.

### Item Splitting
Sometimes during difficulty discussion, a participant realizes an item is actually two things with different difficulty profiles. This is valid — split it and re-place both. But manage the scope: don't let splitting multiply your items beyond what time allows.

### Drawing Connections
When participants notice relationships between items — one enables another, or two are linked — capture these as visible connection lines on the board. This adds a dependency/network layer to the standard matrix and enriches the sequencing conversation.

## Outputs

- **A prioritized 2×2 matrix** showing relative importance and difficulty of all items
- **An emergent road map** — a suggested sequence for tackling items, typically starting from quick wins and building toward strategic investments
- **Documented connections** between related items
- **Shared understanding** of the group's alignment and disagreements — often the most valuable output, though intangible
- **Next steps** with assigned ownership and timeline
- The matrix reflects a moment in time. It's a snapshot of a conversation as much as an artifact. Revisit it as context changes.

## Connections

### Within the Prioritization Progression
- **Simpler:** Visualize the Vote (dot voting), Bullseye Diagramming
- **Related:** Eisenhower Matrix (different second dimension), WSJF / Weighted Shortest Job First (quantitative approach from agile)

### In the Double Diamond
This method lives in the **Define** stage of the first diamond or the transition between diamonds — when a team has discovered possibilities and needs to decide which to pursue. It can also apply in the **Deliver** stage when choosing between validated solutions.

### Upstream Methods (inputs come from)
- Affinity Clustering — synthesized insights become the items to prioritize
- Research synthesis — survey results, interview findings, observation data
- Brainstorming / Concept Ideation — generated ideas become the items

### Downstream Methods (outputs feed into)
- Business case development
- Road mapping and sprint planning
- Decision-making frameworks
- Retrospectives (did we follow through?)
