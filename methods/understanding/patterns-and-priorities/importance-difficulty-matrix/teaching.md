# Importance / Difficulty Matrix — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever been in a meeting where everyone agrees that everything is important, but you leave without any clarity on what to do first? Where the loudest voice wins, or the team just picks whatever seems easiest without talking about whether it actually matters?"*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

Justin and David use this method every week to decide what they'll work on within What Could Be and how it ties back to client objectives. It's not just a workshop tool — it's a thinking tool for ongoing prioritization.

The method sits at the top of a prioritization progression — Visualize the Vote (simplest) → Bullseye Diagramming → Importance/Difficulty Matrix (most robust). What makes it different is the trade-off discussion across two dimensions. When you bring together stakeholders with different expertise — someone who really understands the difficulty of something versus a stakeholder who is really thinking about the importance — you begin to have those discussions of the relative lift of something in comparison to how important it is.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** 8–10 items that need prioritizing (from research, surveys, brainstorms, or prior synthesis), a group familiar with the subject matter, and agreed definitions of what "importance" and "difficulty" mean in this context.

**Outputs:** Items plotted on a 2×2 matrix, characterized as High ROI, Strategic Investments, Quick Wins, or Low Priority. An emergent road map with a suggested sequence. An agreed set of priorities. The beginning of an action plan.

**Connection forward:** The output feeds directly into business case development, road mapping, and sprint planning. Each sticky is a potential business case — the matrix helps you figure out which cases to make first.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full 10-step process and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**
- **Progressive reveal is non-negotiable** — importance axis only, then difficulty, then quadrants. This prevents bias.
- **First item goes in the middle** — always. It establishes a relative anchor.
- **The one-position rule** — "one position difference isn't going to affect this conversation too much." Keeps energy focused on meaningful differences.
- **Definitions before ranking** — agree on what importance and difficulty mean before placing anything.
- **The facilitator is the group's hands** — you move stickies on behalf of the group, but they drive the discussion.

**When teaching someone to facilitate this method:** Use the experiential loop (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`, Step 4). Demo the method with the learner as participant — run the progressive reveal, model the one-position rule, show how to handle disagreement. Then have them facilitate while you play a realistic participant. Debrief with roses/thorns/buds on their facilitation.

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Where could you use this in the next two weeks? Think about a situation where your team has competing priorities and no clear way to decide what comes first."*

**If the experiential loop was used:** *"What did you notice about the facilitation? What felt natural? What was harder than you expected? How would you handle that with your actual team?"*

Close with a commitment: *"Is there a commitment you're willing to make about applying this?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The method produces a conversation as much as an artifact. The discussion about relative importance and relative difficulty — the moments of disagreement, the trade-offs surfaced, the connections discovered — that's where the real value lives. The matrix documents it. But the conversation is the work.

---

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- 45–60 minutes
- A 2×2 workspace (digital or physical) with the y-axis hidden until needed
- 8–12 pre-populated items on sticky notes
- Written definitions of importance and difficulty
- 4–8 participants with relevant expertise
