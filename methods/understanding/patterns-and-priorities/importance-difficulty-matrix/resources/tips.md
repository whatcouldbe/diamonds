# Importance / Difficulty Matrix — Tips & Advice

Collected practitioner wisdom for running the I/D Matrix well. These are the things that consistently trip facilitators up and the moves that make the difference between a good session and a great one.

---

## Setup

**8–10 items is the sweet spot. Defend it.**
More than 10 items and the session bogs down — the relative comparisons lose nuance and time runs out. If a group hands you 20 items, don't try to run the matrix on all of them. Use Bullseye Diagramming first to narrow to 8–10, then run the matrix on the survivors.

**Write definitions before the session, not during.**
Defining importance and difficulty in the room takes longer than expected and can derail the session before it starts. Draft your working definitions in advance, bring them in, and present them for refinement rather than creation. The conversation to refine a definition is valuable. The conversation to write one from scratch is often exhausting.

**Hide the quadrant grid.**
The 2×2 grid must be hidden at the start. If participants see the quadrants before they've done the ranking, they'll immediately start arguing about which quadrant things belong in ("that's clearly a quick win!") without having had the actual trade-off conversation. Progressive disclosure is non-negotiable. Reveal importance only → then difficulty → then the grid.

---

## During the Session

**Be the group's hands, not their voice.**
The facilitator moves stickies on behalf of the group. The group drives the discussion. Never say "I think this should be here" — ask the question and let the group decide. Your neutrality is the thing that makes the session credible.

**Keep definitions visible and physically close to the working area.**
Participants lose track of what "importance" and "difficulty" mean in context, especially partway through a long ranking conversation. Move the definition sticky notes close to the active workspace. When discussions get abstract, point to the definition: "Let's check that against our definition of importance."

**Separating importance from difficulty is harder than it looks.**
Even experienced participants find it hard to assess importance without thinking about difficulty. When someone says "but this will be really hard to do," hold the line: "We'll get to difficulty — right now let's focus on importance relative to our definition." Be warm but firm. This is the most common facilitation challenge with this method.

**Use the one-position rule to break stalemates.**
When debate stalls over a minor positional difference, invoke it: "One position difference isn't going to change this conversation too much. If someone thinks it should move more than one space, let's talk about it." This gives permission to move forward without making anyone feel dismissed. It also surfaces when a disagreement is genuinely significant (when someone *does* think it should move more than one position).

**Remind people it's relative.**
When participants resist placing something low on importance, remind them: "They're all important. It's just relative to one another." This reframe reduces the emotional charge of placing something lower on the scale.

**Draw connection lines.**
When participants notice that two items are linked — one enables the other, or the difficulty of one depends on solving the other — capture that relationship visibly on the board. These connections often matter more for sequencing than the quadrant placement itself.

## The Strategic Quadrant Trap

The most common post-session failure: strategic items (high importance, high difficulty) never get addressed because the team keeps picking quick wins.

When sequencing the road map, draw a visible line from the quick wins up to the strategic items. Make the connection explicit: "These quick wins are not the end state — they're building toward this." Treat strategic items like a horizon model: stage work toward them deliberately while executing on quick wins. Without this, teams default to only ever doing the easy things.

## Facilitation Dynamics

**Know which hat you're wearing.**
If you're the facilitator and also a team member, you need to decide: are you serving the process or contributing content? You can't fully do both. If you have a strong opinion about where something should land, either share it before the session (when you're a participant, not a facilitator) or hold it until the sequencing conversation at the end. Moving a sticky and saying "I think this should be here" breaks your neutrality and undermines the group's ownership of the output.

**Co-facilitation works well for this method.**
The natural split: one facilitator handles importance ranking, the other handles difficulty ranking. This helps with pacing and energy, especially in longer sessions. Keep handoffs clean and brief.

**Timing is the hardest thing to get right.**
Importance discussions consistently take longer than expected. With 8 items, budget more time than you think you need. If you're running tight, it's better to rush the sequencing conversation than to rush the ranking conversations — the ranking is where the real value is.

**Call on specific people.**
Don't just ask open questions and wait. Call on people who are nodding, frowning, or haven't spoken. "I see you nodding — what are you thinking?" This activates quieter participants and surfaces disagreements that might otherwise stay hidden.
