# Importance / Difficulty Matrix — FAQ

Questions that come up repeatedly when teams learn or run the I/D Matrix for the first time.

---

**What if we have more than 10 items?**
Use Bullseye Diagramming first to narrow to 8–10, then run the matrix on the survivors. More than 10 items and the relative comparison conversations lose nuance — you end up with too many items to meaningfully differentiate, and sessions run long. If Bullseye isn't available, ask the group to quickly vote on which items feel most important to include before starting the matrix.

**What if participants don't know the true difficulty of something?**
That's okay. The method captures relative qualitative difficulty, not precise estimates. The conversation about how the group is thinking about difficulty is itself valuable — it surfaces different mental models and assumptions. Encourage participants to share their uncertainty: "I don't know exactly how hard this is, but it feels harder than X because..." That kind of reasoning is useful even without precise data.

**Why hide the quadrants at the start?**
Because seeing the quadrants creates anchoring bias. Participants would immediately start arguing about which quadrant things belong in ("that's clearly a quick win!") without having had the actual trade-off conversation about importance and difficulty. The progressive reveal forces genuine deliberation. The quadrants are a synthesis of that deliberation — not a starting point for it.

**What if the distribution is lopsided after the reveal?**
If most items land in one quadrant, the ranking conversations may have compressed too much. Consider whether items need adjustment. A healthy matrix has roughly equal distribution across quadrants — half above and half below the center line on each axis. Significant lopsidedness is a signal to revisit.

**Can I do this online?**
Yes. Miro, FigJam, and Mural all work well. Digital tools offer alignment and distribution functions that help create visual clarity when items are clustered — you can spread them evenly along an axis with one click. Use color-coded sticky notes for items. Keep the quadrant grid on a hidden layer and reveal it at the right moment. Digital boards also produce a built-in record of the session.

**What if people disagree strongly about where something belongs?**
That disagreement is often the most valuable thing in the room. It surfaces different mental models, different definitions of importance or difficulty, or different information that participants hold. Steps: ask the dissenter to state their position, ask others to respond, seek a third voice if needed. If it's one position apart, invoke the one-position rule. If it's a major disagreement, name it explicitly — "we have a significant difference of perspective here" — and either resolve it or "disagree and commit" and move on.

**What's the difference between Importance/Difficulty and the Eisenhower Matrix?**
The Eisenhower Matrix uses Important / Urgent rather than Important / Difficult. Urgency is not difficulty — something can be urgent and easy, or important and not urgent. The I/D Matrix is more useful for strategic planning and road mapping; the Eisenhower Matrix is more useful for personal task management. Choose based on whether difficulty (how hard to accomplish) or urgency (how soon it needs to happen) is the more relevant second dimension.

**Can items be split during the session?**
Yes. Sometimes during the difficulty discussion, a participant realizes an item is actually two things with different difficulty profiles. Splitting is valid — remove the original item and place both new items. But manage scope: don't let splitting multiply your items beyond what time allows. One or two splits is fine; five splits means the items weren't well-defined to begin with.

**What if the team has fewer than 4 people?**
The method still works but loses some of its diversity of perspective. With very small groups, consider whether you have enough range of viewpoint — someone who understands difficulty alongside someone thinking about importance. If not, consider who else might join even briefly for the ranking conversations.

**How often should we revisit the matrix?**
The matrix reflects a moment in time and a specific group's perspective. Revisit it as context changes — when new information surfaces, when scope shifts, or when you're planning the next cycle of work. It's a snapshot, not a permanent plan.
