# Abstraction Laddering

A structured method for aligning a team on the right level of abstraction for a challenge before investing in solutions. Starting from a given directive, the team asks "Why?" to move up toward goals and benefits, and "How?" to move down toward tactics and approaches — then chooses the framing that best positions the work.

---

## Purpose

Use when a team has been given a directive and needs to make sure they're working at the right level before committing to a direction. Abstraction laddering reveals whether the stated opportunity is too tactical (constraining), too vague (not actionable), or misaligned with the actual goals it should serve.

**The core question:** Are we framing this challenge at the right level — specific enough to act on, broad enough to find the best solution?

**Why abstraction laddering matters:**
- Teams are wired to jump straight to solutions — and organizations reward that instinct
- A directive handed down by a sponsor is often just one framing of the challenge, not the only valid one
- Getting to the goals behind the directive opens up better solutions and builds team buy-in
- Establishing scope and direction up front prevents "great landing, wrong airport" — arriving at the end of a project only to realize the team solved the wrong thing

**What makes abstraction laddering different from just asking "why?":**
- The ladder provides a visual structure that makes levels of abstraction tangible
- Individual generation before group discussion ensures all voices are in the room
- The language starters ("In order to...", "Enabled by...") do active work — they hold responses at the right level and prevent premature solution-jumping
- The team ends with an explicit decision, not just a discussion

**Where it sits in the process:**
Abstraction laddering is used at two common entry points: (1) at the start of a project, when a team has been handed a directive and needs to align on scope before work begins; and (2) after research, when a team is framing an opportunity from what they've learned. In both cases, the goal is the same — make sure the challenge is framed at the right altitude before the team invests in solving it.

## Setup

### Participants
- **Facilitator:** Guides the session through the steps, holds the structure, and manages the level-selection discussion at the end
- **Team members:** The people who will work on the challenge — generate WHYs and HOWs individually, then share and cluster together
- **Optional: Sponsor or project owner:** Valuable to include if available; their WHYs often reveal the real goals behind the directive

### What You Need
- **A directive** — the starting opportunity, initiative, or brief ("We need to...")
- **A ladder template** — pre-drawn with the directive in the center, space above for WHYs, space below for HOWs (Miro or printed)
- **Yellow sticky notes** — for WHYs (goals and benefits)
- **Purple or blue sticky notes** — for HOWs (approaches and tactics)
- **Markers**

### Time
- 45–60 minutes for a group session
- 15–20 minutes for a solo version

---

## Steps

### Step 1 — Frame the Directive
Place the directive in the center of the ladder. Frame it as "We need to..." — present tense, action-oriented, specific enough to respond to.

This is the starting point for both directions. Everything above it asks why; everything below it asks how.

### Step 2 — Individual WHY Generation
*"Why are we doing this?"*

Each person generates individual responses using the starters **"In order to..."** or **"So that..."** — one answer per sticky note. 5–10 minutes, silent and individual.

Yellow stickies. These responses move up the ladder toward goals, benefits, and purpose.

**The starters are doing real work:** "In order to..." and "So that..." naturally push responses toward outcomes rather than activities. They keep the team from restating the directive in different words.

### Step 3 — Individual HOW Generation
*"How might we do this?"*

Each person generates individual responses using the starters **"Enabled by..."** or **"By considering..."** — one answer per sticky note. 5–10 minutes, silent and individual.

Purple or blue stickies. These responses move down the ladder toward tactics, approaches, and methods.

**The starters are doing real work here too:** "Enabled by..." holds responses at the right level — it describes an approach, not a specific solution. "By [specific thing]" tends to drop too quickly into implementation. Redirect when this happens.

### Step 4 — Share and Cluster
One person describes their item and places it on the ladder. Others place similar items nearby. Continue until all stickies are placed. Discuss and rearrange as groupings emerge. Label the clusters that take shape.

This is the first time the team sees the full landscape of WHYs and HOWs together. Themes will emerge — and surprises. It's common to discover that the team had very different assumptions about what the project was really for.

### Step 5 — Choose the Level
*"Which framing is most useful for what we need to do next?"*

The team discusses the ladder and selects the framing that best positions the work going forward. This is the most important step — don't skip it or rush it.

**What to look for:**
- **Too high:** Goals that are real but too abstract to act on ("improve human wellbeing"). These are useful for context, not for scoping.
- **Too low:** Tactics that are actionable but too constrained — they assume a solution before the team has looked. Working at this level closes off better options.
- **Right altitude:** A framing that is clear enough to guide the work, broad enough to leave room for the best solution, and grounded in something the team genuinely agrees is the goal.

The chosen framing becomes the working definition of the challenge going forward.

## Facilitation Notes

### Watch for "Because" — It Signals Root Cause Drift
If a WHY answer starts with "because," the person has shifted into root cause analysis — diagnosing why a problem exists rather than articulating what solving it would make possible. These are fundamentally different lines of thinking, and the methods that serve them are different too.

When it happens, redirect: *"That's describing why the problem exists — which is worth exploring. But in this method, we're asking what solving it would make possible. Can you reframe that as a goal or benefit?"* If the team genuinely needs to understand root causes, name it: *"It sounds like we might need to spend time on why this problem is happening before we frame the opportunity. That's a different activity — problem tree analysis is built for it."*

The starters — "In order to..." and "So that..." — are your first line of defense here. They structurally prevent "because" answers. Enforce them consistently.

### The Level-Selection Discussion Is the Method
Everything before it is generating material. The real value comes from the team looking at the full ladder together and choosing a level. Don't let the session end without an explicit decision. If the team can't agree, that disagreement is the insight — surface it, name it, and decide how to proceed.

### Watch the Language
The starters are not suggestions — they're guardrails. If a WHY doesn't begin with "In order to..." or "So that...", ask the person to reframe it. If a HOW drops into a specific product or solution, redirect: "Can you say that as an approach rather than a specific answer?" The discipline of staying in the right register is what makes the method work.

### Individual Generation First
Always generate individually before sharing. This protects quieter participants, prevents anchoring (the first speaker doesn't set the frame for everyone), and ensures the team sees genuine diversity of perspective. Skip this step and you'll get groupthink — one or two voices and a lot of nodding.

### It's Okay to Reframe the Directive — And It's Okay Not To
If the level-selection discussion reveals that the original directive was framed at the wrong altitude — too tactical, too vague, or pointed at the wrong goal — that's a successful outcome. The team has just learned something critical before investing in the wrong thing. Name it as a win.

But the team may also end up right where they started — and that's equally valid. The method doesn't mandate a reframe. Sometimes the original framing was correct, and the ladder confirms it. What the team gains is everything around it: the WHYs become the narrative for marketing and communicating the initiative; the HOWs point to quick wins and early approaches. Armed with that context, the team can move forward with more confidence and a richer story to tell.

### The Sponsor's WHYs Are Gold
If a project sponsor or stakeholder is in the room, their WHYs often reveal the unstated goals behind the directive — the things they care about that never made it into the brief. Ask them to generate WHYs individually alongside the team. The gaps between their responses and the team's are frequently the most important thing in the room.

## Variations

### AI-Assisted Generation
Use the structured AI prompt (see `resources/ai-usage.md`) to generate WHY and HOW ladders from the directive. Use the output as a starting point for team discussion rather than individual sticky generation. Good for solo preparation or for kickstarting a session when a team is stuck.

### WHYs Only — When You're Starting Tactical
If the team has been handed a very specific, highly tactical challenge, go up the ladder only. Ask WHY questions to surface the goals and benefits behind the directive. This is especially useful when the team suspects they're in the weeds and needs to evaluate whether this is the right thing to work on — the WHYs give them the criteria to assess it against other options.

### HOWs Only — When You're Starting Broad
If the team is facing a large, ambitious directive — the kind of organization that's always trying to boil the ocean — go down the ladder only. Ask HOW questions to break the challenge into manageable approaches and identify quick wins. The HOWs are the "one bite at a time" — a way to make a very large initiative actionable without losing sight of the ambition.

### Solo
Done individually to prepare a brief, proposal, or project plan. Follow the same steps — generate WHYs and HOWs, then choose the level. Writing forces clarity. Useful before a stakeholder conversation when you want to be sure you're framing the challenge well.

### Extended Ladder
Ladder multiple levels up and down to explore more abstract goals or more concrete tactics. Useful when the team is genuinely unsure of the right altitude and needs to see a wider range of framings before deciding.

---

## Outputs

- **A mapped ladder** — WHYs (goals and benefits) above the directive, HOWs (approaches and tactics) below, clustered and labeled
- **A chosen framing** — the statement the team agrees best represents the real challenge at the right level of abstraction
- **Shared vocabulary** — the language of goals and approaches, established early and carried into the project

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
Abstraction laddering lives in **Understanding / Problem Framing**. It is most often used at the boundary between Discover and Define — when the team has gathered initial context and needs to frame the challenge before investing in research or solutions. It can also be used earlier (to frame the scope of a discovery effort) or after research (to frame an opportunity from findings).

### Upstream Methods (abstraction laddering receives from)
- A stakeholder brief, project directive, or design challenge
- Interviewing or other research methods — when laddering an opportunity derived from research findings
- Any prior work that has produced a problem or opportunity statement worth reframing

### Downstream Methods (abstraction laddering feeds into)
- **How Might We** — the chosen framing often translates directly into HMW questions
- **Creative Matrix** — the goals surfaced by WHYs and the approaches surfaced by HOWs provide axes for ideation
- **Importance/Difficulty Matrix** — HOWs can be evaluated for feasibility and priority
- Research planning — the chosen framing scopes and focuses what the team sets out to learn

### Related Methods
- **How Might We** — also a reframing method, but focused on converting insights into design challenges. Abstraction laddering comes before HMW; HMW typically follows.
- **Rose/Thorn/Bud** — evaluative rather than framing; surfaces what's working, what's not, and what's emerging. Different purpose, but sometimes used together at project kickoff.
