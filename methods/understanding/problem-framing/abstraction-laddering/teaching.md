# Abstraction Laddering — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"As humans, we're just wired to jump into solution mode. Part of this is because our organizations and leaders expect action. You're given a go-and-do-this type of project, and we're constantly encouraged — and even rewarded — for answers, and too rarely for questions.*

*Because here's the thing: excellent solutions require excellent understanding of a problem. Einstein said it well: 'If I had an hour to solve a problem, I'd spend 55 minutes defining the problem and 5 minutes on the solution.' At the start of any project, it's well worthwhile to use 55 minutes to align on the problem to be solved — before anyone starts building anything."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

The method is called abstraction laddering — which sounds complicated, but the structure is simple.

Picture a ladder drawn on a page. Clouds at the top — goals, purposes, the big "why." Grass at the bottom — tactics, specific solutions, the "how." The opportunity, directive, or challenge you're working on sits in the middle.

Most teams start in the weeds — they start at the grass. They've been handed a brief and they start building. The ladder gives you a structured way to climb up and look around before committing to where you're going.

Going up, you ask: *"Why are we doing this?"* The language starters — *"In order to..."* and *"So that..."* — help you get there. Each time you answer, you can ladder up again: *"And why does that matter?"* You might start with "we need to get more sleep" and find, three levels up, that what you're really after is better compensation. That's useful to know before you start optimizing your sleep habits.

Going down, you ask: *"How might we do this?"* The starters — *"Enabled by..."* and *"By considering..."* — hold you at the right level of approach without collapsing into specific solutions too early. "Enabled by better sleep habits" is more useful than "by going to bed at 9pm" — it leaves room to explore.

The result: you end up with a visible landscape of goals above the directive and approaches below it. Then — and this is the most important step — the team chooses the level. Not the highest, not the lowest. The level that's actually useful: clear enough to direct the work, broad enough to find the best solution.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A directive or opportunity statement ("We need to..."), a team with relevant perspective on the challenge, yellow stickies (WHYs), purple or blue stickies (HOWs), and a pre-drawn ladder template.

**Outputs:** A mapped ladder with WHYs and HOWs clustered and labeled, a chosen framing the team has agreed on, and shared vocabulary for goals and approaches to carry into the project.

**Connection forward:** The chosen framing often translates directly into a How Might We statement — the bridge from problem to design challenge. The WHYs become the implicit success criteria. The HOWs become initial hypotheses.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full steps and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **The starters are guardrails, not suggestions.** "In order to..." and "So that..." hold WHYs at the outcome level. "Enabled by..." holds HOWs at the approach level. Enforce them — if someone's WHY restates the directive in different words, ask them to reframe it.
- **Individual generation first.** Always. This prevents anchoring and ensures genuine diversity of perspective before the group starts influencing each other.
- **The level-selection discussion is the whole method.** Everything before it is generating material. The value comes from the team looking at the landscape together and choosing a level. Don't let the session end without an explicit decision.
- **Discovering the wrong framing is a success.** If the team realizes mid-session that the original directive was pointed at the wrong goal, name it as a win. They just saved themselves from investing in the wrong thing.

**When teaching someone to facilitate abstraction laddering:** Run it live with a real directive — ideally one the learners are actually working on. Debrief on what surprised them. What WHYs came up that weren't on anyone's radar? What level did they choose, and why not higher or lower? Then practice the redirect: what do you say when someone's HOW collapses into a specific solution?

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about a project you're working on right now — or one you've recently finished. Was the challenge framed at the right level when you started? If you'd done this at the beginning, what might you have discovered?"*

*"Where in your current work could a 45-minute session like this save you from going in the wrong direction? What's the directive that most needs to be interrogated?"*

**If the activity was run live:** *"What level did your team choose, and why not one level higher or lower? What surprised you about the WHYs that came up? Did anyone's responses shift your understanding of what the project is really for?"*

Close with a commitment: *"Is there a directive or brief you could run this on in the next two weeks — before work begins?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

Abstraction laddering is a 45-minute investment that protects everything that comes after it. The alternative — starting with the directive and building — is faster until it isn't. Because at some point, someone will look at what the team has built and say something you never want to hear at the end of a project:

*"Great landing. Wrong airport."*

The ladder doesn't guarantee you'll build the right thing. But it makes sure you've at least agreed on where you're going before you get on the plane.
