# Rose, Thorn, Bud

A codification method for organizing observations, feedback, and research notes into three categories — what's working, what's not, and what has potential — to surface patterns and prepare data for synthesis.

---

## Purpose

Use when you have a body of data — observations, interview notes, workshop feedback, research findings — that needs to be organized before it can be analyzed. Rose, Thorn, Bud converts raw, unstructured information into a consistent format that makes patterns visible and prepares the data for clustering and synthesis.

**The core question:** What's working, what isn't, and what has potential?

**What makes this different from unstructured note-taking:**
- Unstructured notes reflect what captured the observer's attention — they don't systematically surface the full picture
- RTB forces three lenses: positive, negative, and potential. This ensures the data doesn't skew toward what was most dramatic or most recent
- The color coding creates a visual heat map that makes the distribution of sentiment immediately readable — useful in affinity clustering and prioritization

**Where it sits in the synthesis progression:**
Research / Observation → Rose, Thorn, Bud (codification) → Affinity Clustering (synthesis) → Prioritization → Framing (How Might We)

---

## The Three Categories

| Color | Name | Meaning | Alternative names |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pink | Rose | Positive — something working, something in full bloom | Strength, warm feedback, I like..., what worked well? |
| Blue | Thorn | Negative — something challenging or painful, like pricking your finger | Weakness, cool feedback, I dislike/worry..., what didn't work? |
| Green | Bud | Has potential — something that with care and attention could become a rose | Opportunity, feed-forward suggestion, I wish..., what should we try/do differently? |

The botanical metaphor is intentional: a rose is in full bloom, a thorn is painful, and a bud could become a rose with the right conditions. The metaphor helps people hold the three categories intuitively.

## Setup

### Participants
- Works as an **individual activity** — one person codifying their own observations or notes
- Works as a **group activity** — multiple people contributing perspectives in a working session
- Can be done **asynchronously** — team members add items independently before a shared review

### Data sources
- Research notes from interviews or contextual inquiry
- Observations from a user session or field visit
- Feedback from a workshop or event
- Perspectives on a product, service, process, or experience
- Personal reflection (e.g., end of day, onboarding, performance review preparation)

### Materials
- Sticky notes in three colors: **pink** (rose), **blue** (thorn), **green** (bud)
- One item per sticky note
- A board or wall to place them (physical or digital)

**On color choice:** The default colors — pink, blue, green — matter. Pink avoids the red-means-danger association that can bias what people write. If you change the color scheme, make sure every participant understands the new mapping and apply it consistently throughout. Color carries meaning in this method; inconsistency undermines the heat map.

### Time
- As a quick workshop activity: **~10 minutes** (2–3 minutes per color)
- As a research codification exercise: variable — depends on the volume of data
- A good dataset for moving into affinity clustering: **20–30 items** total

## Steps

### Step 1 — Frame the Purpose
Name what you're codifying and why. Is this research data from interviews? Feedback from a workshop? Personal reflection at the end of a project? The context helps participants direct their observations appropriately.

Introduce the three categories and their colors. Show the visual if available. Explain the alternative naming conventions if any of them resonate better with this group or context.

### Step 2 — One Item Per Note
Each observation, quote, or thought goes on its own sticky note. This granularity matters — it's what makes the data useful in affinity clustering later. One item per note means one idea can travel to the cluster where it belongs, rather than being stranded with other ideas it doesn't share affinity with.

Write enough on each note that someone who wasn't present could understand it without explanation. One or two words is not enough. A fragment of context, a brief quote, or a short sentence is the right level. The data should be able to speak for itself.

### Step 3 — Codify
Assign each item to a rose, thorn, or bud based on what it represents:
- **Rose (pink):** This was working. Something positive was observed or said.
- **Thorn (blue):** This was a challenge. Something negative was observed or said.
- **Bud (green):** This has potential. An opportunity, idea, or suggestion emerged.

Record items as they were intended — what the person said or what was observed, not your interpretation of it. Quotes from participants are particularly valuable. If you observed a non-verbal signal alongside a statement (a smile, hesitation, visible frustration), note it.

If you're not sure whether an item is a rose or a thorn, record what was actually said or observed and let the context carry the meaning. The person speaking or the situation being observed has already categorized it — your job is to capture that faithfully.

### Step 4 — Aim for Balance, Accept Reality
In a workshop setting, encourage participants to generate items across all three colors. A prompt of two or three items per color is a useful starting point when time is limited.

If one color dominates, that's fine — it reflects the reality of the situation. A particularly challenging stage of an experience will naturally produce more thorns. Don't force balance where the data doesn't support it.

If someone can't think of anything for one color, they move on. There's no obligation to generate items in every category.

### Step 5 — Place and Review
Once items are generated, place them on the board. In a group setting, this is also the moment to read items aloud — it builds shared understanding of what's been captured and surfaces any items that need clarification.

If the same observation appears across multiple people's notes — whether as the same color or different colors — that's significant. Repetition signals a pattern. Divergence signals a point worth exploring.

## Facilitation Notes

### Record as Intended, Not as Interpreted
The most important discipline in RTB is recording what was observed or said, not what the observer thinks it means. Analysis comes later — in affinity clustering and synthesis. At this stage, the job is faithful capture.

When someone asks whether an item is a rose or a thorn, redirect to what was observed: *"What did the person say? What did you see?"* The answer to that question usually resolves the categorization.

### One Person's Thorn Is Another's Bud
The same observation can legitimately appear on different colors for different people — and that divergence is itself a finding. *"It was quick"* might be a rose for someone who valued efficiency and a thorn for someone who felt rushed. Both are valid. Record each as it was intended, and let the clustering process surface the divergence.

### Write Enough to Be Understood Without You
A sticky note that says "confused" tells you nothing when you return to it in a clustering session. A note that says *"participant couldn't find the next step button — spent 45 seconds searching"* tells you something. The bar: if you weren't in the room, could you still understand what this item means?

### Embed It as a Feedback Frame
RTB works well as a built-in feedback structure at the end of a session. Rather than asking "what did you think?", give people colored sticky notes and 10 minutes. The structure lowers the barrier to giving feedback and produces data that can actually be analyzed.

### It's Not About Argument
If two people categorize the same observation differently, don't turn it into a debate about which color is correct. Record both. The divergence is information — it means the observation is genuinely ambiguous or that it's being experienced differently by different people.

## Variations

### As a Research Codification Tool
Used after interviews or contextual inquiry to organize raw notes. Each note from the session gets assigned to a rose, thorn, or bud — converting unstructured fieldwork data into a structured dataset ready for affinity clustering.

### As a Workshop Feedback Tool
Used at the end of a session to collect participant perspectives. Gives everyone a voice and produces structured feedback the facilitator can analyze rather than just a verbal debrief that's hard to act on.

### As an Onboarding Check-In
Used at the end of a first day or first week to structure a new joiner's reflections. Prompts like *"two things that went well, two things that were challenging, two things you wish you had"* make the conversation concrete and bidirectional.

### As a Performance Reflection Tool
Used before a performance review conversation to help someone organize their own perspective — what's working, what isn't, what they'd like to develop. Converts a potentially abstract conversation into a structured exchange.

### Embedded Within Critique
RTB can be used as the feedback structure within a Critique session — participants assign colors to their feedback as they give it, making the sentiment of each piece of feedback immediately visible and sortable.

### Alternative Naming Conventions
The names and colors can be adapted for the audience without changing the method:
- **Strength / Weakness / Opportunity** — familiar to business audiences
- **Warm feedback / Cool feedback / Feed-forward suggestion** — softer framing for sensitive contexts
- **I like / I dislike or worry / I wish** — personal, direct framing for individual reflection
- **What worked well / What didn't work / What should we try differently** — retrospective framing for team reviews

## Outputs

- **A color-coded set of items** — observations, quotes, and notes organized by positive, negative, and potential
- **A visual heat map** — the distribution of colors across the board shows at a glance where things are working, where they're challenging, and where opportunities lie
- **A structured dataset** — ready to move directly into affinity clustering or other synthesis methods
- **Shared understanding** — in a group setting, the act of placing and reading items aloud builds collective awareness of what the data contains

## Connections

### In the Synthesis Progression
- **Upstream:** Interviewing, Contextual Inquiry, What's on Your Radar, observation, workshop facilitation
- **Downstream:** Affinity Clustering, Importance/Difficulty Matrix, Bullseye Diagramming

### In the Double Diamond
Rose, Thorn, Bud sits at the boundary between Discover and Define — it is the codification step that converts raw research into structured data, ready for the synthesis work that defines insights and themes.

### Natural Sequences
- **Before Affinity Clustering:** RTB codification produces the color-coded items that become the input to clustering. The heat map from RTB then persists through the clustering session, helping orient prioritization
- **Within Critique:** RTB provides the feedback structure — warm, cool, and feed-forward — for a Critique session
- **Before Importance/Difficulty Matrix:** When cluster titles emerge from an RTB-informed clustering session, the matrix is the natural next step for deciding which insights to act on
