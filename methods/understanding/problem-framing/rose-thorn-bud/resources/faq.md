# Rose, Thorn, Bud — FAQ

Questions that come up in practice. Grows over time as practitioners contribute from real sessions.

---

**One person's thorn seems like another person's bud. How do you handle that?**

It happens, and it's valid. Record each item as it was intended by the person who said it, wrote it, or experienced it. If the same observation genuinely appears as different colors for different people, record both — that divergence is a finding. It means the observation is being experienced differently, which is often more interesting than consensus.

---

**Does it matter which color you start with?**

Yes — the colors matter and consistency is important. The default is pink (rose), blue (thorn), green (bud). Avoid red for thorns: red triggers danger associations that can bias what people write or make them self-censor. People sometimes confuse a pink/blue/green scheme with a red/blue/green scheme and bring assumptions about what red means. If you change the colors for any reason, make sure every participant understands the new mapping and apply it throughout. Color carries meaning in this method.

---

**What if someone can't think of anything for one color?**

That's fine — they move on. There's no obligation to generate items in every category. RTB is also sometimes embedded within a Critique session as the feedback structure, where the same principle applies: generate what you genuinely have to say in each category, and don't force it.

---

**How specific should observations be?**

Specific enough that someone who wasn't present could understand the note without your explanation. Quotes from participants are ideal. If you observed a non-verbal signal — a smile, hesitation, visible frustration — note it alongside what was said. The bar: a word or two is not enough; a brief, self-contained observation is. The note needs to speak for itself in a clustering session, potentially days later.

---

**What do you do with the output once you've generated it?**

Typically it feeds directly into affinity clustering. The color-coded items become the dataset for clustering, and the colors persist through the session as a heat map — showing which clusters are predominantly positive, challenging, or full of opportunity. That heat map then helps orient prioritization.

---

**Is it okay if one color dominates?**

Yes — it reflects the reality of what's being reviewed. If you're observing a particularly challenging part of an experience, most items will be thorns. If you're reviewing something that's working well, most will be roses. Don't impose artificial balance. The distribution is data.

---

**What if people disagree about whether something is a rose or a thorn?**

RTB isn't really a method where disagreement about color should arise — because you're recording what was observed or said, not interpreting it. If there's confusion, redirect to the observation: *"What did the person say? What did you see?"* That usually resolves it. If one data point is categorized differently from all the others, you might gently note the discrepancy. If the group is genuinely split, that divergence is worth a closer look — but it doesn't need to be resolved in the RTB session itself.

---

**Can you use RTB on things other than workshops or research sessions?**

Yes — it's a flexible method that works anywhere you have observations to organize. Common uses beyond research and workshops: end-of-day reflection during onboarding, preparation for a performance review conversation, feedback on a piece of work in progress, team retrospectives. Any situation where you want to look at something through three lenses — what's working, what isn't, what has potential — is a valid use.

---

**How many items per color should people aim for?**

In a workshop setting, two or three per color is a useful starting point when time is limited — it gives people a concrete target without being prescriptive. For moving into affinity clustering, a dataset of 20–30 items total is a good working volume. In a research codification context, the number will be driven by the volume of data rather than a target.

---

**Can the same observation appear in more than one color?**

Yes. The same words can legitimately appear on different colors for different people. "It was quick" might be a rose for someone who valued efficiency and a thorn for someone who felt rushed. Both are valid recordings of genuine reactions. This is also why writing enough context on each note matters — "it was quick" with no further detail is ambiguous; "participant said it was quick but seemed unsure they'd done it right" is not.
