# Rose, Thorn, Bud — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Have you ever collected a load of research or feedback and then sat there wondering where to even start? You've got notes everywhere — some things people said, some things you observed, some half-formed ideas that came up. And none of it is in a form that you can really share or work with. You need a way to organize it before you can make sense of it."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

Rose, Thorn, Bud originally comes from the Boy Scouts — a simple daily reflection practice. A rose: something good that happened today. A thorn: something that was hard. A bud: something you're looking forward to.

We've borrowed that structure because it travels. It works in research codification, in workshop feedback, in onboarding, in performance conversations. Anywhere you have a body of observations that need organizing before they can be analyzed.

The metaphor does a lot of the work. A rose is in full bloom — something thriving, something working. A thorn is painful — like pricking your finger. And a bud could become a rose with a little care and sunlight. Once people get the metaphor, they hold the three categories intuitively.

We use it at the end of workshops: what did participants like, what did they find challenging, what do they wish they had? We use it to codify interview notes before affinity clustering. We've used it in onboarding: end of day one, two things that went well, two things that were hard, two things you wish you had. It's that flexible.

And when you combine it with affinity clustering, the color coding becomes a heat map — you can see at a glance which clusters are predominantly positive, predominantly challenging, and where the opportunities concentrate. That's where it really earns its place.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** A body of data to codify — interview notes, observations, workshop feedback, personal reflections. Sticky notes in three colors: pink (rose), blue (thorn), green (bud). Participants with first-hand experience of what's being reviewed, or notes from those who do.

**Outputs:** A color-coded set of items — one observation per note, written clearly enough that someone who wasn't there could understand it. A visual heat map showing the distribution of positive, negative, and potential observations. A structured dataset ready for affinity clustering.

**Connection forward:** The output feeds directly into affinity clustering. The colors persist through clustering, turning the board into a heat map that helps orient prioritization — clusters heavy in thorns and buds are often where the design work lives.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full process and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**
- **One item per note** — granularity is what makes the data useful in clustering. Items that contain multiple observations can't be cleanly separated later.
- **Write enough to be understood without you** — one or two words isn't enough. A fragment of context, a brief quote, or a short sentence is the right level.
- **Record as intended, not as interpreted** — capture what was said or observed, not what you think it means. Analysis comes later.
- **Color matters** — stick with pink, blue, green. Avoid red (danger associations). If you change the scheme, apply it consistently.
- **Balance is a prompt, not a rule** — encourage items across all three colors, but if one dominates, that's the data.

**The alternative names:** Show the visual (`resources/visuals/rtb-color-guide.png`). The table of naming variants — strength/weakness/opportunity, warm/cool/feed-forward, I like/I dislike/I wish — is often what makes the method click for a new audience. They've been doing this under a different name and don't know it yet.

---

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Where in your current work do you have a body of observations that hasn't been organized yet? What would it take to run a Rose, Thorn, Bud session on it this week — even just by yourself?"*

Close with a commitment: *"Is there a piece of data you're sitting on that this could help you make sense of?"*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The method is simple. That's the point. It doesn't require expertise or facilitation skill to run — it requires a few sticky notes in three colors and a willingness to look at data through three lenses rather than one. Most people only ever look at what went wrong. RTB insists that you also look at what's working and what has potential. That discipline — looking at all three — is what keeps the data honest and the synthesis useful.
