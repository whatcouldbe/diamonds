# Statement Starters

A method for converting research insights and problem statements into design challenge questions that invite broad, ambitious solution thinking. The most commonly used form is the "How Might We" question — a three-word phrase engineered to open rather than constrain.

---

## Purpose

Use when a team has completed discovery and definition work and is ready to move into developing solutions. Statement Starters translate what the team has learned about the problem into the question that will drive ideation.

**The core question:** How do we frame what we know about the problem in a way that invites the most expansive, ambitious thinking about what to do about it?

**Why Statement Starters matter:**
- Teams that skip this step carry problem statements into ideation — and problem statements constrain. They define the situation rather than open a direction for exploration.
- A poorly framed challenge produces predictable, incremental ideas. A well-framed challenge produces unexpected ones.
- The framing of the question determines the range of answers people can imagine.
- This is where research becomes generative — the insight is the fuel; the HMW question is the engine.

**Why "How Might We" specifically:**
The three words do distinct work:
- **How** assumes that solutions exist — it doesn't ask whether something is possible, only how
- **Might** defers judgment and promotes risk-taking — "can" and "should" imply correctability; "might" makes failure safe
- **We** signals collaboration — the challenge belongs to everyone in the room, not to the individual who asked it

This is not arbitrary phrasing. Alternatives like "How should we..." or "How can we..." collapse the space. The word choices are the method.

**Where it sits in the process:**
Statement Starters sit at the transition between the two diamonds — after discovery and definition work has produced validated understanding of the problem, and before development begins. The team has done the work to know what problem is worth solving. Statement Starters is the bridge: it converts that knowledge into the question that will drive what comes next.

## Setup

### Participants
- **Facilitator:** Guides the session, holds quality on the statements, challenges weak framings
- **Team members:** The people who did the research — they carry the insight; their instinct about good framings matters
- **Optional: Stakeholders** — useful for reviewing and voting on final statements; they often respond well to seeing research translated into a direction

### What You Need
- **Research insights** — ideally from Affinity Clustering, interviewing, or other research activity. Statements should be grounded in what was learned, not pulled from assumption.
- **Sticky notes or a digital board** — one statement per note
- **Markers**
- **Dot stickers** — for voting at the end

### Time
- 30–45 minutes for a full group session
- 15–20 minutes solo or as prep

## Steps

### Step 1 — Anchor in Research
Begin with the insights or problem findings from research. Each statement starter should be traceable to something the team learned — a pattern, a tension, a human need, an unmet expectation. This is not a brainstorm from assumption; it is a translation exercise from evidence.

If insights already begin with a verb ("People struggle to..."), the HMW reframe is often immediate: *How might we eliminate the struggle to...?*

### Step 2 — Draft the Statement
For each insight or problem area, complete the prompt: **How might we...**

The statement has three parts:
- **Verb** — the action or change you're pursuing. Choose ambitiously: *eliminate, transform, make, reimagine, enable, ensure, delight.*
- **Context** — the where, when, or what. Be specific enough to give the question direction.
- **People** — who will benefit. Name them. This keeps the statement human-centered and prevents solutions from drifting toward the system rather than the person.

The verb is the fixed anchor — it always comes first. Context and people can switch order depending on what reads most naturally for a given statement.

*Example:* How might we **eliminate** [verb] **waiting for room-temperature insulin** [context] **for people with diabetes** [people]?

### Step 3 — Check for Common Failure Modes
Before moving on, run each statement against these:

**Embedded solution** — If the statement names a specific solution, it has already closed down the thinking it was supposed to open. *How might we create an employee recognition program?* prescribes the answer. *How might we make employees feel genuinely valued?* does not.

**Compound statement** — Two challenges in one question split the energy and make ideation unfocused. *How might we increase sales and decrease customer requests?* is two separate statements. Split them.

**Too narrow** — An absolute metric collapses the space. *How might we reduce maintenance costs by 30%?* limits the team to efficiency thinking. *How might we create products that maintain themselves?* opens entirely new territory.

**Too broad** — A question so expansive that it can't be acted on produces ideas that can't be built. Find the level that's ambitious but grounded in something real the team is positioned to solve.

### Step 4 — Make Them More Provocative
A serviceable HMW question is better than none, but a provocative one is the goal. Five techniques:

- **Metaphor** — Ask "who or what does this best?" and borrow the language. Zappos does service. A family does belonging. A wedding does anticipation. *How might we make the onboarding process feel like a welcome home?*
- **Strong emotions** — Love, Hate, Delight. *How might we make people delight in doing their tax returns?*
- **Absolutes** — 100%, Eliminate, Instant. *How might we instantly connect a new employee to the people who will shape their first year?*
- **Inspire action** — Verbs that suggest energy and agency: Beg, Cheer, Champion. *How might we make employees champion each other's work?*
- **No -er words; -est is best** — Comparative framing is weak. "Easier" hedges. "Easiest" commits. *How might we make filing taxes the easiest thing someone does all year?*

### Step 5 — Generate Variations
One initial statement often suggests others. Draft variations at different levels of ambition or abstraction — then choose. Seeing three or four versions of the same challenge often reveals which framing is doing the most work.

### Step 6 — Share and Vote
Present the statements to the group. Give each person dot stickers and ask them to vote for the statement or statements they most want to explore. Discussion is welcome; the vote surfaces genuine preference. The highest-voted statement (or statements) become the basis for ideation.

## Facilitation Notes

### Ground Every Statement in an Insight
The quality of the statement is a direct function of the quality of the research behind it. A team working from weak insights will write weak statements. If the group is struggling to write HMW questions that feel interesting, the problem is usually upstream — the insights aren't sharp enough to point anywhere. Surface this: "What did we actually learn that surprised us? What did people tell us that we didn't expect?" That's where the generative statements live.

### The Verb Is the Most Important Word
Most weak HMW statements have weak verbs. "Improve," "enhance," "support," and "help" are flags — they describe a direction of travel but not an ambition. Replace them with verbs that commit to a change: *eliminate, transform, ensure, make, reimagine.* When a group defaults to weak verbs, slow them down on this step specifically.

### Distinguish Provocation from Vagueness
Ambitious statements and vague statements can look similar, but they are different. "How might we reinvent the entire financial system?" is vague — there's no human in it, no context, nothing to anchor ideation. "How might we make saving money feel as satisfying as spending it for people living paycheck to paycheck?" is ambitious — bold, but grounded and specific. Push for the second kind.

### Metaphors Unlock Non-Obvious Directions
When a group is stuck writing variations of the same obvious framing, metaphor is the fastest way out. Ask: "Who or what is already great at this? What if we borrowed their approach?" A customer service challenge framed through the lens of a concierge hotel will produce different ideas than one framed through the lens of a call center. The metaphor is a lens, not a literal answer.

### Voting Isn't Elimination — It's Prioritization
Teams sometimes resist voting because it feels like discarding work. Reframe it: we're not discarding the others, we're choosing where to start. Unselected statements can come back. For now, we focus.

### One Statement per Ideation Session
Resist the urge to take multiple HMW statements into a single ideation session. They dilute focus and produce shallower ideas. Better to run a focused session on one sharp question than a sprawling session on three okay ones. If the team has multiple strong statements, run sequential sessions or choose a different challenge for each workshop.

## Variations

### Insight-to-HMW Translation
When insights from research already have a clear structure ("People feel frustrated when..."), run the translation explicitly as a step: write the insight on one sticky, write the HMW version on another. This makes the relationship between research and challenge framing visible and teachable.

### Abstraction Laddering into HMW
After running Abstraction Laddering to find the right level of challenge, translate the chosen framing directly into a HMW statement. The transition is usually immediate: the ladder has already identified the goal and the context; the HMW adds the verb and the people.

### HMW Variations as a Warmup
Before ideation, ask the team to generate three HMW variations on the same challenge: one that's too narrow, one that's too broad, and one that's just right. This calibrates their sense of scope and produces better ideas when the real session starts.

### Solo as Preparation
Use Statement Starters individually before a stakeholder conversation or design review to ensure the challenge is framed at the right level and can be stated crisply. A team that can't state their challenge in a single HMW question doesn't yet have a clear challenge.

---

## Outputs

- **A set of How Might We statements** — grounded in research, with strong verbs, clear context, and named people
- **A prioritized working question** — the statement the team has chosen as the basis for ideation
- **Optional variations** — alternate framings at different levels of ambition, held in reserve

---

## Connections

### In the Double Diamond
Statement Starters sit at the boundary between Define and Develop — the hinge point between the two diamonds. They are the last act of understanding before the team opens up to solution thinking.

### Upstream Methods (Statement Starters receives from)
- **Affinity Clustering** — insights from clustered research are the primary input
- **Interviewing** — direct field research findings translate readily into HMW framings
- **Abstraction Laddering** — the chosen framing from a ladder often translates directly into a HMW statement
- **Rose/Thorn/Bud** — thorns and buds often surface challenges worth converting into HMW questions
- Any prior work that has produced problem statements, tension areas, or validated insights

### Downstream Methods (Statement Starters feeds into)
- **Round Robin** — the HMW statement becomes the prompt for rapid ideation
- **Creative Matrix** — the HMW statement provides the challenge that axes of the matrix respond to
- Any ideation method — Statement Starters is the setup for all of them

### Related Methods
- **Abstraction Laddering** — also a reframing method, but earlier in the process; used to find the right level of challenge before HMW translates it into a design question
- **Affinity Clustering** — the source of insights that become HMW statements; strong clusters produce strong statements
