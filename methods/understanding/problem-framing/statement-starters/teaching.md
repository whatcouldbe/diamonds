# Statement Starters — Teaching

Content for delivering the 7-step instructional arc (see `modes/mechanics/method-instruction-mechanics.md`).

**This arc is a conversation, not a script.** Deliver one step at a time. After each step, pause — check for resonance, invite a question, or ask the person to connect it to something real. Let their response shape what comes next. A content dump covers all seven steps; teaching covers the ones that matter to this person in this moment.

---

## Step 1 — Context (The "Have You Ever..." Hook)

*"Think about the last time your team sat down to generate ideas. What question were you answering? Was it written down anywhere? Was everyone in the room answering the same question — or were people solving slightly different problems without realizing it?*

*The framing of a challenge is one of the most consequential decisions a team makes, and most teams never consciously make it. They inherit a brief, or pull a problem from a whiteboard, and start generating — without asking whether the question they're answering is the right one, or whether it's framed in a way that invites the most ambitious thinking.*

*The result: predictable ideas. Not because the team isn't creative, but because the question they were answering was already pointing at an obvious answer."*

---

## Step 2 — Content (The Personal Story)

The Polaroid story is the best introduction to this method — not because it's clever, but because it's true.

In 1943, Edwin Land was on a family vacation taking photographs. His daughter looked at the camera and asked a simple question: *"Why can't I see the picture now?"*

At the time, amateur photographers had two options: send the film off and wait a week for it to come back, or set up your own darkroom at home. Neither was quick. Neither was accessible. The problem had been there for decades — and nobody had framed it as a question worth answering.

Land's daughter asked the question. Land heard it. And he spent the next several years figuring out how to answer it. The result was the Polaroid camera — an entirely new product category, not an incremental improvement to existing options.

His How Might We statement might have been: *"How might we immediately let people see the pictures they've captured?"*

That question doesn't prescribe instant film, or chemical processes, or a specific camera design. It opens a challenge. Everything that followed was an attempt to answer it.

This is what a well-framed statement starter does. It converts a human observation — a frustration, a need, an unmet expectation — into a question that makes a whole range of solutions imaginable.

---

## Step 3 — Inputs > Outputs

**Inputs:** Research insights — from interviews, Affinity Clustering, or other discovery work. The better the research, the more generative the statements. A team working from real understanding writes better questions than a team working from assumption.

**Outputs:** A set of How Might We statements, prioritized through voting, with one working question chosen as the basis for ideation. Optional variations at different levels of ambition, held in reserve.

**Connection forward:** The HMW statement is the prompt for all ideation. Every idea generated in Round Robin, Creative Matrix, or any other method should be a direct response to it. The statement is the bridge between what the team understands and what they're going to build.

---

## Step 4 — Activity

See `method.md` for the full steps and `mechanics.md` for the facilitation cheat sheet.

**Key mechanics to emphasize when teaching:**

- **The three-word structure does real work.** "How" assumes solutions exist. "Might" defers judgment. "We" signals collaboration. This is not a stylistic preference — it's the reason the phrasing works. Alternatives like "How should we" or "How can we" close down the space these words open.

- **The verb is the most important word.** Weak verbs ("improve," "enhance," "support") describe a direction without committing to a change. Strong verbs ("eliminate," "transform," "ensure," "delight") set an ambition. Teach people to interrogate their verb before anything else.

- **Ground every statement in research.** If a team can't trace their HMW statement back to something they learned, the statement is working from assumption — not insight. The quality of the question is a direct function of the quality of the research behind it.

- **Don't embed the solution.** This is the most common failure mode. *How might we create an employee recognition program?* is a project brief, not a design challenge. *How might we make employees feel genuinely valued for their work?* is a question. The difference is whether ideation is already over before it starts.

- **One statement, one challenge.** Compound statements split focus. "How might we increase sales and decrease customer requests?" is two separate questions. Split them and run ideation on each.

**The five techniques for more provocative statements** — useful to teach as a distinct layer once people understand the basic structure:

1. **Metaphor** — "Who or what already does this brilliantly? What if we borrowed their approach?" Good anchor questions: who delivers the best service (Zappos), who creates the most belonging (a family, a wedding), who generates the most loyalty? The metaphor becomes a lens for ideation.
2. **Strong emotions** — Love, Hate, Delight. Emotional language forces the team to think about the human experience of the solution, not just its function.
3. **Absolutes** — 100%, Eliminate, Instant. These deliberately push past the incremental. *How might we eliminate the wait entirely?* is a more generative question than *How might we reduce the wait?*
4. **Inspire action** — Verbs with energy: Beg, Cheer, Champion. These produce ideas with more agency and momentum.
5. **No -er words; -est is best** — "Easier" hedges. "Easiest" commits. This one small shift changes the level of ambition in the room.

**Using a worked example.** Before applying the method to real work, run it on a relatable challenge to establish the mechanics without the cognitive load of the actual project. Good examples: filing your taxes, waiting for customer service, onboarding at a new job.

The tax returns example works well because it has clear human emotion attached to it and a well-known failure mode in the first draft:
- First draft: *How might we encourage people to file their tax returns?* — functional, weak verb, no provocation
- Better: *How might we make the tax return process so enticing that people are begging to do it?* — strong emotion, absolute ambition, traces back to the insight that people find it dull rather than just avoiding it

Walk through both versions and ask: which question would produce more interesting ideas in an ideation session?

When teaching someone to facilitate: run the method on a real challenge the group is working on. Debrief on the statements that felt alive versus flat. What made the difference? Which technique shifted the framing? Then practice the redirect: what do you say when someone embeds a solution, or writes a compound statement?

## Step 5 — Reflection

*"Think about a project you're working on right now. Do you have a challenge statement? Is it written down somewhere that the whole team can see it? If you framed it as a How Might We question — with a strong verb, clear context, and named people — would that change how the ideation session goes?"*

*"Where in your current work is the question you're trying to answer too narrow, or too obvious? What would a more provocative framing look like?"*

**If the activity was run live:** *"Which statements felt most alive — and why? What was different about the verb, the context, or the level of ambition? Did any of the five techniques shift a statement from serviceable to genuinely interesting?"*

Close with a commitment: *"Is there a challenge coming up where you could use this — before the team sits down to generate ideas? The statement is worth writing before the session, not during it."*

---

## Step 6 — Final Moment (Conviction Close)

The question determines the answers. A team that sits down to answer *"How might we create an employee recognition program?"* will generate recognition programs. A team that sits down to answer *"How might we make people feel seen and valued in ways they've never experienced before?"* will generate something entirely different.

The Polaroid camera was not an improvement to existing options. It was an answer to a question that had never been asked before. Edwin Land's daughter didn't know anything about photography. She just asked what she wanted.

That is what a well-formed statement starter does. It gives a team permission to stop improving the existing answer — and start imagining a better one.

## Step 7 — Supplies

See `mechanics.md` → Stuff section for the complete list. Key items:
- 30–45 minutes
- Research insights — available to the group during drafting
- Sticky notes and markers — one statement per note
- Dot stickers — 3–5 per person for voting
- Wall space or digital board for displaying and clustering statements
