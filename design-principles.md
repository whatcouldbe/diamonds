# Diamonds Design Principles

These principles are the north star for anyone building on or contributing to Diamonds. They govern architectural decisions, method design, and how the system evolves over time. When a decision feels unclear, come back here.

---

## 1. Everyone designs.

**Ask first:** Who else is this for?

Design is not a profession — it's what happens whenever someone tries to change an existing situation into a preferred one. Every person trying to affect change in their product, organization, community, or life is already designing, whether they know it or not. Diamonds exists to make that capacity visible, accessible, and effective — not to gatekeep it behind expertise. The practitioner is not the primary user. The person trying to change something is.

**Check yourself:**
- Could someone with no design background engage with this?
- Are you building for practitioners or for everyone?
- Does this require expertise as the price of entry?

---

## 2. Plain language is the floor, personalization is the ceiling.

**Ask first:** Would a 12-year-old understand this — and would an expert find it useful?

Clarity is not dumbing down — it's respect. If specialized vocabulary is required to engage with something in Diamonds, it has already excluded the people it should serve. At the same time, plain language is a floor, not a ceiling. People bring different levels of experience, expertise, and context to this work. Diamonds should meet them where they are — progressively disclosing depth for those who can use it, without requiring expertise as the price of entry. The same principle applies across culture, language, and context: accessibility is not a feature, it is the foundation.

**Check yourself:**
- Is this written in the plainest language that still does the job?
- Does this work for a first-time user without overwhelming an experienced one?
- Are you assuming a level of familiarity that excludes people you intend to serve?

---

## 3. Outcomes over outputs.

**Ask first:** What change does this make possible?

Artifacts, deliverables, and methods are not the point — the change they enable is. Diamonds should consistently orient people toward the question: what difference will this make, and how will we know? This applies both to what Diamonds teaches and to how Diamonds itself is built. A method file, a coaching arc, or a new feature should be evaluated not by what it produces but by what it makes possible for the person using it. Building without a clear picture of the desired outcome is the failure mode Diamonds exists to prevent — including in its own development.

**Check yourself:**
- What outcome does this enable for the person using it?
- Are we measuring the right thing — the change, not just the output?
- Would we know if this wasn't working?

---

## 4. Validate, don't assume.

**Ask first:** How do we know this is true?

Everything is an assumption until tested with reality. This is the central methodological principle of HCD and it applies equally to the development of Diamonds itself. False mastery — the confidence that comes from expertise rather than validation — is as dangerous as ignorance. Practitioners are not exempt. The question is never whether to validate, only when and how. Diamonds should make this principle visible and actionable at every level: in the methods it teaches, in the coaching it provides, and in the decisions made about how the system is built.

**Check yourself:**
- Is this validated knowledge or a working assumption?
- When did we last test this with a real person?
- Are we moving forward because we know, or because we're confident?

---

## 5. Distributed authorship over expert mediation.

**Ask first:** Does this push decision-making closer to the people who will live with it?

The goal of Diamonds is not to produce better designers. It is to make design less dependent on designers — to push the authority to make decisions closer to the people who will be affected by them. Men have designed for women. Outsiders have designed for communities. Practitioners have averaged out individual needs because of time and scale constraints. These aren't failures of skill — they're failures of structure. Expert mediation has its place, but it should be in service of distributed authorship, not a substitute for it. AI makes genuine representation possible at a scale that was previously impossible. Diamonds should use that capability to expand who gets to participate in design, not to create a more efficient version of the same proxy structures that existed before.

**Check yourself:**
- Does this put more agency in the hands of the people it's designed to serve?
- Are we building a better expert layer, or dissolving the need for one?
- Who is making the decision here, and should it be them?

---

## 6. Challenge all constraints.

**Ask first:** Is this constraint intrinsic to the principle, or inherited from the medium?

Every method, process, and format in Diamonds carries two kinds of constraints: those intrinsic to the principle it serves, and those inherited from the medium it was built for. The first kind are worth protecting. The second kind are worth questioning every time the medium changes. Physical rooms, digital whiteboards, and AI-assisted work each inherit constraints from what came before — and most of those constraints were workarounds, not requirements. When a constraint is removed, the question is not how to do the same thing without it. The question is what becomes possible now that wasn't before.

**Check yourself:**
- Is this constraint load-bearing, or is it a legacy of the medium we came from?
- What becomes possible if we remove this constraint entirely?
- Are we challenging this, or just replicating what we've always done in a new surface?
