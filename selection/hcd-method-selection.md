# HCD Method Selection

*Layer 3 of the Skill Library — bridges the Navigation layer (10 Questions) and individual Method skills*

---

## Purpose

This file serves two functions depending on mode:

- **Coaching / Facilitating / Executing** — a reasoning engine. Use these principles internally to decide which method to recommend, how to sequence methods into a recipe, or how to help a team choose their next move.
- **Teaching** — a learnable framework. When someone asks "how do you know which methods to use?", these six principles can be taught directly as a transferable skill.

The same six principles serve both functions. Mode determines how you surface them.

---

## The Six Principles of Method Selection

### 1. Where Are You in the Process?

Your position in the innovation process is the first filter.

Map the person's situation to the 10 Questions framework:
- **Q1–2 (Strategic Context):** Alignment and planning methods — goal-setting, prioritization, stakeholder mapping
- **Q3 (Discover):** Looking methods — ethnographic research, empathy interviews, observation, contextual inquiry
- **Q4 (Define):** Understanding methods — synthesis, affinity clustering, abstraction laddering, How Might We framing
- **Q5 (Develop):** Making / Concept Ideation methods — brainstorming, creative matrix, analogous inspiration
- **Q6 (Deliver):** Making / Prototyping and Evaluative methods — concept testing, usability testing, pilots
- **Q7–10 (Post-Launch):** Scaling, sustaining, and sunsetting methods

**Reasoning rule:** When someone describes their situation, internally map it to a Question. That Question maps to a practice area (Looking, Understanding, or Making) and a subcategory. The right method lives in that subcategory. Start there before evaluating individual methods.

**Important:** If someone appears to be answering a later Question without having validated an earlier one, name the gap. "It sounds like you're exploring solutions — have you validated what the actual customer problem is?" Skipping checkpoints is the most common reason teams end up building the wrong thing.

---

### 2. Diverge Before You Converge

Some methods create choices. Others help you make them.

**Divergent methods** expand the space — they generate data, options, ideas, or perspectives. Examples: empathy interviews, brainstorming, creative matrix, analogous inspiration.

**Convergent methods** narrow the space — they synthesize, prioritize, or make decisions. Examples: affinity clustering, importance/difficulty matrix, bullseye prioritization, How Might We reframing.

**Reasoning rule:** Ask what the person's last move produced. If they have a lot of raw material (interview notes, a long list of ideas, a pile of observations), the next method is almost certainly convergent. If they're stuck, thin on data, or working from assumptions, the next method is probably divergent.

Watch for imbalance:
- **Too much divergence, no convergence** = analysis paralysis, endless research, no decisions
- **Too much convergence, no divergence** = premature closure, narrow thinking, solutions that miss real needs

---

### 3. Input/Output Chaining

Methods compound. The output of one method becomes the input of the next.

Think of methods as elements in a periodic table — there are natural combinations that work because they're chemically compatible. Forcing incompatible combinations produces nothing useful.

**How to reason about chaining:**
- Ask: "What will this method produce?" (the output)
- Ask: "What does the next method need to work?" (the input)
- If the output of method A is the input of method B, they chain well

**Example chain:**
> Statement Starters (output: reframed opportunity statements) → Creative Matrix (input: opportunity prompts; output: a large set of raw ideas) → Importance/Difficulty Matrix (input: ideas to evaluate; output: prioritized shortlist)

This chain works because each handoff is clean. The output of one method is exactly what the next method needs.

**Reasoning rule:** When recommending a sequence, trace the input/output logic explicitly. If the outputs don't feed the next method's inputs, the sequence will feel disconnected and teams will struggle with transitions.

---

### 4. Know Your Neighbors

Within any subcategory of the taxonomy, multiple methods serve similar purposes — but with different fidelity, time requirements, and team configurations.

Choosing between neighbors is a contextual decision, not a quality decision. A simpler method in the right context is better than a more rigorous method in the wrong one.

**Example — within Patterns & Priorities:**
- **Importance/Difficulty Matrix** — good for cross-functional teams with many options and time to discuss trade-offs
- **Bullseye Prioritization** — good for quick consensus, smaller teams, or when the field is already narrow
- **Visualize the Vote** — good for distributed teams or when you want individual input before group discussion

None of these is better than the others. The right one depends on team size, time available, number of items to evaluate, and whether you need individual votes or group consensus before converging.

**Reasoning rule:** Once you've identified the right subcategory, look at the methods within it as a neighborhood. Help the person or team choose based on their specific constraints — time, team size, fidelity needed, prior outputs.

When a user brings their own method, classify it into the taxonomy and identify its neighbors. That tells you when it applies and what it works well with.

### 5. Maintain a Healthy Heartbeat

Teams that get stuck in one practice area — Looking, Understanding, or Making — stop moving. The work loses energy and often loses value.

**The pattern of healthy teams:** They move back and forth. They make something, test it, learn from it, reframe, make something new, test again. The rhythm is not linear — it's iterative, and the pace can be fast.

Watch for stuck patterns:
- **Stuck in Looking:** "We keep doing research and never do anything with it." Suggest a synthesis method to move into Understanding.
- **Stuck in Understanding:** "We keep mapping and frameworks but nothing's getting built." Suggest a lightweight ideation or prototyping method to move into Making.
- **Stuck in Making:** "We keep building things but we don't really know if they're right." Suggest an evaluative research method to move back into Looking.

**Important nuance:** Most projects are front-weighted — more Looking and Understanding at the start, more Making later. That's appropriate. The heartbeat principle isn't about equal time in each area — it's about not getting trapped. The question is always: "Is this team moving, or are they stuck?"

**Reasoning rule:** When diagnosing where someone is, notice not just their current position but their recent movement. A team that's been in Understanding for months needs a push toward Making. A team that's been building without testing needs to move back to Looking.

---

### 6. Use What Works

Originality is not the goal. Effectiveness is.

Proven method sequences — recipes — exist because they work. Across many projects and teams, certain combinations consistently produce good outcomes. When in doubt, reach for a proven recipe before inventing a new one.

**What makes a good recipe:**
- The methods chain cleanly (Principle 3)
- It moves through at least two practice areas (Principle 5)
- It produces validated outputs at each stage (Principle 1)
- It can be adapted to different team sizes and time constraints

**Starter recipes (to be expanded):**

*Discovery to Definition*
> Empathy Interviews → Affinity Clustering → How Might We Statements

*Definition to Ideation*
> How Might We Statements → Creative Matrix → Importance/Difficulty Matrix

*Ideation to Validation*
> Concept Sketches → Think-Aloud Testing → Iteration

*Full first diamond (compressed)*
> Empathy Interviews → Affinity Clustering → Abstraction Laddering → How Might We Statements

**Reasoning rule:** When someone needs a sequence, start with recipes that have worked. Adapt based on constraints. Invent only when the context is genuinely novel — and even then, use the input/output chaining principle to construct the sequence deliberately.

## Using These Principles Together

In practice, multiple principles apply simultaneously. A good method recommendation reasons across all six:

1. **Where are they?** → Identifies practice area and subcategory
2. **What did they just do?** → Determines whether next move is divergent or convergent
3. **What will this produce?** → Confirms the output feeds into what comes next
4. **What are their constraints?** → Selects the right neighbor within the subcategory
5. **Are they moving?** → Checks whether the recommendation maintains momentum
6. **Does a proven recipe apply?** → Defaults to what works before inventing

A recommendation that satisfies all six is almost always right. A recommendation that violates one or more is worth examining before committing to it.

---

## Teaching This Framework

When in Teaching mode and someone asks how to choose methods, this framework can be taught as six transferable principles. Introduce them in order — they build on each other. Start with process position (Principle 1) as the foundation, then layer in the others as practical tools for reasoning through real situations.

Use the recipes (Principle 6) as worked examples that demonstrate all five preceding principles in action.
