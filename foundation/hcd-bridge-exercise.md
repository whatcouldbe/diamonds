# The Bridge Exercise

A 3-minute facilitated activity that establishes why teams must explore the problem before developing solutions. Use this to open a workshop, introduce human-centred design, or reset a group that has jumped prematurely into solution mode.

---

## Why This Exercise Exists

Most teams arrive at a workshop already attached to a solution. They have a brief, a direction, sometimes a name for the thing they're building. They're in the second diamond before they've spent a moment in the first.

The Bridge Exercise creates a shared experience — not an argument or a lecture — that makes the value of the first diamond felt rather than explained. In three minutes, participants watch their own thinking change as each layer of why and who reframes the solution space. By the end, the case for exploring the problem before developing solutions has been made by the room, not by the facilitator.

This is why it precedes the double diamond introduction. The framework lands differently when people have already experienced its logic.

> *"If you fail to define the problem accurately, no amount of resources — or creativity — will save you."* — Hugh Dubberly

---

## When to Use It

- At the start of any discovery or design workshop, before introducing the double diamond
- When a team is visibly attached to a solution and needs to step back
- When introducing HCD to a group unfamiliar with the practice
- As a quick reset when a session drifts back into solution mode
- One-to-one, as a coaching prompt for someone examining a solution they're already committed to

Works with any group size — 4 to 40 people. Requires no materials and no preparation.

---

## How to Run It

### Open with a grounding question

Ask the room: *"Who's ever been over a bridge?"*

Wait for hands. This is not rhetorical — you're getting physical engagement before you've asked anyone to think.

### State the brief

Say: *"I need a bridge."*

Pause. Then ask: *"What types of bridge solution might there be?"*

Invite responses. People will name bridge types — suspension, arch, beam, drawbridge. Accept them all. This is the solution space for the brief as stated.

### First reframe — the why

Ask: *"Are you interested in why I need a bridge?"*

Let the room respond. Then say: *"I need to cross a body of water."*

New solutions emerge immediately: boat, raft, swimming, ferry, tunnel. The bridge is still valid — but it's no longer the only answer, and it may not be the best one.

### Second reframe — deeper why

Ask: *"Are you interested in why I need to cross the water?"*

Let the room respond. Then say: *"I need to get a message to someone."*

The solution space shifts again. Phone, text, email, letter, video call. The bridge is becoming less relevant — not because it wouldn't work, but because we now know it's solving the wrong layer of the problem.

### Third reframe — the who

Ask: *"Are you interested in who I'm trying to reach?"*

Then say: *"It's my elderly aunt. She lives in a remote part of the Scottish Highlands. Patchy signal on a good day."*

Let this land. The room will respond — sometimes with laughter, sometimes with quiet recognition. The solution space is now shaped by the person, not just the task.

### Name the insight

Say: *"The bridge stopped making sense a while ago. Thinking about who alongside the why really changes the frame — and it should be there from the start."*

Then connect it to real work: *"In one session after this exercise, someone said to me: 'I think we've been building a bridge — and we don't actually know why we're building it or who it's really for.' That conversation led to a programme of work helping the team rethink the problems they were tackling and who would benefit."*

### Transition to the double diamond

Introduce the double diamond framework. Point to the first diamond: *"This is why we always emphasise the first diamond before the second. Understand the problem space before exploring solutions. Get clear on the why and the who before getting attached to the how."*

---

## Facilitation Notes

**The call-and-response structure is the method.** The phrase *"Are you interested in why...?"* models the questioning behaviour you want teams to adopt. Each time you use it, you're demonstrating what it looks like to pause and ask before assuming.

**Let the room generate the solutions.** In each step, invite responses rather than supplying answers. When participants say "boat" or "text message" themselves, they own the insight.

**Don't rush the pauses.** Each reframe needs a moment of silence after the reveal. The pause is where the penny drops.

**The aunt is the pivot.** The who reveal is the most powerful moment — the shift from task-framing to person-centred framing is the core HCD insight. Let it land without overselling it.

**Connect it to the room's actual work in the debrief.** Ask: *"Where in your current work might you be building a bridge without fully understanding the why or the who?"* This is where the insight becomes actionable.

**You can run it entirely from memory.** A single slide listing the four steps is useful in large rooms, but the exercise is designed to be conversational.

---

## What It Produces

- A shared recognition that who and why must shape how — felt, not just understood
- A common reference point ("the bridge") the group can return to throughout the session when solution-first thinking resurfaces
- A primed audience for the double diamond introduction
- Occasionally: a team realising they should not be building the thing they came to build
